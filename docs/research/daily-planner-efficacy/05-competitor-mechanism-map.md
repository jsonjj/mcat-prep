# Competitor mechanism map: which learning-science mechanisms the MCAT market actually delivers

Evidence review. Tiers used inline: **A** = strong direct evidence or field consensus; **B** = reasonable with stated limits; **C** = weak, indirect, or single-source (marketing pages and forum data are **C**). Arithmetic that is mine is labelled *[own calculation]*. Reading depth is stated per source in `## Sources`. No product feature is described here unless I could verify it on a first-party page or in first-party documentation.

---

## Recommendation

**The team's hypothesis is half right and, as stated, not a gap.** Retrieval practice *does* transfer to application and inference items (Pan & Rickard 2018: d = 0.32, k = 41). And UWorld, AAMC, Blueprint, Kaplan and Princeton Review all already sell applied practice with explanations.

**The real gap is one level down.** Pan & Rickard predict d ≈ 0.90 when response congruency, elaborated retrieval practice and high initial accuracy hold, and d ≈ 0.21 (≈ 0 after bias correction) when none do. Anki sits in the bottom cell: correct-answer-only feedback, which carried Δd = **−0.49** for application transfer. Question banks sit mid-scale — elaborated feedback, but single-pass and never re-retrieved, though training repetitions was a positive moderator inside that category.

**The unoccupied combination: spaced, error-driven re-retrieval of passage-based application items, with elaborated explanation feedback and calibration measured against an external standard.** Every incumbent holds two or three. None holds four. Calibration is the most cleanly absent and best-evidenced.

## Findings

### Transfer of retrieval practice

**Pan, S. C., & Rickard, T. C. (2018), *Psychological Bulletin* 144(7), 710–756. Tier A. I read the published full text (PDF mirror), including all category-level results sections and Tables 2, 6, 7 and 8.**

Scope: **192 transfer effect sizes from 122 experiments in 67 published and unpublished articles, N = 10,382**, spanning 1975–2016. (The author's accepted manuscript reports N = 10,396; the published version says 10,382. I have not reconciled the discrepancy.) 53 of the 67 studies were published, 14 unpublished. Comparator throughout is a **non-testing reexposure control** — i.e. restudy, not "nothing."

Grand weighted mean, random effects: **d = 0.40, 95% CI [0.31, 0.50], p < .00001.**

**The decomposition — this is the load-bearing table.** All values are Cohen's *d* from the published Table 2, random-effects, no moderators fitted:

| Transfer category | k | d | 95% CI | p |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Across test formats** (e.g. cued recall → MC, MC → cued recall) | 56 | **0.58** | [0.43, 0.73] | < .00001 |
| **Mediator / related word cues** | 27 | **0.61** | [0.25, 0.97] | .018 |
| **Application and inference questions** | 41 | **0.32** | [0.085, 0.56] | .013 |
| **Problem-solving skills** (medical diagnosis + worked examples) | 18 | **0.29** | [−0.078, 0.65] | .10 (ns) |
| **Stimulus–response rearrangement** (cue and response roles swapped) | 33 | **0.22** | [−0.019, 0.45] | .066 (ns) |
| **Untested materials seen during initial study** | 17 | **0.16** | [−0.10, 0.43] | .20 (ns) |
| Overall | 192 | 0.40 | [0.31, 0.50] | < .00001 |

Two internal inconsistencies I did not resolve: the body text describes the problem-solving category as "17 effect sizes from the nine papers" while Table 2 reports k = 18; and the application/inference p-value is .013 in Table 2 and .0013 in the body text. Neither changes the substantive picture.

**Sub-category splits, which matter more than the headline categories.** These are single-moderator random-effects fits:

- **Problem-solving splits almost completely.** Worked examples: **d = 0.045** (essentially zero). Medical diagnosis and treatment: **d = 0.59** (moderator Δd = 0.59, p = .028, CI [0.093, 1.09]). The authors attribute this partly to response congruency — in the medical-diagnosis studies "the overall scenarios and procedures are essentially the same" across initial and final test, whereas *all* worked-example studies had no response congruency, and the worked-example condition typically involved problem worksheets without feedback.
- **Stimulus–response rearrangement splits by material.** Non-paired-associate stimuli (multi-term facts, term–definition facts — i.e. the shape of a cloze flashcard): **d = 0.063, p = .29** (nil). Paired associates: **d = 0.72**.
- **Untested materials depends entirely on elaboration.** Without elaborated retrieval practice: **d = 0.0028**. With it: **d = 0.37** (p = .032).

**The three moderators that govern everything (overall dataset).**

1. **Response congruency** — whether the correct answers on the practice test and the criterion test are the same or substantially overlapping. Single fit: no congruency **d = 0.28** [0.17, 0.39]; congruency adds **Δd = 0.30** [0.14, 0.47], giving **d = 0.58**. In the simultaneous fit, Δd = 0.35 [0.18, 0.51]. Coding used a stringent criterion (ambiguous cases coded as *not* congruent), so the authors say the effect may be understated.
2. **Elaborated retrieval practice** — broad encoding instructions, higher-order Bloom-level questions, or post-retrieval elaborative feedback. Δd = **0.22** [0.059, 0.38], p = .0094.
3. **Initial test performance** — each 1 percentage point of accuracy on the practice test added roughly d = 0.0058.

**Combined:** both response congruency and elaborated retrieval practice present → **d = 0.78**; neither present → **d = 0.21**. With initial accuracy also set to the dataset maximum (0.98), predicted **d = 0.90** [0.71, 1.08]; with none present and low initial accuracy, **d = −0.053** [−0.22, 0.12].

**Publication bias is material and cuts the intercept, not the moderators.** PEESE indicated at least moderate bias. Moderator effect sizes survived (response congruency Δd = 0.36, elaboration Δd = 0.18), but the intercept fell to **d = 0.015** — i.e. under bias correction, *no positive transfer at all when none of the three moderators is present*. Under the PEESE model the best and worst cells become d = 0.58 and **d = −0.21**. Category-level intercepts under PEESE were near zero for stimulus–response rearrangement, untested materials, application/inference and problem-solving.

**The single most product-relevant finding in the paper.** Inside the application-and-inference category, the simultaneous moderator fit found:

- **Correct-answer feedback was associated with *less* transfer: Δd = −0.49, CI [−0.75, −0.24], p = .011**, yielding an estimated **d = −0.049**.
- **Elaborated retrieval practice was associated with more: Δd = +0.26**, CI [−0.019, 0.54], yielding an estimated **d = 0.70**.
- Number of training repetitions: Δd = +0.29 per additional repetition (p = .054 simultaneous; +0.33, p = .016 in the single fit). Retention interval: +0.0033 per added hour.

The authors explicitly caution that these category-level fits have insufficient degrees of freedom at the paper level and should be treated as tentative — I am treating the −0.49 as **Tier B**, not A. But the direction is corroborated by Butler et al. (2013) independently, and the "number of repetitions" and "retention interval" moderators are the only evidence I found that *repeated, spaced* retrieval improves transfer to application items specifically.

**Butler (2010), *JEP:LMC* 36(5), 1118–1133. Tier A for the qualitative claim; I read the abstract in full plus a results excerpt and did not extract effect sizes.** Four experiments; undergraduates read prose passages, then either repeatedly restudied or repeatedly tested. One week later the final test used the same questions (1a), new inferential questions in the same domain (1b, 2), or new inferential questions in *different* knowledge domains (3). Repeated testing beat repeated studying on retention and on both near and far transfer. **Important limitation, which I did not find flagged in the abstract:** subjects were explicitly told the final-test questions related to what they had studied, so the study does not test whether learners *recognise* the applicability of knowledge to a novel problem — which is precisely the demand an MCAT passage imposes. I found this limitation in a third-party reading note, not in the paper; **Tier C for the limitation itself**, but it is checkable and material.

**Butler, Godbole & Marsh (2013), *Journal of Educational Psychology* 105(2), 290–298. Tier A. I read the abstract in full and the full-text methods/discussion; I could not extract the numeric effect sizes from the text I retrieved.** Two experiments. Subjects studied prose passages, took an initial short-answer test, and received **correct-answer feedback, explanation feedback, or no feedback**. Two days later: repeated questions plus new inference questions. Result: **correct-answer and explanation feedback were equivalent on the repeated questions, but explanation feedback produced superior performance on the new inference questions.** A re-answer phase (where subjects could consult the feedback) let the authors separate the recall step from the application step and attribute the difference to depth of understanding. The effect held across between-subjects (Exp. 1) and within-subjects (Exp. 2) manipulation. Initial-test accuracy was low (grand M = .43), by design.

This is the cleanest available warrant for the product claim "the explanation is the mechanism, not the item." It also means the standard flashcard interaction — reveal answer, self-grade — is the *worse* of the two feedback types on exactly the outcome the MCAT measures.

**Flashcard-style cued recall versus worked problems / application practice: the direct comparison is thin. Say so.**

I found no experiment that pits flashcard-style cued recall against question-bank-style applied practice, equated for time, on a novel applied criterion test. The nearest evidence:

- **Yeo & Fazio (2019), *JEP* 111(1), 73–90. Tier B; abstract read in full.** Three experiments crossing retrieval practice against worked examples. Conclusion: neither dominates. For *remembering the text* of a worked example, repeated testing beat repeated studying at one week. For *learning a novel maths procedure*: on an immediate test, repeated **studying** was better regardless of materials; at one week, repeated testing was **as good as** studying with non-identical problems and **better than** studying with identical problems. Read plainly: retrieval practice's advantage over worked examples is a *long-retention-interval* advantage on procedures, and it evaporates or reverses at short intervals. Pan & Rickard's own worked-examples sub-category (d = 0.045) is consistent.
- **Deng, Gluckstein & Larsen (2015), *Perspectives on Medical Education* 4(6), 308–313. Tier B/C — observational, single institution, n = 72, self-reported resource volumes; I read the abstract and the results passage.** Multivariate regression on USMLE Step 1 score, R² = .672. Significant independent predictors: **boards-style MCQs completed (B = 2.2 × 10⁻³, p < .001)**, **unique Anki cards seen (B = 5.9 × 10⁻⁴, p = .024)**, second-year honours, MCAT score; test anxiety negative. Firecracker flashcards did **not** predict. Translated by the authors: **445 additional MCQs, or 1,700 additional unique Anki cards, each ≈ 1 additional Step 1 point.** An erratum corrected the Table 3 coefficient for Anki from 5.89 × 10⁻³ to 5.89 × 10⁻⁴ (the body text was right).

  **One boards-style question is worth about 3.8 flashcards of Step 1 score, per item** *[own calculation: 1,700 / 445 = 3.82]*. This is the single closest thing to a quantified Anki-versus-qbank comparison in the literature. It is correlational, from one school, in 2015, on a different exam, and self-reported. It is **not** causal evidence and it does not equate time per item — an MCQ takes far longer than a flashcard, so the per-*minute* comparison may be much closer or reversed. Treat as directional only.
- **Bonasso et al. (2015), reported in a secondary systematic review. Tier C.** Retrospective, n = 164: UWorld question-bank users scored **229 (SE 1.4)** on Step 1 versus **215 (SE 4.2)** for non-users (p < .05). Straightforwardly confounded by student motivation and resources.

**Recall of facts versus application of concepts to novel stimuli.** The mechanism literature says the link is real but conditional, not automatic.

- **Morris, Bransford & Franks (1977), *JVLVB* 16(5), 519–533. Tier A; abstract only.** Transfer-appropriate processing. Semantic (deep) encoding beat rhyme (shallow) encoding on a standard recognition test, but rhyme encoding **beat** semantic encoding on a rhyming recognition test, across three experiments including immediate and delayed tests. Memory performance depends on the *match* between encoding operations and criterion-task operations, not on encoding depth alone. Applied here: practising "retrieve a fact from a term cue" is a different operation from "identify which stored principle governs an unfamiliar experimental figure." TAP predicts limited spontaneous transfer between them, and Pan & Rickard's response-congruency moderator is the empirical expression of the same idea at the literature level.
- **Desirable difficulties (Bjork 1994; Bjork, Dunlosky & Kornell 2013, *Annual Review of Psychology* 64, 417–444). Tier A for the framework; I read the relevant passages of the Bjork Lab PDF.** Testing, spacing, interleaving and varying conditions impair performance during acquisition but improve long-term retention and transfer. Two caveats the framework itself insists on and that matter commercially: (i) difficulties become *undesirable* if the learner lacks the background knowledge to respond successfully — which maps onto Pan & Rickard's initial-test-performance moderator and onto the expertise-reversal literature; and (ii) "when retrieval is made so easy as to insure success, by virtue of recency, strong cue support, or some other factor, the benefits of such retrieval as a learning event tend to be mostly or entirely absent." A mature Anki card with a familiar cue is close to that description.
- **Expertise reversal (Kalyuga 2007, *Educational Psychology Review* 19, 509–539; Kalyuga, Chandler, Tuovinen & Sweller 2001, *JEP* 93(3), 579–588. Tier A for the effect, abstracts only).** Worked examples help novices and become redundant or harmful for more expert learners, for whom problem solving is superior. Salden et al. showed **adaptive** fading of worked steps beat fixed fading, which beat plain tutored problem solving, on immediate and one-week delayed post-tests. Implication for MCAT: the right ratio of worked explanation to unsupported problem attempt is a function of the learner's current mastery on that specific concept — which is exactly what no incumbent measures at concept grain.

**MCAT-specific and passage-specific evidence.**

The exam itself, from AAMC's own published specification (**Tier A**): four multiple-choice sections, **230 questions**, **6 h 15 min content time**, **≈ 7 h 30 min total seated time**. Chem/Phys 59 items (10 passage sets of 4–6, plus 15 independent), CARS 53 items (9 passages of 5–7, all passage-based), Bio/Biochem 59 (same structure as Chem/Phys), Psych/Soc 59 (same). **185 of 230 items — 80.4% — are passage-based** *[own calculation: (59−15)×3 + 53 = 185; 185/230 = 0.804]*.

- **Khalifa University pilot (2021), *Journal of Medical Education and Curricular Development*, DOI 10.1177/2382120520981979. Tier C — n = 19; abstract and results read.** Full-length practice-exam performance predicted actual MCAT score (β = 0.74, t = 6.50, p < .001), independent of English proficiency; median practice score correlated r = 0.92 with actual. But **number of practice exams taken did not significantly predict test-day performance (r = 0.24, p = .32)**. So this study supports full-lengths as *measurement*, not as *treatment*. It is far too small to support either claim strongly.
- I could **not** find any peer-reviewed study of MCAT preparation *method* — flashcards versus question banks versus courses — on MCAT outcome. The USMLE literature (Deng; Bonasso; the Anki/COMLEX cohort study below) is the available proxy and is entirely observational.
- **Rocky Vista cohort (*International Journal of Medical Students*). Tier C; abstract only.** 35 Anki users versus 268 non-users, class of 2023. Anki cohort: mean Step 1 223.71 vs 222.58; COMLEX 569.51 vs 559.99; **no significant correlation between Anki use and licensing exam scores**, and the non-Anki cohort had a significantly *higher* GPA (85.60 vs 83.82, p < .01). A useful counterweight to Deng et al.: the Anki-score association is not robust across small cohorts.
- Widely circulated MCAT-specific regression figures (β weights for AAMC full-length count, Section Bank completion, etc.) appear on SEO content sites with no underlying dataset, author or method. **I found no verifiable source for any of them and have deliberately cited none.** See "What I could not verify."

**Anki + community decks.** Verified from the Anki manual and FAQ (**Tier A** for design facts). Cards are self-graded on four buttons; the manual's own guidance is that *Again* is used "about 5–20% of the time" and *Good* "about 80–95%." Scheduling is SM-2-derived (starting ease 2.50; *Again* −20 pp, *Hard* −15 pp, *Easy* +15 pp) or FSRS. New cards are gathered from subdecks in alphabetical order by default; review cards get random "fuzz" to avoid clumping. So: retrieval and spacing are genuinely delivered, and topic interleaving falls out of due-date mixing. Card counts: MileDown ≈ 2,900 across 7 subdecks (Behavioral, Biochemistry, Biology, Essential Equations, General Chemistry, Organic Chemistry, Physics/Math), predominantly cloze deletion; AnKing MCAT ≈ 6,200, merging MileDown, Abdullah and MrPankow, with UWorld question-ID tags. **Card counts are Tier C** — they come from third-party deck listings and aggregator pages, and I found one source claiming MileDown has "over 5,000" cards, which contradicts the rest.

What Anki **structurally cannot** deliver: (i) elaborative feedback — the back of a card is the answer, which is Butler et al.'s *correct-answer* condition, the one that failed to improve inference-question performance, and Pan & Rickard's Δd = −0.49 moderator; (ii) novel-application items — a card has one cue and one response, and re-encountering it is by definition a repeat, not a novel stimulus; (iii) format interleaving — every item in the queue is the same format; (iv) calibration — grading is self-report with no external standard (see below); (v) exam-condition fidelity — no timing, no passages, no endurance load. Its card-level grain and non-paired-associate cloze structure put it squarely in Pan & Rickard's **d = 0.063** sub-category for rearranged cues and its **d = 0.21** (bias-corrected ≈ 0) cell for the three-factor model. Note also that "Anki interleaves" should not be scored as a benefit: **Brunmair & Richter (2019), *Psychological Bulletin* 145(11), 1029–1052** (**Tier A**; 59 studies, 238 effect sizes, 158 samples, N = 8,466) found an overall interleaving effect of **g = 0.42** [0.34, 0.50] but **g = −0.39 for word materials** — blocking beat interleaving — with mathematics at g = 0.34 and paintings at g = 0.67. Cloze fact cards are closer to the word-material end.

**UWorld MCAT QBank.** Verified on uworld.com (**Tier B** — first-party marketing, but these are checkable product claims): **$339** entry price (90-day; 180-day and 360-day tiers exist), **3,000+** exam-level items, **2 UWorld full-length practice exams** (new), an interface that mirrors the exam, **detailed visual rationales for every answer choice — correct and incorrect**, built-in flashcards with spaced repetition, an AI tutor ("UAsk"), an adaptive study planner, and performance analytics. This is the market's best implementation of elaborative feedback on applied items and it maps directly onto Butler et al. (2013). Its structural weakness is that **the spacing applies to user-made flashcards (facts), not to the application items themselves**. The items are effectively single-pass: a widely-reported user concern is that re-doing seen questions "inflates" the percentage and destroys its predictive value — which is exactly the behaviour that prevents repeated retrieval of application items. Pan & Rickard's within-category moderators say repeated training on application-relevant material *is* where additional transfer comes from (Δd ≈ +0.29 to +0.33 per repetition). Nobody, including UWorld, exploits that.

**AAMC Official Prep.** Verified on store.aamc.org (**Tier A** for prices and item counts — first-party store and comparison table). Online-Only Bundle **$323.70** containing **2,710 unique passage-based and independent questions**; individual practice exams **$35** each; Section Banks **$45** each (300 items each); Question Packs **$15** each (120 items each); Independent Question Bank **$10** (150 items). Seven full-length exams now exist, two free (Unscored Sample Test, Practice Exam 1). These are criterion-defining items written by the people who write the exam, so response congruency to the real test is as high as it can get — the single most favourable position in Pan & Rickard's framework.

**The critical structural finding is that AAMC's feedback is mostly the wrong kind.** From AAMC's own product-comparison table: Section Bank Vol. 1, all four science Question Packs and the Independent Question Bank provide **"one rationale for correct answer"** only. Section Bank Vol. 2, both CARS Question Packs and the CARS Diagnostic Tool provide rationales for **all 4 options**. Practice exams include explanations for all answer choices plus Khan Academy links. So among the non-full-length **science** items: **300 of 1,230 (24.4%) carry all-option elaborated rationales; 930 (75.6%) carry correct-answer-only rationales** *[own calculation: elaborated = Section Bank Vol. 2 (300); correct-answer-only = Section Bank Vol. 1 (300) + Bio QP1 (120) + Bio QP2 (120) + Chemistry QP (120) + Physics QP (120) + Independent QB (150) = 930]*. Butler et al. (2013) and Pan & Rickard's Δd = −0.49 both say correct-answer-only feedback is the weaker choice precisely for inference items. AAMC also delivers no spacing, no mastery model and no error-driven remediation, and its bank is finite and hoarded — students ration it rather than distribute it.

**Kaplan / Princeton Review / Blueprint.** Verified on first-party sites (**Tier B**). Kaplan: courses from **$1,599**, **21 full-length practice tests** (including 7 AAMC exams), a **3,000+** customisable QBank, 8 books, 700+ hours of guided prep, 90+ content videos, explainer videos for 400+ AAMC questions, placement quizzes driving a personalised plan, and a 515+/15-point money-back guarantee on premium tiers. Princeton Review Ultimate: **123 hours of live instruction**, **18 full-lengths**, 12 section exams, 89 diagnostic quizzes, 8 books, 500+ on-demand videos, Smart Study Planner, all AAMC materials, 365-day access. Blueprint self-paced: **5,000+** discrete and passage-based questions, 160 modules, 500+ videos, 1,600+ flashcards, **16 full-lengths** (10 Blueprint + 6 AAMC), an adaptive planner that rebalances when sessions are missed. These courses genuinely deliver retrieval, format interleaving, elaborative explanation, worked examples, application practice and exam-condition fidelity — they are *not* pedagogically naive. What they do not deliver is (i) item-level spaced re-retrieval, (ii) mastery measurement at concept grain with a defensible stopping rule (scheduling and analytics are not the same thing), and (iii) any calibration instrument. Their advertised score gains ("15 points average," "12 points average") are **Tier C marketing** — self-selected enrollees, diagnostic-to-real comparisons, no control group.

**Jack Westin.** Verified on jackwestin.com and its support/FAQ pages (**Tier C** — first-party marketing plus support docs). The genuinely distinctive mechanism is **one unique timed daily passage for every day of the year (365 total), free, with streak tracking** — this is the only product in the market that delivers *distributed application practice as a scheduled habit* rather than as an event. Also claimed: 6,700+ question bank, 6 free full-length exams, digital textbooks for 7 subjects, a Chrome extension providing video solutions for AAMC materials, and JW+ at **$29.99/month** adding 450+ videos, adaptive scheduling and live sessions. The CARS course structure (6 lectures with 5–9 mandatory AAMC-sourced homework passages, two-week deadlines, workshop review) is documented in their FAQ. Structural limits: CARS-and-P/S only, so no interleaving across content domains; no mastery model; no error-driven scheduling; no calibration; and daily-passage practice is not full-day endurance practice. Its "12 points average" figure is unverifiable marketing.

**Adaptive/AI entrants.** The two I could verify are extensions of incumbents, not new entrants. **Blueprint's AI-Powered Qbank** (blueprintprep.com, **Tier C** — marketing claims, no published evaluation): 4,000–5,000+ questions, performance analysis that "recommends questions targeting your weaknesses," dynamic adaptation by section, question type, difficulty and time-per-subject, plus **"Blue," an AI tutor for CARS**. **UWorld** ships "UAsk" and an adaptive study planner. Neither publishes any efficacy evaluation. Both implement error-driven remediation (a real mechanism, **Tier B** support in the broader feedback literature) and neither implements calibration or a validated mastery model. I found **no** MCAT-specific AI entrant with a published or peer-reviewed evaluation of learning outcomes. Reference class for expectations: adaptive systems that stack four or five of these mechanisms (ALEKS, Cognitive Tutor) deliver g ≈ 0.05–0.20 against ordinary instruction in large RCTs — see the sibling review `02-mechanism-evidence.md`.

### Metacognitive calibration

**This is the most cleanly absent mechanism in the market and it has the best-evidenced causal chain.**

**The causal claim. Dunlosky & Rawson (2012), *Learning and Instruction* 22(4), 271–280. Tier A; abstract read in full plus results discussion via a secondary review.** College students self-paced study of key-term definitions, then took practice cued-recall tests, judging after each trial how much of their response was correct; trials continued until a definition was judged correct three times; final cued recall two days later. Judgment accuracy was **manipulated experimentally in Study 1** and examined as an individual difference in Study 2. In both, **greater absolute judgment accuracy was associated with higher retention**, and the link could not be explained by differential feedback, effort during study, or trials to criterion. Mechanism: overconfidence → **premature termination of study** → less learning during practice → lower retention. The authors distinguish *absolute* accuracy (over/underconfidence) from *relative* accuracy (item ranking) and argue absolute accuracy is what matters for efficient durable learning.

**The magnitude of the self-grading problem. Tier A for the phenomenon; the specific percentages come from Rawson & Dunlosky (2007) as reported in the full text of Dunlosky et al. (2011), *QJEP*, which I read at the relevant passage — I did not read Rawson & Dunlosky (2007) directly.**

- With **no standard of evaluation** (i.e. judging your own recall unaided), students awarded partial or full credit to **83%** of their commission errors — responses that were entirely wrong.
- With the **correct definition available to check against** — which is exactly the Anki card-flip interaction — students *still* awarded themselves full or partial credit for **43%** of commission errors. In the same paper's experiment, the full-definition-standard group awarded credit to **63%** of commission errors, versus **19–27%** for the group that first made idea-unit-by-idea-unit judgments.
- **Lipko, Dunlosky, Hartwig, Rawson, Swan & Cook (2009), *JEP: Applied*. Tier B; abstract only.** Middle-school replication: **73%** of commission errors given credit with no standard, **44%** with a full-definition standard; idea-unit standards reduced overconfidence further.

Read together: **showing the learner the right answer and asking them to grade themselves is a known, quantified failure mode, not a neutral design choice.** It misgrades roughly two in five outright-wrong responses. Anki's design is that interaction, and the manual's own expectation that *Good* will be pressed 80–95% of the time institutionalises the optimistic bias.

**What incumbents actually deliver on this axis.**

- **Anki: structurally absent.** Grading is self-report against a revealed answer, with no scoring standard, no idea-unit decomposition, and no record of the discrepancy between predicted and actual performance.
- **Question banks (UWorld, AAMC, Blueprint, Kaplan, TPR): objective scoring, but not calibration.** Multiple choice removes the self-grading problem — the item is scored externally, which is a genuine advantage over flashcards and should be credited. But **none of them, on any page I could verify, collects a prospective confidence judgment before the answer is submitted and reports calibration error back to the learner.** They report accuracy, percentile, timing and topic breakdowns. Accuracy analytics tell you *what* you got wrong; calibration tells you *whether your sense of what you know is trustworthy*, which is what governs study-allocation decisions. I mark this **M** for question banks (a learner could keep their own confidence log) and **A** for courses and Anki.
- **AAMC: partially delivered, by accident.** Scaled scores with percentile ranks on full-lengths give a well-calibrated *global* signal — the most honest readiness estimate available. But there is no per-item or per-concept calibration instrument.

**Caveat I must state.** The calibration literature above is on **key-term definition recall in college and middle-school students**, not on passage-based applied reasoning in MCAT candidates, and it establishes that *calibration accuracy predicts retention*, not that *a calibration-training product raises exam scores*. The idea-unit intervention improved judgment accuracy and retention within those experiments (**Tier A** for that), but I found no study taking a calibration intervention into a high-stakes admissions-exam setting. The inferential step from "overconfidence causes premature termination" to "a calibration feature raises MCAT scores" is unvalidated and should be stated as engineering judgement built on a solid mechanism, not as an evidenced outcome.

### Exam-condition fidelity

**The evidence here is genuinely mixed, and it does not support the strong version of the endurance argument.**

**Evidence that within-test performance declines (Tier A for the phenomenon).** From **Brown, Kaur, Kingdon & Schofield (2024), "Cognitive Endurance as Human Capital," *Quarterly Journal of Economics*** (**Tier A**; I read the abstract, introduction and results summary from the full manuscript): in PISA and TIMSS, where question order is randomised, students are more likely to get a given question wrong when it appears later in the test. Decline heterogeneity is large — children in poor countries decline three times as fast as those in rich countries on TIMSS; in the US, Black and Hispanic students showed **72% steeper decline** than White students. In their own field experiment, control students' probability of answering correctly declined **12% from the beginning to the end** of 12–30-minute tests. Corroborating: Reyes analysed 1.9M Brazilian admissions-test takers on a 180-item exam and found a **5–7% decline** across the exam, present even within the first 10 items; Balart found a **9–11% decline** across a 25-item exam (both **Tier B**, read via a secondary conference paper, not the originals).

**Evidence that endurance is trainable (Tier B, with a large population-transfer caveat).** Brown et al. randomised **1,636 Indian primary students across six schools** to either sustained cognitive practice (maths problems, or purely non-academic games such as mazes and tangrams) or a status-quo study-hall control, delivered 1–3 times weekly for ~20-minute sessions across August–January, totalling **10–20 hours** of extra practice. Results: grades improved **0.099 SD in Hindi (p = .012), 0.092 SD in English (p = .024), 0.085 SD in maths (p = .025)** — note Hindi and English received *no* content practice. On endurance specifically, treatment reduced second-half performance decline by **21.9% (p = .006)**, with near-identical effects in the Maths arm (21.9%) and Games arm (22.0%) — i.e. content was irrelevant; sustained effort was the active ingredient. Effects **appeared only later in tests, not in the first quintile**, distinguishing endurance from confidence, motivation or working memory. Cross-randomised performance incentives raised early-test performance sharply but **did not reduce the rate of decline** — motivation is not a substitute for endurance. Gains persisted 3–5 months. A quasi-experimental birthday-cutoff analysis (5,300 students, 66 schools, Pakistan) found an additional year of schooling mitigated declines with effects ~3× the experimental intervention, concentrated in schools assigning more independent practice.

**Evidence against (Tier A/B, and it is real).**

- **Ackerman & Kanfer (2009), *JEP: Applied* 15(2), 163–181. Tier A; abstract only.** 239 first-year university students, fully counterbalanced within-subject design, completing SAT batteries of **3½, 4½ and 5½ hours**. Subjective fatigue rose with time-on-task as expected — but **mean performance was *higher* in the longer conditions.** Individual differences in personality/interest/motivation trait complexes predicted subjective fatigue better than test length did.
- **PLoS ONE (2013), "Investigating the Effects of Exam Length on Performance and Cognitive Fatigue," DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0070270. Tier B; abstract and discussion read; I did not capture the author list.** Undergraduate biology, higher-order-thinking exams, standard versus extended length. Longer exams produced **better** performance on shared items and better final-exam performance; students performed **equally or better on the second 50 questions than the first 50**. Students reported subjective fatigue that the performance data did not corroborate.
- A secondary review notes that repetitive low-stakes items are fatigue-sensitive whereas **high-stakes, complex, varied items appear not to be** — which describes the MCAT, and cuts against the endurance argument for this specific exam.

**Honest reading.** The decline effect is well documented on low-stakes, randomised-order, group-administered tests of children and adolescents. It is *not* established on high-stakes, complex, adult admissions exams — where the two best-designed studies found the opposite. Brown et al. is a strong causal demonstration that endurance is trainable, but in 6–11-year-olds in low-income Indian schools with 12–30-minute tests, which is about as far from a 22-year-old sitting a 7.5-hour passage exam as a population can get. On the product question specifically: incumbents already deliver *some* exam-condition fidelity (AAMC 7 full-lengths with real scaled scores; Kaplan 21; Blueprint 16; UWorld an interface replica plus 2 full-lengths), so "nobody tests under exam-like conditions" is false. What nobody delivers is **endurance as a dosed, progressively-loaded, measured variable** rather than as a handful of 7.5-hour events. That is a plausible design idea with a **Tier C** evidential basis for this population, and it should be sold internally as a hypothesis, not as an evidence-backed mechanism. Do not put it at the centre of a positioning claim.

## The gap

**First, what is *not* a gap — the team's hypothesis fails in three places.**

1. **"Retrieval practice doesn't transfer to application items" is false.** Pan & Rickard: **d = 0.32, k = 41, p = .013**, positive and significant, and higher than stimulus–response rearrangement (0.22), untested materials (0.16) and problem-solving (0.29). Butler (2010) found repeated testing beat repeated studying on new inference questions both within and across knowledge domains.
2. **"Nobody delivers practice problems" is false.** UWorld sells 3,000+ applied items with all-option visual rationales for $339. AAMC sells 2,710 criterion-authored items. Blueprint sells 5,000+. Kaplan and Princeton Review bundle thousands more plus all AAMC material. Jack Westin gives away a timed applied passage every day of the year.
3. **"Application practice transfers better than cued recall" is not what the literature says.** Pan & Rickard's *worked examples* sub-category is **d = 0.045** — near zero. Yeo & Fazio found repeated *studying* of worked examples beat repeated testing on immediate tests, and only matched or lost at one week. The advantage is not "problems beat flashcards"; the advantage is elaboration and repetition, whichever vehicle carries them.

**Second, the sharper version of the team's intuition, which does survive.** The problem with Anki is not that its items are the wrong *kind*. It is that Anki occupies the worst cell of Pan & Rickard's three-factor model. Its cards have no response congruency with MCAT items (the answer to a cloze is not the answer to a passage question); it gives correct-answer-only feedback, which within the application/inference category carried **Δd = −0.49**; and its non-paired-associate cloze materials sit in the **d = 0.063** sub-category. Predicted transfer with neither congruency nor elaboration: **d = 0.21** random-effects, **≈ 0** after publication-bias correction. That is the argument to make, and it is quantitative.

**Third, the actual gap. Four mechanisms, and no incumbent holds all four.**

| Mechanism | Evidence | Who delivers it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Elaborated explanation feedback on applied items** | **A** — Butler, Godbole & Marsh (2013): explanation feedback equalled correct-answer feedback on repeated questions but beat it on new inference questions. Pan & Rickard: elaborated retrieval Δd = +0.22 overall, +0.26 within application/inference; correct-answer feedback Δd = −0.49 there. | UWorld, Blueprint, courses. **Not** Anki. **Not** 76% of AAMC's non-full-length science items *[own calculation, above]*. |
| **Spaced, repeated re-retrieval of those same applied items** | **B** — Pan & Rickard, within the application/inference category: training repetitions Δd ≈ +0.29 to +0.33 per repetition; retention interval +0.0033/hour. Butler (2010) used *repeated* testing. Authors caution on df. | **Nobody.** Anki spaces facts. UWorld spaces user-made fact flashcards, not items. Question banks are single-pass by convention; a second pass is treated as contaminating the percentage. Jack Westin comes closest — daily applied passages — but never repeats a passage. |
| **Calibration measured against an external standard, reported back to the learner** | **A** for the mechanism — Dunlosky & Rawson (2012): overconfidence → premature termination → lower retention, experimentally manipulated. Rawson & Dunlosky (2007): 43% of commission errors self-awarded credit *with the correct answer visible*; 83% without; 19–27% with idea-unit standards. | **Nobody.** Anki actively institutionalises the failure mode. Question banks score objectively (better) but collect no confidence judgment and report no calibration error. AAMC's scaled score is global calibration only. |
| **Mastery measurement at concept grain, driving how much worked support each item carries** | **A** for expertise reversal (Kalyuga 2007; Kalyuga et al. 2001); **B** for adaptive fading beating fixed fading (Salden et al.). | Partially — Blueprint and UWorld recommend *what* to practise next. **Nobody** varies the amount of worked guidance by measured per-concept mastery. |

**The one-sentence gap: no product schedules repeated, spaced re-retrieval of passage-based application items with elaborated explanation feedback, while measuring whether the learner's confidence tracks their actual accuracy.** Anki has the scheduler and the wrong items and the wrong feedback. Question banks have the right items and the right feedback and no scheduler and no calibration. Jack Westin has a scheduler and the right items but only for CARS, and never repeats.

**How large is this gap, honestly? Moderate, not enormous — and the second and third rows are where the defensible novelty sits.** Row 1 is a feature UWorld already sells well; the team would be competing on execution, not on mechanism. Row 4 is real but its evidence base is in geometry tutors and circuit troubleshooting, not applied biology. Rows 2 and 3 are genuinely unoccupied and each has a mechanism-level warrant. But note the honest ceiling: Pan & Rickard's fully-favourable cell is d = 0.90 under random effects and **d = 0.58 under PEESE**, against a *restudy* control in laboratory conditions — not against a diligent student already running UWorld plus AnKing. And the sibling review in this repo (`02-mechanism-evidence.md`) documents that stacked adaptive systems deliver g ≈ 0.05–0.20 in large RCTs, not the sum of their component effect sizes. **The claim to make is mechanism completeness and time efficiency, not score superiority.**

## DOK 1 — established facts

1. Pan & Rickard (2018) meta-analysed **192 transfer effect sizes from 122 experiments in 67 articles, N = 10,382**, against non-testing reexposure controls, yielding **d = 0.40 [0.31, 0.50]**.
2. Category-level: test format **0.58** (k=56) · mediator/related cues **0.61** (k=27) · application and inference **0.32** (k=41) · problem-solving **0.29** ns (k=18) · stimulus–response rearrangement **0.22** ns (k=33) · untested materials **0.16** ns (k=17).
3. Sub-category: worked examples **d = 0.045**; medical diagnosis/treatment **d = 0.59**; non-paired-associate rearrangement **d = 0.063**; paired-associate rearrangement **d = 0.72**; untested materials without elaboration **d = 0.0028**, with elaboration **d = 0.37**.
4. Three moderators govern transfer: response congruency (Δd = 0.30–0.35), elaborated retrieval practice (Δd = 0.22), initial test accuracy (≈ 0.0058 per percentage point). Both present → **d = 0.78**; neither → **d = 0.21**; both plus max accuracy → **d = 0.90**; PEESE intercept with none present → **d = 0.015**, and **d = −0.21** at the unfavourable extreme.
5. Within the application/inference category, **correct-answer feedback was associated with Δd = −0.49** (estimated d = −0.049) and **elaborated retrieval practice with Δd = +0.26** (estimated d = 0.70). The authors flag insufficient degrees of freedom.
6. Butler, Godbole & Marsh (2013): explanation feedback and correct-answer feedback were **equivalent on repeated questions** but explanation feedback was **superior on new inference questions**, across two experiments and both between- and within-subjects manipulations.
7. Butler (2010), four experiments: repeated testing beat repeated studying on same questions, on new inference questions in the same domain, and on new inference questions in **different** domains, at a one-week delay.
8. Morris, Bransford & Franks (1977): rhyme encoding beat semantic encoding on a rhyming recognition test while semantic beat rhyme on a standard test — memory depends on the match between encoding and criterion operations, not depth alone.
9. Brunmair & Richter (2019): overall interleaving **g = 0.42** [0.34, 0.50] (238 effect sizes, 158 samples, N = 8,466); paintings **0.67**, mathematics **0.34**, **words −0.39** (blocking better).
10. Yeo & Fazio (2019): for novel maths procedures, repeated **studying** beat repeated testing on immediate tests regardless of materials; at one week, repeated testing matched studying with non-identical problems and beat it with identical problems.
11. Kalyuga et al. (2001) / Kalyuga (2007): worked examples help novices and become redundant or harmful with growing expertise, at which point problem solving is superior; Salden et al. found **adaptive** fading > fixed fading > plain tutored problem solving.
12. Dunlosky & Rawson (2012), two studies (accuracy manipulated in one, measured as an individual difference in the other): greater absolute judgment accuracy predicted higher two-day retention; overconfidence caused **premature termination of study**; not explained by feedback, effort or trials to criterion.
13. Rawson & Dunlosky (2007): students awarded partial or full credit to **83%** of commission errors with no standard and **43%** even with the correct definition available. In the related experiment, **63%** with a full-definition standard versus **19–27%** with idea-unit judgments. Lipko et al. (2009), middle school: **73%** → **44%**.
14. Anki's design (manual/FAQ): four self-graded buttons; guidance that *Again* is used 5–20% of the time and *Good* 80–95%; SM-2 starting ease 2.50 with *Again* −20 pp, *Hard* −15 pp, *Easy* +15 pp; new cards gathered in alphabetical subdeck order; random fuzz on review intervals.
15. MCAT (AAMC): four sections, **230 items**, **6 h 15 min** content time, **≈ 7 h 30 min** seated time. Chem/Phys 59 (10 passages + 15 discrete, 95 min) · CARS 53 (9 passages, all passage-based, 90 min) · Bio/Biochem 59 (95 min) · Psych/Soc 59 (95 min). **185 of 230 items (80.4%) are passage-based** *[own calculation]*.

AAMC Official Prep: Online-Only Bundle **$323.70** with **2,710 unique questions**; practice exams **$35** each with all-option explanations plus Khan Academy links; Section Banks **$45**/300 items; Question Packs **$15**/120 items; Independent Question Bank **$10**/150 items. Seven full-lengths exist, two free.

AAMC rationale coverage: Section Bank Vol. 1, all four science Question Packs and the Independent Question Bank give **correct-answer rationale only**; Section Bank Vol. 2, both CARS Question Packs and the CARS Diagnostic give **all four option rationales**. **300 of 1,230 non-full-length science items (24.4%) carry elaborated rationales** *[own calculation]*.
18. UWorld MCAT QBank: from **$339** (90/180/360-day tiers), **3,000+** items, **2** full-length practice exams, exam-replica interface, **rationales for every answer choice with visuals**, spaced-repetition flashcards, AI tutor, adaptive planner.
19. Kaplan: from **$1,599**, **21** full-lengths (incl. 7 AAMC), **3,000+** QBank, 8 books, 700+ hours guided prep, 515+/15-point money-back guarantee on premium tiers. Princeton Review Ultimate: **123** live hours, **18** full-lengths, 12 section exams, 89 diagnostic quizzes, 500+ videos. Blueprint self-paced: **5,000+** questions, **16** full-lengths (10 Blueprint + 6 AAMC), 160 modules, adaptive planner, AI Qbank, "Blue" AI CARS tutor.

21. Deng, Gluckstein & Larsen (2015), n = 72, R² = .672: **445 additional boards-style MCQs** or **1,700 additional unique Anki cards** each associated with **one additional Step 1 point**; Firecracker flashcards did not predict. **One MCQ ≈ 3.8 flashcards per item** *[own calculation]*. Erratum corrected the Anki coefficient to 5.89 × 10⁻⁴.

## DOK 2 — insights and implications

1. **The correct argument against Anki is quantitative and it is about feedback, not item type.** Pan & Rickard's three-factor model puts an Anki cloze review at **d ≈ 0.21** transfer to a different criterion (≈ 0 after bias correction), because it has neither response congruency with an MCAT item nor elaborated retrieval practice. Add the category-specific finding that correct-answer feedback carried **Δd = −0.49** for application/inference transfer, and the case is made without ever claiming flashcards "don't work." Lead with this, not with "flashcards aren't practice problems."
2. **The team's stated gap is occupied; the adjacent gap is not.** Application practice with elaborate explanations is UWorld's core product. What no product does is *schedule* application items for repeated retrieval over time. Question banks treat a second encounter with an item as contamination of a predictive statistic — a measurement concern that has silently overridden a learning mechanism. Pan & Rickard's within-category repetition moderator (Δd ≈ +0.29 to +0.33 per repetition) says that is the wrong trade.
3. **AAMC's feedback structure is the market's biggest unexploited weakness, and it is verifiable from AAMC's own comparison table.** Three quarters of AAMC's non-full-length science items carry correct-answer-only rationales *[own calculation]*. These are the highest-response-congruency items in existence — the exact items where elaborated feedback would compound most, per Pan & Rickard's simultaneous moderator fit. A product that supplies genuine explanation feedback *for AAMC items* is operating on the strongest cell in the entire framework. Note the obvious constraint: several competitors already do this (Jack Westin's Chrome extension, Kaplan's 400+ AAMC explainer videos), so this is contested ground, not open ground.
4. **Metacognitive calibration is the cleanest structural absence in the market and the best-evidenced mechanism nobody sells.** Self-graded recall misgrades ~43% of outright-wrong responses even with the answer visible, and Anki's manual normalises pressing *Good* 80–95% of the time. Question banks fix the scoring problem but collect no confidence judgment, so they tell learners what they got wrong without telling them whether their sense of their own knowledge can be trusted — which is the input to every study-allocation decision they make. A prospective confidence rating per item plus a calibration-error report is cheap to build, has an A-tier mechanism behind it, and is currently sold by nobody.
5. **But be disciplined about what the calibration evidence licenses.** It establishes that calibration accuracy predicts retention and that idea-unit standards improve both. It does not establish that a calibration feature raises MCAT scores. That inferential step is unvalidated. Sell it as "the only product that tells you whether to trust your own confidence," not as a score claim.
6. **Drop endurance training from the positioning.** The two best-designed studies on adults taking long, complex, high-stakes tests (Ackerman & Kanfer 2009; the PLoS ONE biology-exam study) found *no* fatigue decrement and in fact higher performance on longer tests. The strong decline evidence is on children and adolescents taking low-stakes tests, and the strong trainability evidence is on 6–11-year-olds in Indian primary schools with 12–30-minute tests. The mechanism is plausible and Brown et al. is a good paper, but the population gap is too wide to build a claim on. Keep exam-condition fidelity as table stakes (everybody has full-lengths); do not make endurance a differentiator.
7. **Do not count Anki's interleaving as a benefit, and do not count your own either — unless you interleave *formats*.** Brunmair & Richter found interleaving *hurt* for word materials (**g = −0.39**) while helping for mathematics (0.34) and complex visual material (0.67). Cloze fact cards are word-like. The interleaving that plausibly helps here is **format** interleaving — mixing fact retrieval with applied passage items in one session — which Anki structurally cannot do and which AAMC full-lengths do by accident. That is a cleaner claim than topic interleaving and it is the one column in the map where Anki is **A** and AAMC is **D**.
8. **Expertise reversal implies mastery measurement is a prerequisite for getting worked-example dosing right, and nobody in this market measures mastery at concept grain.** Blueprint and UWorld recommend *what* to do next; neither varies *how much worked support* an item carries based on measured per-concept mastery. Salden et al.'s adaptive-versus-fixed-fading result is the relevant warrant, and it is from a geometry Cognitive Tutor — so this is a B-tier bet in an untested domain, worth building but not worth headlining.
9. **The honest efficacy ceiling is a time-efficiency claim, not a score claim.** Pan & Rickard's best cell (d = 0.90 random-effects, 0.58 PEESE) is measured against *restudy* in laboratory conditions, not against a student already running UWorld plus AnKing plus AAMC. Combined with the sibling review's finding that stacked adaptive systems land at g ≈ 0.05–0.20 in large RCTs, any claim of the form "our mechanism stack beats the incumbent stack on score" is unsupportable. "Same mechanisms, fewer hours, plus one mechanism nobody has" is supportable.
10. **The Deng et al. per-item ratio is rhetorically useful and analytically weak — handle with care.** 445 MCQs ≈ 1,700 flashcards ≈ 1 Step 1 point implies one question is worth 3.8 cards *[own calculation]*, which sounds like it settles the argument. It does not: it is correlational, n = 72, one school, self-reported, on a different exam, and crucially it does not equate *time* — a UWorld passage set takes far longer per item than a cloze card, so the per-minute comparison could easily reverse. The Rocky Vista cohort found no significant Anki–score relationship at all. Use it as colour, never as proof.
11. **Jack Westin has already proved the "spaced application practice as a daily habit" mechanism works commercially — in one section.** 365 unique free timed passages with streaks is exactly the mechanism combination row 2 of the gap table describes, restricted to CARS and P/S and without repetition, mastery or calibration. That is both validation of the concept and a warning that the idea is not unthinkable to incumbents.

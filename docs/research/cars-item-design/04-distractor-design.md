# Lane 04 — Distractor design

The lane with the widest gap between what is practised and what is evidenced. Read §1 before §6.

## 1. The headline: the popular taxonomy has no authoritative source

The familiar distractor-family list — *true-but-irrelevant, too-extreme, too-narrow, too-broad, opposite, plausible-but-unsupported, right-answer-to-wrong-question, half-right/half-wrong, distortion, uses-passage-vocabulary-wrong-relation* — **is not from the item-writing literature.** I searched for it and found it in IELTS coaching sites, GMAT/CAT verbal-ability pages, and MCAT prep material. **[FOLKLORE].**

The closest published thing is the **5Ps typology** (ERIC EJ1505365, https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1505365.pdf): *plausible, peripheral, polyconceptual, prejudicial, pragmatic*. It is EFL-specific, recent, derived from expert consultation rather than response data, and **not validated against selection rates or discrimination**. Its most useful contribution is a rationale for *peripheral* (near-irrelevant) distractors that is not "padding": *"Peripheral distractors are used more to ensure fairness, avoiding options that are [obviously] incorrect. Good distractors keep the question from being too easy and keep from inflating scores and reducing the diagnostic value of a question."* **[PEER, weakly]**

**So the honest position is:** the family list is a useful authoring vocabulary and a plausible QA checklist, and it is **not evidence**. §6 keeps it, labels it, and pins each family to whichever peer-reviewed mechanism it is really an instance of. The mechanisms in §2–§5 are the load-bearing content of this lane.

**This is also the single largest genuine hole in the evidence base**, and it sits exactly where the brief identified the most important part of the problem. It is therefore an opportunity: coding your own generated items by family and correlating family membership with response data would be novel, publishable, and directly product-serving. See 00-README gap 5.

## 2. Haladyna, Downing & Rodriguez — the 31 guidelines

**[PEER]** *A Review of Multiple-Choice Item-Writing Guidelines for Classroom Assessment*, Applied Measurement in Education 15(3), 309–333, 2002. Full text: https://cmapspublic3.ihmc.us/rid=1P2XTLCSS-11K09T9-BD5/Haladyna_2002_-Appl_Meas_Educ.pdf

Validated against two sources: consensus across **27 educational-testing textbooks** and **27 research studies and reviews published since 1990**. Supersedes the 1989 taxonomy of 43 rules (Haladyna & Downing, *Applied Measurement in Education* 2(1), 37–50 and 51–78). Structure: 8 content · 2 formatting · 3 style · 4 stem · **14 choices**, with 6 sub-variants under Guideline 28. The weighting toward option-writing is the point — 20 of 43 rules in 1989 addressed options, and the authors read that as *"a general concern about the importance of options."*

### Table 1, verbatim

**Content concerns**
1. Every item should reflect specific content and a single specific mental behavior, as called for in test specifications (two-way grid, test blueprint).
2. Base each item on important content to learn; avoid trivial content.
3. Use novel material to test higher level learning. Paraphrase textbook language or language used during instruction when used in a test item to avoid testing for simply recall.
4. Keep the content of each item independent from content of other items on the test.
5. Avoid over specific and over general content when writing MC items.
6. Avoid opinion-based items.
7. Avoid trick items.
8. Keep vocabulary simple for the group of students being tested.

**Formatting concerns**

9. Use the question, completion, and best answer versions of the conventional MC, the alternate choice, true-false (TF), multiple true-false (MTF), matching, and the context-dependent item and item set formats, but AVOID the complex MC (Type K) format.
10. Format the item vertically instead of horizontally.

**Style concerns**

11. Edit and proof items.
12. Use correct grammar, punctuation, capitalization, and spelling.
13. Minimize the amount of reading in each item.

**Writing the stem**

14. Ensure that the directions in the stem are very clear.
15. Include the central idea in the stem instead of the choices.
16. Avoid window dressing (excessive verbiage).
17. Word the stem positively, avoid negatives such as NOT or EXCEPT. If negative words are used, use the word cautiously and always ensure that the word appears capitalized and boldface.

**Writing the choices**

18. Develop as many effective choices as you can, but research suggests three is adequate.
19. Make sure that only one of these choices is the right answer.
20. Vary the location of the right answer according to the number of choices.
21. Place choices in logical or numerical order.
22. Keep choices independent; choices should not be overlapping.
23. Keep choices homogeneous in content and grammatical structure.
24. Keep the length of choices about equal.
25. None-of-the-above should be used carefully.
26. Avoid All-of-the-above.
27. Phrase choices positively; avoid negatives such as NOT.
28. Avoid giving clues to the right answer, such as **(a)** Specific determiners including always, never, completely, and absolutely. **(b)** Clang associations, choices identical to or resembling words in the stem. **(c)** Grammatical inconsistencies that cue the test-taker to the correct choice. **(d)** Conspicuous correct choice. **(e)** Pairs or triplets of options that clue the test-taker to the correct choice. **(f)** Blatantly absurd, ridiculous options.
29. Make all distractors plausible.
30. Use typical errors of students to write your distractors.
31. Use humor if it is compatible with the teacher and the learning environment.

### The authors' own caveat, which matters

> "Research on these MC item-writing guidelines has been asystematic. The number of studies bearing on any single guideline in this analysis is small relative to the number of guidelines."

Four guidelines have received nearly all the empirical attention: **18** (number of options), **25** (NOTA), **26** (AOTA), and **29** (plausibility). And the authors note that plausibility research is *"linked to Guideline 18"* — i.e. the plausibility evidence *is* the option-count evidence. **So the rule that matters most to you (29, at 96% consensus) has essentially no independent empirical base.** It is a strong professional consensus, not a finding.

### Guidelines that do not transfer to CARS

- **G8 "Keep vocabulary simple"** — directly conflicts with [OFFICIAL] *"sophisticated vocabulary."* G8 is aimed at construct-irrelevant vocabulary load in content tests. In a reading test, passage vocabulary is on-construct. **Apply G8 to stems and options, never to the passage.**
- **G3 "Use novel material"** — automatically satisfied; every CARS passage is novel to the reader.
- **G4 "Keep items independent"** — cannot hold within a set; all 5–7 items share a passage. G9 explicitly blesses *"the context-dependent item and item set formats,"* which is what CARS is. Enforce independence **within** a set differently: no item may reveal another's answer.
- **G21 "Logical or numerical order"** — mostly inapplicable to prose options. Interesting empirical wrinkle: Huntley & Welch tested 32 ACT maths items in ordered vs random option order and found **no difficulty difference but higher discrimination with randomly ordered distractors**, concluding random order *"may pose obstacles for lower ability students."* [PEER] The authors still recommend logical ordering.
- **G25/G26 NOTA/AOTA** — do not use either. All five NOTA studies found it **increased item difficulty**; three of four found no discrimination change [PEER]. AOTA: MTF versions of AOTA items were far more difficult, nearly all examinees picked AOTA when offered, attributed to cuing, and **reliability was significantly reduced** [PEER]. For a four-option comparative-judgement format neither has any role.
- **G6 "Avoid opinion-based items"** — needs care. CARS items are constantly *about* opinions (the author's). The rule targets items where the **key** is a matter of opinion. CARS keys are matters of textual evidence about an opinion. Distinct; keep the distinction explicit in review training.

## 3. NBME's flaw catalogue — an independent convergent source

**[PEER]** NBME *Item-Writing Guide*, https://acvd.memberclicks.net/assets/docs/AOK/NBME_Item%20Writing%20Guide_R_6.pdf. Independently developed by a different medical-testing organisation and converges on the same mechanisms, which is the best validity evidence the guideline literature has.

Its framing is better than Haladyna's because it names the **two distinct harms**:

> "1. A flaw that **adds irrelevant difficulty** to the item can confuse all test-takers. These flaws make the item challenging for reasons unrelated to the testing objective... and can add construct-irrelevant variance to the final test score.
> 2. A flaw that **cues the more savvy and confident test-takers (aka the 'testwise')** and aids them in guessing the right answer... it easier for some students to answer the item correctly based on their test-taking skills alone, without necessarily knowing the content."

And the standard: *"A test-taker's probability of answering an item correctly should be determined by his or her amount of expertise on the topic being assessed; ideally, that probability will not decrease due to a suboptimally written item and will not increase due to test-taking strategies."*

### The one-best-answer principle, which is exactly CARS's format

> "Even though the incorrect options are not completely wrong, they are less correct than the 'keyed answer'... As long as the options can be laid out on a single continuum... **distractors in one-best-answer items do not have to be totally wrong.**"

This is the same standard as AAMC's *"most likely and most reasonable"* [OFFICIAL] and it has a strong operational corollary NBME states directly: *"The item writer should be able to rank order each option on the same dimension."*

**[INFERENCE] Adopt this as a hard authoring requirement: every four-option CARS item must have a single named dimension along which all four options can be ranked, and the key must be the extremum.** For A1 the dimension is scope-and-stance fidelity; for A9 it is structural correspondence; for A10.d it is thesis disruption (where the key is the *minimum*, not maximum). If you cannot name the dimension, the option set is heterogeneous and G23 is violated. This one requirement subsumes a large fraction of the prep-industry family list, because most of those families are just "off the dimension" or "wrong end of the dimension."

### NBME flaws, with the CARS-relevant translation

| NBME flaw | Mechanism | CARS translation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Collectively exhaustive subset** | Testwise reader spots that a subset (increase / decrease / no change) covers all outcomes and eliminates the rest | Fatal in A10.a (support/contradict/neutral). If three options tile the logical space, the fourth is free to eliminate |
| **Absolute vs hedged terms** | *"options A, B, and E contain terms that are less absolute than those in C and D. The testwise student will eliminate C and D... because they are less likely to be true"* | **This is the mechanism behind the entire "extreme options are wrong" folklore.** It is a *flaw*, not a design principle — see §5 |
| **Longest / most-detailed correct answer** | Writers craft the key first and lavish detail on it | Directly measurable; lint for it |
| **Word repetition from stem (clang)** | *"the word 'unreal' in the vignette can clue test-takers to... 'derealization'"*; also warns about etymological repetition | In CARS the analogue is **repeating distinctive passage vocabulary in the key**. Prep-industry advice to prefer paraphrased options over verbatim-vocabulary options is an artefact of *this* flaw |
| **Convergence cluing** | *"the correct answer is the option that has the most in common with the other options, and thus the testwise test-taker can converge on the right answer just by counting the number of times certain terms appear"* | Subtle, frequent, and **absent from the prep-industry list entirely.** Worth an automated check: tokenise options, flag when the key has maximal lexical overlap with the distractor set |
| **Grammatical non-parallelism** | Options that do not follow grammatically from the lead-in are eliminable | Fix: closed lead-ins, uniform option grammar |
| **Heterogeneous option length/structure** | Inflates reading time; *"can shift the construct that is being measured from content knowledge to reading speed"* | In a 53-item/90-minute section this converts reasoning variance into speed variance |

## 4. What a distractor *does* — the only mechanistic account

**[PEER]** Embretson & Wetzel, *Component Latent Trait Models for Paragraph Comprehension Tests*, Applied Psychological Measurement 11(2), 175–193, 1987. Full text: https://conservancy.umn.edu/bitstreams/d4abeede-25f1-44f2-bcd4-c1db83fb8a90/download

They decompose the item into **text representation** and **response decision**, and the decision stage into three events: **encoding** the options, **mapping** them to the text, and **evaluating truth status**. Truth evaluation is two-stage: **falsification** then **confirmation**.

The passage that should shape your distractor design, verbatim:

> "The first evaluation stage is falsification, in which the examinee attempts to falsify as many alternatives as possible. **In many multiple-choice items, the distractors are not wholly false or their truth status cannot be determined.** In the initial componential models of decision processes (Sternberg, 1977), falsification was the method by which an alternative could be eliminated. However, **in paragraph comprehension items, often no information in the text contributes to falsifying a distractor. Thus, the alternative could possibly be true, but the paragraph is mute with respect to the assertions contained in the alternative.** An item is difficult if few alternatives are falsifiable."

This gives you a **three-state truth status per option**, which is far more useful than right/wrong:

```
CONFIRMED    the passage confirms it            → this is the key
FALSIFIED    the passage explicitly refutes it  → eliminable from the text
MUTE         the passage says nothing either way → NOT eliminable from the text
```

Their operationalisations, all measurable at authoring time:

| Variable | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| **Text mapping difficulty** | *"the proportion of text over which the relevant propositions are located"* — easy when in one short sentence, hard when spread throughout or when the option concerns the *"gist"* |
| **Falsification difficulty** | *"1.0 minus the probability that the correct answer could be selected by random guessing among the remaining non-falsified set"* |
| **Confirmation** | dichotomous: does the text confirm the key? |
| **Encoding conversion** | dichotomous: must the option's wording be converted to the text's? |
| **Propositional comparisons** | log of the number of propositional comparisons needed to falsify or confirm an option |

**Falsification difficulty is a formula you can compute directly.** With four options: if three are falsifiable, difficulty = 1 − 1/1 = 0. If one is, difficulty = 1 − 1/3 ≈ .67. If none are, = 1 − 1/4 = .75. **[INFERENCE]** This is the cleanest quantitative distractor-design lever in the entire review, and it means **`MUTE` distractors are the hard ones** — the reader must positively confirm the key rather than eliminate its rivals.

That maps directly onto the prep-industry family *plausible-but-unsupported*, and explains why it is the family students find hardest: it is not "tricky," it is genuinely non-eliminable from the text. But see §5 — there is a construct catch.

## 5. The falsifiability catch — a construct-validity finding, not a difficulty finding

**[PEER]** Ozuru, Rowe, O'Reilly & McNamara, *Where's the difficulty in standardized reading tests: The passage or the question?*, Behavior Research Methods 40(4), 1001–1015, 2008. https://doi.org/10.3758/brm.40.4.1001

They coded, per Embretson & Wetzel, whether each distractor was explicitly falsifiable from the passage, and found:

> "A negative coefficient (−.035) of falsifiability indicates that an item tends to be **more difficult when one or more distractors are falsifiable than when no distractors are falsifiable, a rather surprising trend.**"

Surprising, and it interacted with sentence length and word frequency, so treat the direction as unstable. But their interpretation is the part that matters:

> "This finding is consistent with Embretson and Wetzel's (1987) findings and indicates that **the quality of distractor options in multiple-choice tests is an important attribute of an assessment. Unfortunately, this result implies that the GMRT 7th–9th grade level is influenced by a variable that has little to do with reading comprehension, given that eliminating improbable answer options is associated more strongly with general test-taking skills than with reading comprehension skills.**"

And, on test-maker strategy:

> "some specific groups of extreme items included in the test can also be perceived as the test makers' strategy to increase the range of test discrimination by including almost unnaturally easy or difficult items. These examples include **items with several falsifiable distractors (to make an item easy)** and items based on passages composed of extremely long sentences with low-frequency words."

**[INFERENCE] The design conclusion, and it is the most important one in this lane.** Falsifiability is a **construct-relevance lever first and a difficulty lever second**. Items whose distractors are eliminable by strategy rather than by reading measure testwiseness. So:

- Prefer distractors that are **`MUTE`** or **`FALSIFIED-only-by-careful-reading**, over distractors falsifiable by surface cue.
- Prefer distractors that require **`CONFIRMED` vs `MUTE` discrimination**, which is reading, over **`FALSIFIED` elimination**, which is partly strategy.
- Never build difficulty by making distractors *less* falsifiable through vagueness — that is the `vague_or_evasive_language` defect (Lane 01) turned inward on your own options.

This also explains, mechanistically, why the "extreme options are wrong" heuristic works on badly written tests: an extreme option is *cheaply* falsifiable — the reader eliminates it on the word *always*, without consulting the passage. That is precisely the testwiseness pathway. **The folklore heuristic is a diagnostic for the flaw NBME names.** Its existence in the prep world is evidence that many published items contain it. The fix, per Lane 02 §4, is passage-side: vary genuine commitment in the prose so that option-level commitment stops being diagnostic.

## 6. Distractor families, labelled

**[FOLKLORE] as a taxonomy.** Retained as authoring vocabulary, each pinned to the peer-reviewed mechanism it instantiates, with truth status per §4 and a construct-risk rating per §5.

| # | Family | Truth status | Real mechanism | Construct risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| D1 | **True-but-irrelevant** — accurate per the passage, answers nothing asked | CONFIRMED (of something else) | Off the ranking dimension (NBME); 5Ps *peripheral* | Low. Good family: requires stem re-reading, not strategy |
| D2 | **Right-answer-to-wrong-question** — would be the key for a different stem on this passage | CONFIRMED (of another item) | Off-dimension; G15 stem focus | Low. Strong family. Especially natural in item sets, where the sibling item's key is a ready-made D2 |
| D3 | **Too extreme / absolute** — correct direction, overstated commitment | FALSIFIED cheaply | NBME *absolute vs hedged terms*; G28(a) *specific determiners* | **High.** Eliminable on a single word. Use only when the passage contains genuine strong commitment so the cue is non-diagnostic |
| D4 | **Too narrow** — true of part of the passage, offered as whole-passage | CONFIRMED locally, MUTE globally | Scope error on the ranking dimension | Low. The canonical A1 distractor and a good one |
| D5 | **Too broad** — exceeds the passage's scope | MUTE | Generalisation-appropriateness (an [OFFICIAL] evaluation criterion) | Low–moderate |
| D6 | **Opposite / reversal** — inverts a passage relation | FALSIFIED | Falsification; text negation | Moderate. Genuinely eliminable by reading, but often *too* eliminable. Reversals of *distant* relations are much better than of local ones |
| D7 | **Plausible-but-unsupported** — true in the world, absent from the passage | **MUTE** | Embretson & Wetzel: *"the paragraph is mute with respect to the assertions contained in the alternative"* | **Low risk, high value — and the family with the strongest mechanistic warrant.** Also the family that most directly enforces the [OFFICIAL] "outside knowledge is inadmissible" rule, because selecting it *is* the error the rule forbids |
| D8 | **Half-right / half-wrong** — compound option, one clause supported, one not | mixed | Propositional comparisons (log count required) | Low, but violates G22/G24 if it makes the option longer. Keep compounds uniform across all four options or not at all |
| D9 | **Distortion** — a real passage claim with one element altered | FALSIFIED on close reading | Encoding conversion; propositional comparison | **Low risk, high value.** Requires actual text mapping. Pair with D7 |
| D10 | **Passage vocabulary, wrong relation** — reuses distinctive terms, misstates their relation | FALSIFIED on close reading | Inverse of G28(b) *clang* — here the lure is deliberate | Low. Legitimate *when the key paraphrases*; but note this makes verbatim-vocabulary a distractor signal, which is a systematic cue if used every time. **Vary it: sometimes the key should carry the passage's vocabulary** |
| D11 | **Surface-similar, structurally wrong** (A9 only) | MUTE | [OFFICIAL]: *"looking beneath surface imagery to discern underlying relationships"* | Low. The one family AAMC essentially specifies |

**[INFERENCE] Recommended default composition** for a four-option item, given §4 and §5: **one key (CONFIRMED), one FALSIFIED-on-close-reading (D9/D10/D6-distant), one MUTE (D7/D5), one off-dimension-or-scope (D1/D2/D4).** This yields falsification difficulty ≈ 1 − 1/3 ≈ .67, requires text mapping for one distractor and positive confirmation against another, and avoids any cheaply eliminable option. Deviate deliberately to move difficulty, and record which composition each item used so you can correlate with response data later.

## 7. How many distractors actually work — the numbers

**[PEER]** Three independent datasets, consistent conclusion.

**Haladyna & Downing (1993)**, *How Many Options is Enough for a Multiple-Choice Test Item?*, Educational and Psychological Measurement 53(4), 999–1010. Four standardized tests:
- *"about two thirds of all items had one or two effectively performing distractors, with between 1% and 8% of all items having three effective distractors"*
- **modal number of effective distractors per item = one**
- no relation between number of effective distractors and difficulty, but **items with more effective distractors were more discriminating**
- in a 200-item exam for US physicians: **no item had four functioning distractors; only 8.4% had three**

**Rogausch et al.**, Swiss Federal graduation exam, 737 questions: at the 5% selection threshold, **only 2.8% had four functional distractors.**

**Cardiff undergraduate medical SBAs** (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4923093/), 480 items / 1,920 distractors:
- mean functional distractors per item = **1.82**
- distribution: **0 → 14.2% · 1 → 26.5% · 2 → 33.1% · 3 → 19.2% · 4 → 7.1%**
- **55.3%** of all distractors non-functional; **17.8%** never chosen by anyone
- of the 44.6% functional, only **10.7%** were chosen by >20% of examinees
- **14.2% of items were completely non-discriminatory**

Haladyna/Downing/Rodriguez's own read, verbatim: *"it is very unlikely that item writers can write three distractors that have item response patterns consistent with the idea of plausibility."*

**Standard definition of "functional":** selected by **≥5%** of the cohort. Some authors add negative discrimination as a second criterion.

### The finding that actually matters for authoring

**Reliability loss on option reduction depends entirely on *which* distractor is removed.**

> "There was an average reduction in reliability of .016 when reducing the number of options from 5 to 3 including all four methods of deletion (not significantly different than zero). However, this reduction was a function of the distractor deletion method: **for the trials deleting ineffective distractors, no change in reliability was observed (.006); whereas trials randomly deleting distractors yielded an average reduction in reliability of .059** (a statistically significant difference: Q_BETWEEN = 108, much larger than the χ²[1] = 3.8 critical value for p < .05)."

Same pattern 4→2: ineffective-deletion **−.040** vs random **−.089**. *"In both cases, random distractor deletion was significantly more damaging than deleting ineffective distractors."*

Rodriguez's summary, verbatim: *"Moving from 5- to 3-option items reduces item difficulty by .07, does not affect item discrimination, and does not affect reliability on average. However, when eliminating random distractors to create 3-option items, reliability drops .06 on average, with no change if ineffective distractors are deleted."*

Validity evidence is thin and he says so: only two studies, both static-form, and *"the argument made by so many researchers regarding the increase in validity... due to the opportunity to increase the number of items because of the reduction in options has not been empirically tested."* The one dissenting team (Budescu & Nevo) showed the proportionality assumption fails — response time is not a simple function of option count — and argued three would be insufficient.

### What this means for a four-option CARS clone

**CARS is four-option and that is [OFFICIAL].** You must ship four. So:

1. **Do not read this literature as "ship three."** Read it as: your fourth option is statistically likely to be inert, and the *marginal* fourth distractor is where quality collapses.
2. **The 4→3 row is the interesting one.** Dropping to three *increases* discrimination (+.031) and reliability (+.019). That means the fourth option in typical four-option items is **actively slightly harmful**. Your fourth distractor must clear a real bar or it makes the item worse than a three-option version would be.
3. **[INFERENCE] Concrete rule: write the key and two strong distractors first, from different families in §6, then write the fourth against a stated purpose** — usually the 5Ps *peripheral* role, or a second MUTE option to raise falsification difficulty. Never write a fourth to fill a slot. The Cardiff 17.8%-never-chosen figure is what slot-filling produces.
4. **Expect and measure inertness.** Target ≥2 functional distractors per item (≥5% selection). Getting 3 is better than the published field achieves. Getting 4 is not a realistic target and pretending otherwise will cause you to reject good items.

## 9. Authoring protocol

**[INFERENCE]**, assembled from everything above.

1. **Name the ranking dimension.** Per NBME: all four options must be orderable on one dimension, key at the extremum. If you cannot name it, stop.
2. **Write the key against the passage, recording `evidence_span`.** Feeds the FOC/RWT tag (Lane 01 §3) and the difficulty features (Lane 05).
3. **Write two strong distractors from different §6 families**, at least one MUTE and one FALSIFIED-on-close-reading.
4. **Write the fourth against a stated purpose.** Record the purpose.
5. **Record per option:** family, truth status, and — for FALSIFIED — the passage span that falsifies it and whether falsification requires reading or only a surface cue.
6. **Compute falsification difficulty** = 1 − 1/(count of non-falsified options).
7. **Run the six G28 cluing checks**, all automatable: specific determiners · clang / stem-word repetition · grammatical parallelism from lead-in · key length and detail vs distractors · pairs and triplets · absurd options.
8. **Run the two NBME checks** the prep list misses: collectively-exhaustive subset · convergence cluing (key has maximal lexical overlap with distractor set).
9. **Run the passage-withheld leakage test** (Lane 02 §2). Mandatory for A8.
10. **For A6, run the negation check** (Lane 03 A6): negate the key, verify the argument fails; negate each distractor, verify it survives.
11. **Check the cheap-elimination trap:** if any distractor is eliminable without consulting the passage, rewrite it. This is §5's construct-validity rule and it is the one most likely to be skipped.

## 10. QA protocol once response data exists

**[PEER]**, from NBME's option-analysis guidance.

> "Were any of the options not selected? This is a sign that these options were not plausible or could be ruled out due to a structural flaw or the sophistication of test-takers, and thus may need to be rewritten. Was any incorrect option chosen more often than expected, or chosen more often than the key? If the option was somewhat more likely to be chosen, this is an indication that the item could have more than one right answer; **if much more likely, this is a sign that the item is probably miskeyed.**"

Decision rules:

| Observation | Action |
| --- | --- |
| Distractor selected by <5% | Non-functional. Rewrite or repurpose |
| Distractor never selected | Implausible. Rewrite |
| Distractor selected slightly more than key | Likely **two defensible keys** — violates [OFFICIAL] "one defensible and demonstrably correct" |
| Distractor selected much more than key | Likely **miskeyed** |
| Item–total correlation ≈ 0 | Measuring something else, or flawed |
| Item–total correlation negative | Probably miskeyed or contains a cue that misleads high scorers |
| Difficulty far from expectation | Review structure for irrelevant difficulty |

NBME's warning, worth quoting because it is the failure mode of iterative item repair: *"Keep in mind that revising options to make them more plausible can change the difficulty and discrimination of the item, sometimes in unpredictable ways."* An edited item is a new item and needs recalibration.

## Evidence quality for this lane

**Strongest:** §7 and §8. The functional-distractor counts replicate across three independent datasets in three countries, and Rodriguez's tables are a proper precision-weighted meta-analysis with standard errors, homogeneity tests, and a moderator analysis. The distractor-deletion-method finding (Q_BETWEEN = 108) is the most robust actionable result in the review.

**Solid but consensus-not-evidence:** §2. The 31 guidelines are a professional consensus validated by textbook agreement, and the authors are unusually candid that empirical support is *"asystematic"* and concentrated on four rules. **Guideline 29 — make distractors plausible — is the rule this lane most depends on and it has essentially no independent empirical base.** §3's NBME material is convergent expert practice, which raises confidence without being a study.

**Interesting but thin:** §4 and §5. Embretson & Wetzel is one 1987 study on 75 ASVAB paragraph-comprehension items; Ozuru et al. is one 2008 study on the Gates-MacGinitie at two grade bands, with a falsifiability effect that was **marginal, direction-surprising, and interaction-dependent**, and coding reliability poor enough that they had to dichotomise the variable and report the finer counts *"for descriptive purposes only."* I have leaned hard on the *interpretation* of these two papers because it is the only mechanistic account available — but the empirical base under §5's central recommendation is **two studies and a marginal coefficient.** Treat the construct-validity argument as sound reasoning, not as a demonstrated effect.

**Weakest, and flagged as such throughout:** §1 and §6. The family taxonomy is folklore. §6's truth-status and construct-risk columns are my mapping onto §4–§5, not published findings. §9's recommended composition and §6's default mix are [INFERENCE] with no direct empirical support.

**Not established at all:** whether any of the option-count literature transfers to 500-word argumentative verbal-reasoning items. 19 of 56 trials were language arts, which is the nearest, and none is a high-stakes verbal-reasoning test of this kind. See 00-README gap 8.

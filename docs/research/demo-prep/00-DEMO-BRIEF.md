# Demo brief

For a demo and pitch of the original idea plus the current state of the app. Synthesised from
the research bundles in `../daily-planner-efficacy/`, `../effectiveness-eval/`,
`../calibration/`, `../cars-duel/`, and the repo audit in `../repo-state/`.

Two companion lanes are being researched separately: `01-market-and-evidence-bar.md`
(volume, pricing, what evidence buyers demand) and `02-voice-of-the-customer.md` (what
students say their problem is versus what it is).

---

## The narrative that actually works

Do not pitch this as a finished product with a score claim. Every competitor does that, two
of them were ordered to stop in April 2024, and a sophisticated audience has heard it.

Pitch it as **the only MCAT product whose method has been checked against the evidence, by
people willing to say which parts failed.**

The arc, in four beats:

1. **The origin insight.** The most important lesson isn't what the right answer was — it's
   *why you got it wrong*. Everything started there.
2. **We went and checked it.** Not vibes: the transfer literature, the metacomprehension
   literature, the psychometrics of score gains, the SAT/LSAT coaching meta-analyses, and
   AAMC's own published reliability data.
3. **Some of our assumptions died.** Say this out loud. We dropped the "higher score" claim
   because coached gains don't predict the criterion as well as natural ones. We stopped
   calling activity variety "interleaving" because there's no literature for it. We found our
   own prerequisite graph too sparse to sequence on.
4. **What survived is narrow, defensible, and measurable.** Same score, fewer hours — and a
   mechanism gap nobody in the market is filling.

The credibility comes from beat 3. A room full of people who've been pitched by test-prep
companies has never heard a founder volunteer what didn't work.

## Open with the student's problem, in their own numbers

From `02-voice-of-the-customer.md`. AAMC's Post-MCAT Questionnaire is the source for the survey
figures — tier **A**, roughly 185,000 cumulative responses across seven measured years
(2014–2018, 2020, 2021).

| What students report | Share |
|---|---|
| "Difficulty staying confident that I could do well" — the highest-scoring item in the whole survey | **67.6%** |
| Can't get through the volume of material | 60–62% |
| **"Difficulty relearning material I learned a while ago"** — stable across all seven years | **49–53%** |
| Job conflict with study time | 51–56% |
| School conflict · family conflict | ~50% each |
| Difficulty affording prep courses and materials | 37–43% (AAMC); independently 42.5% of 81,755 examinees, *JAMA Health Forum* 2023 |

Also usable: roughly **one in five people who sit the MCAT never submit an application**, and
about **38% sit it more than once**. Do **not** claim a void or no-show rate — AAMC publishes
none.

**The forgetting number is the one to lead with.** Half of all examinees, stable across seven
years and ~185,000 responses, report difficulty relearning material they already studied. That
is a scheduling failure with a known fix, and it is the single best-powered student complaint in
existence.

### The self-diagnoses that are wrong

This is the part that makes a demo land, because it shows you know the customer better than they
know themselves.

- **"I need to read faster for CARS."** Wrong twice. Rayner et al. (2016) document a hard
  speed–comprehension trade-off with no high-speed high-comprehension mode, and reading is only
  about **14–28% of the CARS time budget** anyway *[own calculation]* — so rate is the wrong
  lever even if it could be trained.
- **"When I'm down to two, I should trust my first instinct."** Inverted. Kruger, Wirtz & Miller
  (2005): **51% of real answer changes went wrong→right against 25% right→wrong**, while
  students predicted the opposite. A widely-held belief that costs points.
- **"I have a bad memory."** Actually an unspaced schedule plus stopping each topic early on a
  confidence signal that is itself unreliable.
- **"I'm plateauing."** Real, but expectations are mis-set: AAMC's own retest data show a median
  gain of only a few points across an entire second attempt.
- **"Prep is too expensive."** Simply correct. Do not correct it — concede it and price against it.

### Why this is the strongest thing in the research

**Three independent lanes converged on one gap.** The competitor mechanism map concluded that
external calibration is the only unoccupied mechanism in the market. The CARS research found
metacomprehension accuracy at .178 — 54.5% discrimination against a 50% coin flip. The student
survey data show the top complaint is *lost confidence* and the best-powered one is *forgetting
what they studied*. All three describe the same failure: **students cannot tell what they
actually know, so every downstream decision runs on a bad signal.**

That is the demo's opening line. Sell measurement, not more content.

## The differentiation argument, in its strongest form

Not "we learn better." Anki has the same two best-evidenced mechanisms — retrieval practice
and spacing — for free, and you will not beat it on those.

**The market splits three ways and nobody occupies the middle:**

| Segment | Products | Has | Structurally lacks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Retrieval without congruent format | Anki + community decks ($0) | Retrieval, spacing, topic interleaving | Format congruence with passage-based items, elaborated feedback, external grading |
| Transfer without scheduling | UWorld ($339), AAMC official | Real passage items, elaborated explanations | Algorithmic spacing, mastery measurement, teaching |
| Coverage without either | Kaplan, Princeton Review, Blueprint | Structure, accountability, instruction | Spacing (a syllabus is one pass), retrieval density, adaptivity |

The unoccupied middle is **spaced, externally-measured practice on format-congruent items.**
Every mechanism in that sentence is A-tier evidence.

**The sharpest single mechanism claim — calibration against an external standard.** Anki has
learners grade their own recall. Self-graded recall awards credit to 43% of outright-wrong
answers *with the correct answer visible*, and 83% without; Anki's own manual normalises
pressing "Good" 80–95% of the time. Overconfidence causes premature termination of study
(Dunlosky & Rawson). Question banks fix the scoring but collect no confidence judgment and
report no calibration error — we could not find this feature on any competitor's product page.

That is a real, mechanism-level, evidence-backed gap. Lead with it.

**Why the transfer framing matters.** Pan & Rickard (2018), 192 effect sizes, N = 10,382:
transfer of retrieval practice is governed by response congruency and elaborated feedback.
Both present → d = 0.78. Neither → 0.21. Publication-bias corrected with no moderator present
→ d = 0.015, i.e. nothing. Anki's cloze cards sit in the worst cell on both. Our practice loop
— real passage-style items, plus explanation *and* refutation of the chosen distractor — sits
in the best one. That is a defensible design claim, not marketing.

## Numbers worth having on the tip of your tongue

| Claim | Number | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Retrieval practice, classroom studies | g = 0.499, 222 studies, 48,478 students; grows with duration | Yang et al. 2021 |
| Spacing at long retention intervals | d = 1.1; optimal gap beats zero gap by 64% | Cepeda et al. 2008, n = 1,354 adults |
| Transfer, both moderators present vs neither | d = 0.78 vs 0.21 | Pan & Rickard 2018 |
| MCAT measurement error | SEM ±2 total, ±1 per section (68% band) | AAMC |
| Reliable individual change | 6 total points, 3 per section (95%) | *[own calculation from SED = √2·SEM]* |
| No-intervention retest gain | +2 to +4 median (initial 472–517) | AAMC, 3 cohorts of ~45,000 |
| Stacked adaptive systems in large RCTs | g = 0.05 (ALEKS) to 0.20 (Cognitive Tutor) | Two meta-analyses; 18,700-student RCT |
| Self-graded recall error | credits 43% of wrong answers with key visible | metacomprehension literature |

The most useful of these in a hostile room is the **6-point reliable-change threshold against
a +2 to +4 no-intervention baseline.** It reframes the entire category: nobody in this market
can demonstrate an individual score gain, including the incumbents, because the instrument
cannot resolve one. Saying that first makes you the most honest person in the room and
pre-empts the question you cannot win.

## The commercial frame

From `01-market-and-evidence-bar.md`. Numbers verified July 2026; sources and tiers in that file.

| Fact | Figure |
|---|---|
| MCAT exams per year | ~101,831 (305,494 across 2023–2025 testing years) *[own calculation]* |
| Applicants, 2025 | 54,699, **up 5.3%** — reversing three straight declines; 76.5% first-time |
| Retester share | ~38% of examinees |
| Price spread | **$0** (Khan Academy, Anki, AAMC free products) to **$7,349** (Princeton Review 515+ Immersion) |
| Reference prices | AAMC official bundle $323.70 · UWorld QBank $339 · Jack Westin $29.99/mo |
| US TAM | ~$100–200M/year *[own calculation]* — no credible third-party figure exists |
| Cross-check | Kaplan's Supplemental Education segment: $317.2M in 2025, up 9% (SEC filing) — and that covers *all* its US test prep plus licensure and corporate training |

**The pricing wedge is the cleanest commercial observation available.** Almost nothing credible
sits between **$324 and $1,199** except UWorld at $339 and Jack Westin at $29.99/month. The
market is barbelled into free-or-cheap tools and four-figure courses, with no serious
mid-market product. That is a position to occupy, and it is a factual observation rather than a
projection.

Do **not** quote a market-size figure from the web. The circulating "$1.2B LSAT+MCAT prep
market" traces to an SEO content mill and fails a sanity check. Use the arithmetic and show it.

### The evidence certification you can actually earn before launch

This is the most actionable finding in the bundle. **ESSA Tier 4 / Digital Promise
"Research-Based Design" is achievable pre-launch**: it requires a logic model, roughly five
supporting citations, an annotated bibliography, and one qualitative study. You have vastly
more than five citations already; the research bundles in this repo are most of the
application.

For contrast, ESSA Tier 1 needs **≥350 students across ≥2 sites** — out of reach now. And
**only 15.3% of 2,135 ISTE-indexed edtech products hold any evidence certification at all.**

So there is a real, named, third-party-verifiable credential that you can hold and that 85% of
the market does not. That is a far better answer to "what's your evidence?" than a promise of
future studies.

### One number to reconcile before you present

Three lanes reported the AAMC retest baseline and two independently landed on a median gain of
**+2 to +4 points** (for initial scores 472–517); the third reported AAMC stating **1–3 points**.
The difference is probably band- or year-specific. Two of three agree, so **+2 to +4 is the safer
figure** — but read the AAMC source directly and quote it exactly before presenting. This number
is load-bearing for the honesty argument and is exactly the detail a hostile questioner checks.

## Hard questions, and honest answers

**"What's your evidence it works?"**
None yet on outcomes — and neither does anyone else in this market, which is the more
interesting fact. Three things we do have. A method built only from mechanisms with strong
independent evidence. A pre-registered plan to measure time-to-criterion per concept, which is
measurable at small n, where score-gain studies need 400–1,500 learners per arm plus
near-complete score reporting. And a concrete near-term credential: ESSA Tier 4 /
Digital Promise Research-Based Design, which needs a logic model and about five citations — we
have far more than five — and which only 15.3% of indexed edtech products hold in any form.

**"How are you different from Anki, which is free?"**
Anki is our real competitor, not Kaplan. It has the two best mechanisms and we won't beat it
there. It cannot do three things by construction: it doesn't know what you should study, it
practises a format that isn't the exam's, and it lets you grade yourself — which learners do
generously about 43–83% of the time on wrong answers.

**"UWorld already has an adaptive planner. Isn't adaptivity commoditised?"**
Yes, and we don't lead with it. Adaptive scheduling is table stakes now. Our claim is about
what the scheduling is *for*: eliminating hours spent re-covering known material, measured.

**"How many points will users gain?"**
We won't say, and I'd be suspicious of anyone who does. The NAD ordered both Blueprint and
Princeton Review to stop quantifying exactly that in April 2024. What we will claim, and can
measure, is hours saved at equal coverage.

**"How much of the app is built?"**
Be direct; the audit numbers are defensible and evasion is worse. The engine is built and
tested: a 140-item multistage diagnostic validated in simulation, BKT mastery with decay,
FSRS-6 spaced repetition, a time-budgeted planner, ~1,550 passing tests. The content is ~13%
authored — 15 playable lessons of 114 — and that is the current constraint, with a teammate on
lessons and another on question generation. Framing that works: *the hard part, the engine, is
done and the part that scales with headcount is in progress.*

**"You have no users, so how do you know any of this?"**
We don't, and the plan is built around that. There's a documented instrumentation
plan — propensity-logged decisions, tolerance-band sampling — that has to be in place before
the first cohort, because off-policy evaluation is mathematically impossible on logs from a
deterministic scheduler, and that specific mistake cannot be repaired later.

**"How do you calibrate items with no users?"** (the sharpest technical question)
We can't yet, and we know exactly what it costs: every item's difficulty is currently a
DOK-derived cold-start estimate, we have zero response data, and all 2,108 items are singleton
families so there's no parameter-inheritance path either. The fix is ordered — real item
families during the current relabelling pass, then ~100 responses per item for rank-ordering.
Until then we do not show precision we haven't earned.

**"What stops Kaplan copying this?"**
Nothing technical. What's hard to copy is a business willing to say "fewer hours" instead of
"+15 points," because the incumbents' entire funnel is built on the point claim they were just
told to stop making.

## Demo path, and what to avoid

**Demo locally**, not against the deployed environment. The deployed build is missing the
`todaysPathActivity` route, so activity completion 404s and Practice never unlocks. Local
runs the full 2,108-item corpus.

Server is up at `http://localhost:5190/app`. Restart fresh before the demo and clear the Vite
cache — stale servers have twice made screens look broken.

**Show, in this order:**
1. **Diagnostic** — the strongest artefact. A 140-item multistage form, block navigation and
   answer revision, and a two-priority report with honest framing rather than fake precision.
2. **Practice with a miss** — this is where the origin insight becomes visible. The refutation
   of the chosen distractor plus the explanation of the key, immediately. Tie it to Pan &
   Rickard: both moderators present.
3. **Mistakes tab** — misconception patterns grouped at knowledge-component grain, not a flat
   list of every wrong answer.
4. **Today's Path** — a time-budgeted day.

**Avoid or pre-frame:**
- **Curriculum** — 112 of 114 entries are disabled "Coming soon." If asked, answer with the
  content plan rather than scrolling.
- **The Home brain** — currently a decorative fully-mastered visual, not real state. Don't
  present it as progress.
- **The vector lesson** — carries an `INTERNAL_DRAFT_ONLY` badge on screen.
- **Progress rings** — they won't move from lesson completions, because scheduled lessons
  write no mastery evidence yet. Practice does move them.
- **CARS Duel** — needs `AI_API_KEY` or it shows "your sparring partner is offline." It is
  also a debate, not passage practice; the name will mislead an audience that expects
  passages. Either explain it precisely or skip it.

# MCAT Prep: Market, Pricing, and the Evidence Bar

**Purpose:** commercial and market context for a founder pitch. Assumes learning-science and psychometric evidence is already in hand.
**Compiled:** July 30, 2026. All prices verified on or before this date; test-prep pricing changes with promotions and should be re-checked the morning of the pitch.
**Tiering:** **A** strong/authoritative (AAMC, SEC filings, FTC, BBB National Programs, vendor's own live pricing page) · **B** reasonable with limits (peer-reviewed abstract only, reproduced official PDF, trade press) · **C** weak/single-source/marketing (vendor PR, review-affiliate sites, SEO market-research mills).

---

## Recommendation

Three facts worth saying out loud. First, this is a **narrow, deep market, not a big one**: roughly 102,000 MCAT exams a year (**A**, AAMC), each attached to a buyer who will spend hundreds to low thousands. That supports a real business around $100–200M US TAM *[own calculation]* — but not a billion-dollar story, and the $1.2B figure circulating online is from a low-quality SEO mill (**C**). Quote the exam count, not the market-size report. Second, **incumbent pricing is absurdly stratified** — $0 (Khan Academy) to $7,349 (Princeton Review Immersion, **A**, verified live) — with almost nothing credible between $324 and $1,199. That gap is the wedge.

The dangerous one: **do not put a number on a score gain.** In April 2024 the National Advertising Division ordered both Blueprint and Princeton Review to drop quantified MCAT point claims (**A**). A pre-launch product has zero substantiation. One "+10 points" slide converts a learning-science pitch into an advertising-law liability.

## Findings

### 1. Market size and volume

**Exam volume is the reliable anchor.** The AAMC does not publish a clean annual examinee count in an easily citable place, but it publishes the sample size behind its percentile tables, which is a three-year total. The percentile ranks in effect May 1, 2026 – April 30, 2027 are based on **N = 305,494** exams across the 2023, 2024, and 2025 testing years (**A**, AAMC PDF, read in full). That is **~101,831 exams per year** *[own calculation: 305,494 ÷ 3]*.

The prior window (2022–2024) was 293,882 exams, or ~97,961/year (**C** for the number itself — I found it only on a review-affiliate site, though it is consistent with AAMC's methodology). Taken together the trend is **modestly upward**: roughly 98,000/year rising to roughly 102,000/year *[own calculation]*. Treat this as directional; AAMC's percentile denominators are the sample, and I could not open the underlying testing-year reports.

**Applicant volume rebounded sharply in 2025 after three years of decline** (**A**, AAMC press release and news item, Oct 2025):

- **54,699** people applied to US MD-granting medical schools in 2025, **up 5.3%** from 2024 — the largest gain other than the 2021-22 COVID spike since 2015-16.
- **First-time applicants were 76.5%** of the pool and rose **8.4%**; re-applicants **fell 3.6%**.
- **23,440 matriculants** (largest class ever); total enrollment passed 100,000 for the first time at **100,723**.
- Prior-year context: the January 2025 data showed applications at their lowest since 2017-18, a third consecutive decline. So the 2025 rebound is off a low base — worth saying honestly rather than presenting 2025 as a secular growth trend.

**Repeat-tester share is high and is the commercially important segment.** AAMC's *Using MCAT Data in 2023 Medical Student Selection* reports **~38%** of individuals who took the MCAT in 2019–2021 were retesters; the 2022 edition reports the same ~38% for 2018–2020 (**B** — I read these as reproductions of AAMC PDFs on a third-party document host, not on aamc.org; the figure is stable across two editions, which raises confidence). AAMC also reports the **median gain on retest is 1–3 points** (**A**, AAMC advisor deck, 2024) — elsewhere given as 2–4 points for first attempts in the 472–517 band and 0–1 points for 518+ (**B**).

Note the asymmetry: ~38% of examinees are retesters, and retesters buy prep twice. They are the highest-intent, highest-willingness-to-pay, most-underserved segment, and the honest AAMC number (median +1 to +3) is *exactly* why nobody can substantiate a +10 claim.

**Total addressable market: no credible third-party figure exists.** I looked. What surfaces are SEO-generated "market research" pages — researchintelo ("Global LSAT and MCAT Prep Platforms market $1.2B in 2024, $3.8B by 2033"), marketintelo ("medical board exam prep $4.8B"), dataintelo ("test preparation $27.4B"), zipdo, wifitalents. These are **Tier C and should not be cited in a pitch.** They share templated prose, invented segment splits, and cite no methodology. The researchintelo $1.2B figure fails a basic sanity check: at ~102,000 MCAT exams plus ~130–140k LSAT tests annually, $1.2B implies roughly $4,000–5,000 of platform spend per test taker, which is above the list price of most full courses *[own calculation]*.

**Scope it down yourself instead.** Arithmetic on AAMC volume and observed prices *[own calculation throughout]*:

| Blended spend per exam occasion | Implied annual US MCAT-prep revenue |
| --- | --- |
| $324 (AAMC official bundle only) | ~$33M |
| $500 | ~$51M |
| $1,000 | ~$102M |
| $1,500 | ~$153M |
| $2,000 | ~$204M |

Basis: 101,831 exams/year. A defensible headline is **"a roughly $100–200M/year US MCAT-prep market."**

**Cross-check against the only public financials in the space** (**A**, Graham Holdings 2025 Q4 earnings release / SEC 8-K exhibit, filed 2026): Kaplan's **Supplemental Education** segment did **$317.2M** revenue in 2025, **up 9%** from $291.6M in 2024. But that segment is *all* of Kaplan's US test prep (SAT/ACT/LSAT/GMAT/GRE/MCAT/nursing/bar), plus professional licensure, certification, corporate training, continuing education, and career advising. MCAT is a modest slice of one segment of the largest incumbent. If MCAT were ~10% of Kaplan SE (~$32M) and Kaplan held ~20–25% of paid MCAT prep, total paid market is ~$130–160M *[own calculation, assumptions unverified]* — consistent with the range above and an order of magnitude below $1.2B.

**Typical spend per student: I could not find an authoritative figure.** Every "$2,000–$3,500 for MCAT prep" or "$2,500–$3,500 per cycle" number I found traces to advisory blogs or the same SEO mills (**C**). AAMC runs the **Post-MCAT Questionnaire** and publishes annual summary reports (2020–2025) that would likely contain resource-use data (**A** as a source, unread — Excel downloads I did not open). If the founder wants a spend-per-student number, that is the place to get it. **Do not quote $2,500 as if AAMC said it.**

**The spread is $0 to $7,349 — a 1:∞ ratio — and the middle is thin.** Between AAMC's $323.70 official bundle and the ~$1,199 entry price for a real course, the only substantial offerings are UWorld QBank ($339–$439) and Jack Westin at $29.99/month. Jack Westin's own marketing makes the argument for the founder: 3–6 months of JW+ costs **$90–$180** against **$1,100–$1,450** for a comparable self-paced course (**C** — vendor's own framing, but the underlying price points check out against **A**-tier vendor pages).

**Business models, and which are moving:**

- **One-time / access-window purchase** remains the dominant incumbent model, but it is quietly becoming a subscription: Kaplan, Blueprint, and UWorld all now sell *tiered time windows* (90/180/360 days; 3/6/12 months) rather than one price. That is subscription economics in one-time clothing.
- **True monthly subscription** is the visible growth vector at the low end — Jack Westin $29.99/mo, plus a wave of AI entrants at ~$20–40/mo. Blueprint also advertises financing "from $99/month," and Kaplan offers 0% APR Affirm at **$88–$112/month**, which tells you incumbents know the sticker price is a conversion barrier.
- **Course-with-guarantee** is being *doubled down on*, not retreated from. Blueprint's June 2025 relaunch made a **515+ minimum score guarantee standard** on all live instruction, replacing a non-guaranteed Live Course (**B**, Blueprint PR via PR Newswire, June 11, 2025).
- **Tutoring / high-contact mentoring** is where the money is at the top — Altius's entire model is 15–45 private sessions plus 120–200+ group hours, priced $2,399–$8,999.
- **Freemium funnels** are now table stakes: Blueprint 14-day free trial no credit card, UWorld 100 questions / 7 days, Jack Westin 7 days.

**AI-branded entrants, last ~2 years.** This is a thin and unimpressive field, which is good news for the founder:

- **Studyverse / MyMCAT.ai** — gamified AI tutor ("an AI cat named Kalypso"). June 2025 press release announced a pivot to B2B ("Palantir for tutoring firms, Duolingo for students") and claimed **11.2-point average score improvement among retakers, "more than triple the national benchmark"** (**C** — company press release; no study, no methodology, no sample size). A competitor's marketing page now states flatly that "Studyverse is gone" (**C**, single hostile source — treat as unconfirmed, but the pivot-then-disappear pattern is itself the signal). **No funding disclosed that I could find.**
- **Milo AI (askmilo.io)** — adaptive planner + AI tutor, ~$20+/mo, positioned explicitly against "$200/hour tutors" and "$2,500 courses," and deliberately scoped as a layer *on top of* UWorld/Anki/AAMC/Khan rather than a content replacement (**C**, vendor site). **No funding disclosed.**
- **Jenova.ai** — general AI agent platform with an MCAT tutor landing page; free to start, subscription for heavy use (**C**, vendor site).
- **King of the Curve** — gamified adaptive QBank (**C**, referenced only in aggregate search synthesis; I did not read the vendor site).
- Incumbents are also AI-branding: Blueprint advertises an "AI-powered Qbank," and Princeton Review's live page markets an "AI-Powered Study Recommendation Tool... trained on the study habits of successful Princeton Review students" (**A** — read on TPR's page).

**I found no disclosed venture funding for any MCAT-specific AI entrant.** That is a genuine finding: the category has no well-capitalised AI challenger. It also means no comparable valuation to anchor against.

**Consolidation.** The MCAT prep market is already consolidated and PE-owned:

- **Blueprint** — New Harbor Capital took majority control April 2018, simultaneously buying Blueprint (LSAT) and **Next Step Test Preparation** (the MCAT business) and merging them into Blueprint Education Holdings (**A**, New Harbor's own releases). Blueprint has since made **five** acquisitions: Cram Fighter, Med School Tutors, Rosh Review (2021), and Sarah Michelle NP Reviews. Its strategy is to own the whole pre-med → med-student → resident → NP/PA licensure funnel.
- **The Princeton Review** — passed through Charlesbank (2012, **$33M**), Tutor.com/IAC (2014), ST Unitas (2017), and since **January 2022** is owned by **Primavera Capital Group**, a Hong Kong-based Chinese PE firm; CFIUS approval was reported ~mid-2023 (**B** — Wikipedia + The Wire China + The College Fix reporting on a WSJ story; I did not read the WSJ original).
- **Kaplan** — Graham Holdings (NYSE: GHC), public financials available (**A**).
- **Notable failure/attrition:** Graham Holdings' own 2025 annual report says of Kaplan's historic competitors that "most of our competitors from [that era] are gone or are shells of their [former selves]" (**A**, but it is the incumbent's self-serving framing). The only recent candidate failure I found is Studyverse, unconfirmed (**C**).

**Regulatory and advertising-standards activity — the most important part of this section.**

The two April 2024 NAD decisions are real and I read both press releases in full (**A**, BBB National Programs):

- **April 18, 2024 — The Princeton Review** (Fast-Track SWIFT, brought by Blueprint). NAD recommended TPR discontinue *"Score a 515+ on the MCAT or add 15 points depending on your starting score. Guaranteed or your money back."* Crucially, NAD held the money-back guarantee was clearly disclosed **but still conveyed an affirmative performance claim** that a substantial portion of customers would hit those numbers — and there was no evidence in the record that they would. NAD quoted a 20-year-old FTC FAQ: *"Offering a money-back guarantee is not a substitute for substantiation."* TPR said it would comply while disagreeing that the guarantee conveys a performance message.
- **April 22, 2024 — Blueprint** (brought by TPR). NAD recommended Blueprint discontinue claims that students raise scores **by 15 or 13 points on average**, and claims that 41% raise by +15 or more / 69% by +10 or more. NAD's narrower point matters: increase claims measured against a *diagnostic or practice* test may be permissible **if qualified as such**, but framing them against "official MCAT scores" was not supportable.

**Further activity since then: I found none specific to test prep.** I searched NAD decisions and monthly case reports and found no test-prep case after April 2024; the February 2026 NAD case report I sampled covers skincare and personal finance. NAD does continue to challenge quantified performance claims and survey-based claims routinely as part of its monitoring program (**A**), so the doctrine is alive even though test prep has been quiet. **Absence of a newer decision is not evidence of relaxation — and I cannot rule out cases I did not surface.**

**Does anyone still advertise point guarantees? Yes — conspicuously.** This is the single most useful competitive-intelligence item here:

- **The Princeton Review still advertises the substance of the claim NAD told it to drop.** On the live MCAT 515+ Immersion page I fetched July 2026, the product is named "515+," and the page carries a "515+ Money-Back Score Guarantee" with an explicit table: *starting score under 500 → at least a 15 point increase; 500 or higher → score of at least 515*, repeated verbatim in the FAQ. It has been restructured from a headline slogan into a named guarantee with a qualifying-conditions table (attend all classes, complete 6 practice tests, etc.), but the quantified numbers are still front-and-centre. **A**, read in full.
- **Blueprint went the same direction, harder.** Its June 2025 relaunch made a **515 minimum score guarantee** standard across live instruction and renamed the product the "515+ Course" (**B**, Blueprint PR).
- **Altius**, not a party to either NAD case, advertises a **"Guaranteed 90th-Percentile MCAT Score OR a Minimum 12-point Score Increase,"** a **10-point improvement guarantee** on Summer Immersion, **double-your-money-back** on its Elite classroom track, and an **"average 516.4 MCAT score"** across 20+ years (**C** — review-affiliate tables and Altius's own promo page; I did not fetch Altius's main site).
- **Kaplan** advertises a "Higher Score Guarantee" plus **515+/15+** on select programs (**B**).

The pattern: after NAD, the industry migrated from *"our students gain +15 points on average"* (an average-outcome claim, hard to substantiate) to *"hit 515 or your money back, subject to conditions"* (a conditional refund offer attached to a threshold). NAD's TPR decision says that restructuring **does not** cure the substantiation problem. So the whole top of this market is standing on ground NAD has already declared unsound — which is an opportunity for a competitor that makes an honest claim, and a trap for one that imitates them.

### 3. What the market is not serving

**Cost is a documented, quantified barrier, and AAMC itself acts as if it is.** The AAMC **Fee Assistance Program** exists precisely because of it and is explicit about what it is solving: it "assists individuals with extreme financial limitations who otherwise could not take the exam." Benefits (**A**, AAMC, 2026 cycle):

- MCAT registration cut from **$355 to $145**.
- **Free** MCAT Official Prep Online-Only Bundle (a ~$324–$365 value).
- Free 2-year MSAR subscription; AMCAS fees waived for one cycle up to 20 schools; discounted PREview; 60% ERAS discount.
- AAMC describes the package as **over $2,000 in benefits**.
- Constraints worth knowing: **benefits are not retroactive** and applications for 2026 opened **February 2, 2026**. Students who buy prep before approval get nothing back — a real, concrete pain point.
- Kaplan mirrors this with a **60% discount** through its own fee-assistance program (**B**).

**AAMC has published directly on prep-access disparities, and the findings are more useful than the founder may expect.** Two things to distinguish:

1. **The long-standing finding (2019–2020):** examinees from lower-SES backgrounds and from less-resourced undergraduate institutions **use free and low-cost MCAT prep resources at *lower* rates than their peers** — despite AAMC's widespread dissemination of them (**B**, Academic Medicine 2019, Girotti et al. / *Investigating Group Differences in Examinees' Preparation for and Performance on the New MCAT Exam*; and the March 2020 Academic Medicine supplement, which I read at length). The 2020 supplement also establishes that score gaps are **not** a technical artefact of the exam: students admitted with the same MCAT score succeed similarly in medical school regardless of background, so there is no predictive bias. The gap is an opportunity gap, not a measurement gap.

2. **The newer and sharper finding (2025):** *Examining Differences in the Preparation and Performance of US MCAT Examinees from Lower-SES Backgrounds*, **Teaching and Learning in Medicine**, 2025, doi:10.1080/10401334.2025.2492620. First large-scale review of MCAT prep strategies among lower-SES examinees: **3,240 lower-SES respondents** to AAMC's Post-MCAT Questionnaire, 2021–2023 examinees. Higher-scoring lower-SES examinees reported greater use of evidence-supported strategies — discussing strategy with advisors/peers, establishing a baseline, practising applying knowledge to questions, taking a practice test to evaluate readiness — and, critically, **"utilization rates of high-value, free/low-cost MCAT resources were significantly higher among top scorers."** (**B** — **I read the abstract only; the full text is paywalled and Cloudflare blocked my fetch.**)

That 2025 paper is the strongest single piece of commercial ammunition in this brief, and it is being widely under-used. It says the binding constraint for under-resourced students is **not access to expensive content** but **knowing which free resources to use and how to study**. A product that is cheap and *structures the strategy* is aimed at a documented gap, published by the test's own owner. It also reframes a free tier from a loss-leader into the core value proposition.

**A related finding on the demand side** (**B**, *BMC Medical Education*, analysing all 2017–2019 MCAT examinees): among socioeconomically disadvantaged examinees, only **51.8%** scored in the middle or top tercile versus **73.1%** of non-disadvantaged. And **over 20% of middle-tercile performers never applied to medical school at all** — 78–80% application rate for middle-tercile vs 92–94% for top-tercile, with the pattern similar across SES once score is controlled. The authors conclude disadvantaged examinees "may under-appreciate their academic achievements." There is a confidence-and-guidance market here that is separate from the score-improvement market.

**Free / low-cost options and their reach — and a correction to the brief's premise.**

**Khan Academy's MCAT collection has *not* been retired.** The timeline (**A** unless noted):

- July 15, 2020 — Khan Academy retired a batch of "older and partner courses" as part of a K-14 refocus. MCAT was slated to go.
- Late 2021 — after user backlash, Khan Academy announced it would **retain the free MCAT course "until 2026" with the support of the AAMC** (**A**, Khan Academy Help Center — still live, still worded that way).
- **July 2026 (now)** — the collection is **still available and still described on AAMC's own free-resources page**: "The Khan Academy MCAT Collection contains sample content from all four sections... includes **1,100 videos and 3,000 review questions**... created by Khan Academy with support and funding from the **AAMC and the Robert Wood Johnson Foundation**." AAMC's free "What's on the MCAT Exam?" content-outline course also links out to Khan Academy lessons and now bundles **120 free practice questions** from the retired Official Guide.
- A third-party review site with a February 12, 2026 fact-check note says Khan Academy "has announced continued MCAT support with AAMC" and content is still being updated (**C**).

**So the correct framing is not "the free tier was retired, therefore a free tier is defensible." It is the opposite and more urgent:** the largest free MCAT resource is running on a commitment whose stated end-date is **this year**, is funded by third parties (AAMC + RWJF) rather than by a sustainable model, and has already been slated for retirement once. Its content is also structurally incomplete — **no full-length practice exams**, content review only. Two consequences for the pitch: (a) a well-built free tier has a credible path to inheriting Khan Academy's role if the AAMC arrangement lapses, and (b) Khan Academy's history is the cautionary case that **free content without a business model gets switched off** — which is an argument *for* a paid tier subsidising a free one, not against.

Also note the free tier already has a *strong* incumbent that is not going anywhere: **AAMC's own free products** (Unscored Sample Test, Practice Exam 1, content-outline course with 120 free questions) and **Anki**, which is free, open-source, and community-supplied. Anki is the real competitor at $0 — it is spaced repetition, which is exactly the mechanism a learning-science pitch will lean on. Be ready for "why not Anki plus Khan Academy plus the AAMC bundle, for $324?" The honest answer is the 2025 TLM finding: because knowing *what to do with them* is the actual gap.

### 4. The evidence bar for an early-stage education product

**What sophisticated buyers formally ask for: the ESSA tiers.** Codified in ESEA §8101(21)(A) and used across US Dept. of Education discretionary grants (**A**, ED non-regulatory guidance; IES/WWC):

| Tier | Requirement |
| --- | --- |
| **1 — Strong** | ≥1 well-implemented **RCT** meeting WWC standards *without* reservations; low attrition; statistically significant positive effect; **≥350 students**, **≥2 sites** |
| **2 — Moderate** | ≥1 **quasi-experimental** study meeting WWC standards *with* reservations (or RCT with high attrition), baseline equivalence met; significant positive effect; ≥350 students, ≥2 sites |
| **3 — Promising** | ≥1 **correlational** study with **statistical controls for selection bias** |
| **4 — Demonstrates a rationale** | A **well-specified logic model** with an outcome aligned to a stakeholder need, informed by existing research, **plus an evaluation already planned or under way** |

**What a pre-launch product can actually certify.** Digital Promise operates two product certifications mapped to these tiers (**A**, Digital Promise). Both require exactly five artefacts:

1. A logic model (problem statement, inputs, activities, outputs, short- and long-term outcomes) **supported by ≥5 empirical citations**, at least one published in the last five years.
2. An **annotated bibliography** tying rigorous research to **five significant and distinct design decisions**.
3. **One well-designed study** — **qualitative** for *Research-Based Design (ESSA Tier 4)*; **quantitative** (correlational, quasi-experimental, or randomised) for *Evidence-Based Edtech (ESSA Tier 3)*.
4. Public access to the product's research basis.
5. A signed FERPA letter.

Certification lasts two years. Digital Promise states explicitly that certification **"is not an endorsement of products or their efficacy"** — it narrows a buyer's options for piloting. Quote that caveat before an investor does.

**The gap, concretely.** A pre-launch product with deep learning-science grounding and no users can meet **Tier 4 / Research-Based Design today**: it already has the literature, it needs a written logic model, an annotated bibliography of five design decisions, and one qualitative study of learner experience. It **cannot** meet **Tier 1 or 2** at any price, because those require ≥350 students across ≥2 sites with a significant positive effect — which is 6–18 months of operation minimum, and for an MCAT product means waiting on real score outcomes. **Tier 3** is the first genuinely gated rung: it needs a real cohort and a comparison group with controls for selection bias, i.e. post-launch.

**How rare this actually is — the most quotable number in this section.** Of the **2,135** products in the ISTE EdTech Index as of April 2026, only **327 (15.3%)** hold **any** evidence-based certification; **85% have no formal evidence of any kind**. Among the ~100 most-used tools in US K-12 (LearnPlatform data) the figure rises to ~40% — evidence exists where the money is. And **fewer than 100** of 2,200+ indexed products hold the Digital Promise Research-Based Design certification (**B** — LXD Research, April 2026; LXD is a partner/author of the ISTE Index *and* sells ESSA Tier 4 certification services, so it has a commercial interest in the gap looking large; the underlying ISTE Index counts are checkable).

**Implication: the bar the founder can clear pre-launch puts them in the top ~15% of the sector, and possibly the top ~5%.** That is a legitimately strong pitch line, and it is defensible because it is a documentation claim, not an efficacy claim.

**Two important caveats to say out loud before an investor says them:**

1. **ESSA is a K-12 federal-funding framework. It does not govern a direct-to-consumer MCAT app.** There is no institutional buyer applying an ESSA rubric to a pre-med purchasing decision. Its value is as **shared vocabulary** — the ladder sophisticated edtech investors and institutional buyers already think in — plus a real option if the founder ever sells to undergraduate pre-health advising offices, post-baccalaureate programmes, or pipeline programmes, where procurement rubrics *do* award points for these certifications. One district CIO is quoted saying certification "can be a tiebreaker with competing products," and California's Ed Tech JPA (purchasing for 2 million students) added Digital Promise certifications to its RFP process (**A**, Digital Promise, April 2024).
2. **ISTE's own buying guide concedes the timing problem** the founder faces: "the edtech landscape changes so quickly that the best solution may not yet have amassed a significant amount of published research to support its claims," which requires buyers "to consider how to review the newest and most promising tools without the benefit of multiyear formal research studies" (**A**, *Better Edtech Buying: A Practical Guide*, Revised Edition, ISTE). Use this. The buyer-side literature explicitly acknowledges that demanding Tier 1 from a new product is unreasonable.

**Cautionary cases — products that pitched on mechanism and were challenged on efficacy.**

- **Lumosity / Lumos Labs, FTC, January 2016 — the canonical case, and uncomfortably close to home.** Lumosity sold subscriptions ($14.95/mo to $299.95 lifetime) for 40 games "purportedly designed to target and train specific areas of the brain," recommending 10–15 minutes several times a week. **It settled FTC deceptive-advertising charges for $2M.** The FTC's language is the warning: *"Lumosity simply did not have the science to back up its ads."* The order bars claims that the product "improves performance in school, at work, or in athletics" absent competent and reliable scientific evidence, and requires future testing be **"randomized, adequately controlled, and blinded to the maximum extent practicable."** The FTC's business-guidance blog post drew the exact distinction a mechanism-led pitch must respect — **"Mind the gap"**: users improving at the *games* does not establish improvement in real-world performance. The FTC named the category explicitly, including *"boosting board scores for high school students."* The complaint also charged Lumosity with failing to disclose that it had **solicited testimonials via a contest** (**A**, FTC press release, complaint, stipulated order, and business blog — all read).
- **The Princeton Review and Blueprint, NAD, April 2024** — see §2. Both pitched quantified outcomes; both were ordered to stop. Blueprint's failure mode is instructive: it *had* data, but the data compared diagnostic/practice scores to official scores while the ad implied official-to-official. **The mechanism was fine; the measurement framing sank it.**
- **Studyverse / MyMCAT.ai, June 2025** — claimed **"11.2-point score improvement among retakers... more than triple the national benchmark, which is 3 points for retakers"** in a press release, with no study, sample size, comparison group, or methodology (**C**). This is precisely the claim structure NAD ordered TPR and Blueprint to abandon fourteen months earlier, made by a company with far less substantiation. The company appears to have pivoted and possibly wound down (unconfirmed, **C**). Use it as the *near-peer* cautionary tale — it is more persuasive than Lumosity because it is same-market, same-year, and same-claim.

**The synthesis for the pitch:** the defensible position is a **mechanism claim plus a process commitment**, never an outcome number. "Built on X, Y, Z with a documented logic model; here is the Tier 3 study we will run in cohort one, and here is the pre-registered outcome" is both honest and — given that 85% of edtech carries no evidence at all — genuinely differentiating.

## DOK 1 — established facts

1. AAMC's percentile ranks effective May 1, 2026 rest on **N = 305,494** exams from the 2023–2025 testing years; mean total score **500.6**, SD 11.2. (**A**)
2. **54,699** people applied to US MD-granting medical schools in 2025, **up 5.3%**, reversing three consecutive years of decline; first-time applicants were **76.5%** of the pool and rose **8.4%**. (**A**)
3. **23,440** matriculants and **100,723** total enrolled students in 2025-26 — the first time enrollment exceeded 100,000. (**A**)
4. AAMC guidance reports **~38%** of MCAT examinees in recent three-year windows were retesters, and the **median gain on retest is 1–3 points** (0–1 points for first attempts of 518+). (**A**/**B**)
5. MCAT registration is **$355**, reduced to **$145** under the Fee Assistance Program, which AAMC values at **over $2,000** total and which includes the **free** Official Prep Online-Only Bundle. Benefits are **not retroactive**. (**A**)
6. The AAMC Online-Only Official Prep Bundle lists at **$323.70** for one year (2,710 questions); Practice Exams 2–6 are **$35** each, Section Banks **$45** each, question packs **$10–15**; the Unscored Sample Test and Practice Exam 1 are **free**. (**A**)
7. Advertised MCAT prep prices run from **$0** (Khan Academy, Anki, AAMC free products) to **$7,349** (The Princeton Review MCAT 515+ Immersion LiveOnline, verified on TPR's live page July 2026). (**A**)
8. Between **$323.70** and roughly **$1,199** the only substantial commercial products are UWorld QBank (**$339–$439**) and Jack Westin+ (**$29.99/month**). (**A**/**B**)
9. On **April 18, 2024** NAD recommended The Princeton Review discontinue "Score a 515+ on the MCAT or add 15 points... Guaranteed or your money back," holding that a clearly disclosed money-back guarantee **still conveys a performance claim requiring substantiation**. On **April 22, 2024** NAD recommended Blueprint discontinue "+15" and "+13 points on average" claims framed against official MCAT scores. (**A**)
10. **Quantified score guarantees are still being advertised in 2026.** TPR's live page states "under 500 → at least a 15 point increase; 500 or higher → score of at least 515." Blueprint made a **515 minimum score guarantee** standard across live instruction in June 2025. Altius advertises "90th percentile or minimum 12-point increase." (**A**/**B**/**C** respectively)

The Princeton Review has been owned by **Primavera Capital Group since January 2022**. Kaplan is a Graham Holdings (NYSE: GHC) business. (**A**/**B**)

It contains **no full-length practice exams**. (**A**)

(**B**, abstract only)

(**A**)

## DOK 2 — insights and implications

1. **Lead with exam volume, never with a market-size report.** ~102,000 exams/year from AAMC's own percentile denominator is unimpeachable and rounds the same way every time. Any figure from researchintelo/dataintelo/marketintelo will be recognised as an SEO mill by any investor who has seen one, and using it will discredit the genuinely strong research in the rest of the deck. If pressed for a TAM, show the arithmetic: 101,831 exams × a blended spend you can defend.

2. **The single most dangerous slide is a quantified score gain.** Both category leaders were ordered to stop making exactly that claim in April 2024, on the record, in reciprocal challenges. A pre-launch product has no substantiation whatsoever. NAD's TPR ruling closes the obvious workaround too: wrapping the number in a money-back guarantee does **not** cure it. And the FTC's Lumosity order shows where the escalation path ends. **Say the mechanism; never say the number.**

3. **Blueprint's failure mode is more instructive than TPR's, and is the one a data-driven founder will walk into.** Blueprint *had* outcome data. NAD's objection was that a diagnostic-or-practice-to-official comparison was being advertised as if it were official-to-official. A product that measures in-app improvement and reports it as "MCAT score gain" makes precisely this error. Any internal metric must be labelled with the instrument it was measured on, in the ad, not the footnote.

4. **The pricing gap between $324 and $1,199 is the commercial opening, and it is real rather than assumed.** Above $1,199 sit Kaplan, Blueprint, Altius and TPR; below $324 sit free resources with no structure. The only occupants of the middle are UWorld's QBank (content, no curriculum) and Jack Westin at $29.99/month. That incumbents advertise financing — Kaplan at $88–$112/month via Affirm, Blueprint "from $99/month" — is direct evidence they know the sticker price is losing them conversions they would rather keep.

5. **AAMC's own 2025 research reframes the free tier from a cost centre into the thesis.** The finding is not "poor students can't afford prep" but "top-scoring lower-SES students used free resources at *higher* rates." The binding constraint is **knowing what to do**, not access to premium content. A cheap, highly structured product that orchestrates free and low-cost resources is therefore aimed at a gap documented by the exam's owner — which is a far stronger claim than a generic affordability pitch, and it is largely unexploited.

6. **Correct the Khan Academy premise before the pitch, because getting it backwards is an unforced error.** The collection is still live and still on AAMC's own resource page. The real story is more useful: the largest free MCAT resource runs on a philanthropic commitment whose stated horizon is **this year**, has already been slated for retirement once, and lacks full-length exams. That is simultaneously a succession opportunity and the cleanest available argument that free content needs a paid tier underneath it to survive.

7. **The retaker segment is the most commercially attractive and the most honest to serve.** ~38% of examinees, buying prep for the second time, having already learned that their first approach failed, with AAMC telling them to expect a median **+1 to +3**. They are the buyers most likely to pay for diagnosis of *what went wrong* rather than more content. They are also the segment where an honest expectation ("median gain is small; here is how we change your process") is a differentiator rather than a weakness — every competitor is promising them +15.

8. **Kaplan's $317M Supplemental Education line is the best defence against "the market is too small."** It reframes the question: that figure is *all* of the largest incumbent's US test prep plus licensure, certification and corporate training, and it grew **9%** in 2025. A category where the leader's entire multi-exam segment is ~$317M is not a billion-dollar MCAT market — but it is a market where a focused entrant taking single-digit millions is a real business, and where an honest founder gains credibility by saying so before being asked.

9. **The evidence-bar answer should be a specific tier and a specific plan, not a claim of rigour.** "We meet ESSA Tier 4 today — logic model with N empirical citations, annotated bibliography covering five design decisions, one qualitative study — and here is the Tier 3 correlational design with selection-bias controls we will run on cohort one" is concrete, checkable, and beats 85% of the sector. Attach the Digital Promise caveat yourself: certification is not an efficacy endorsement.

10. **Name the Tier 1 gap out loud rather than letting an investor find it.** Tier 1 needs ≥350 students across ≥2 sites in an RCT; that is post-launch by definition and, for an MCAT product, gated on real score outcomes months later. ISTE's own buying guide concedes new products "may not yet have amassed a significant amount of published research." Volunteering the gap converts the weakest part of the pitch into evidence of unusual rigour.

11. **The absence of a funded AI competitor is a finding worth stating — carefully.** I found no disclosed venture funding for any MCAT-specific AI entrant. The visible field is Milo AI, Jenova, King of the Curve, and Studyverse (pivoted, possibly defunct), plus incumbents bolting "AI-powered" onto existing QBanks. That means no well-capitalised challenger — and also no comparable to anchor a valuation against, and a category where "AI tutor for the MCAT" alone is not differentiation. The differentiation has to be the evidence discipline.

12. **Studyverse is the cautionary tale to use, because it is a near-peer.** An AI MCAT startup claimed **11.2 points, "more than triple the national benchmark"** in a June 2025 press release with no study behind it — fourteen months after NAD ordered the two market leaders to stop making that exact kind of claim. It then pivoted to B2B and appears to have wound down. Lumosity is the famous case; Studyverse is the one that is same-market, same-year, and same-claim, and it lands harder.

13. **This market is consolidated and PE-owned, which cuts both ways.** Blueprint (New Harbor, five acquisitions, building the pre-med → NP/PA licensure funnel) and TPR (Primavera) are owned by financial sponsors who will defend price points and can acquire. A credible product with a differentiated free tier and clean evidence documentation is an acquisition target, which is a legitimate exit narrative — but expect to be asked how you survive a sponsor-funded price response.

14. **Rising applicant volume is a tailwind, but present it honestly.** 2025's +5.3% reversed three straight declines and came off the lowest base since 2017-18. Presenting 2025 as a growth trend invites a correction from anyone who has read the January 2025 AAMC release. "Recovering from a multi-year decline, with first-time applicants up 8.4%" is both accurate and still a good story.

# Voice of the Customer: What MCAT Students Say Is Wrong, and What Actually Is

Research date: July 30, 2026. Web research only; no application code read.

Evidence tiers used inline: **A** authoritative (peer-reviewed, or AAMC primary data), **B** reasonable with stated limits, **C** weak / forum sentiment (directional only). Arithmetic I performed is marked *[own calculation]*.

---

## Recommendation

Open on the calibration gap, not the content mountain. Students do not fail for lack of effort or material — they fail because they cannot tell what they have actually learned, so every downstream decision (what to study next, when to stop, when to sit the exam) is made on a bad signal. This is the rare founder claim that is both emotionally true to students and defensible in the literature: roughly half of MCAT examinees report difficulty relearning material they studied earlier (49.4%, n = 27,257, AAMC 2020, **A**), two-thirds report difficulty staying confident they could do well (67.6%, same sample, **A**), and readers' judgments of their own comprehension correlate with actual comprehension at only about .18–.24 (Yang et al. 2022 meta-analysis, 502 effects, **A**). Sell measurement, not more content.

## Top reported problems

### 1. Forgetting content learned months earlier — strongest hard number in this brief

AAMC Post-MCAT Questionnaire item: "Difficulty relearning material I learned a while ago."

| Year | % reporting | n |
|---|---|---|
| 2014 | 53.5 | 33,064 |
| 2015 | 50.0 | 23,894 |
| 2016 | 49.8 | 27,545 |
| 2017 | 50.4 | 26,902 |
| 2018 | 52.4 | 26,293 |
| 2020 | 49.4 | 27,257 |
| 2021 | 49.8 | 19,628 |

Tier **A**. Remarkably stable at roughly half of all examinees across seven measured years and ~185,000 cumulative responses *[own calculation: sum of the n column]*. The 2019 value is not comparable because the AAMC re-engineered the survey mid-cycle. This is the most durable, best-powered student complaint available.

### 2. Running out of time before test day

The AAMC does not ask "will I run out of time," but it asks three time-conflict questions (2020 / 2021, n = 27,257 / 19,628, tier **A**):

- Difficulty balancing MCAT study and my job: **51.3% / 56.0%**
- Difficulty balancing MCAT study and school: **49.9% / 52.8%**
- Difficulty balancing MCAT study and family obligations: **50.5% / 50.3%**
- Difficulty getting through the amount of material to be learned: **60.0% / 62.1%**
- Difficulty sticking to my study plan: **39.7% / 41.6%**

So over half of examinees hit a hard time conflict, and about 60% feel the volume exceeds their capacity. The student's sense of a time crunch is empirically well-founded.

### 3. Not knowing what to study next

There is no PMQ item that maps cleanly onto this. Nearest proxies (2020 / 2021, tier **A**):

- Difficulty getting concrete and detailed information about the MCAT exam: **22.3% / 21.4%**
- Difficulty sticking to my study plan: **39.7% / 41.6%**
- Difficulty finding a mentor to provide resources, support, guidance, and/or role modeling: **26.3% / 27.9%**

The one study that speaks to prioritization directly is Swan Sein et al. (2025, *Teaching and Learning in Medicine*), analyzing PMQ data from 3,240 lower-SES examinees, 2021–2023. Its verbatim finding: higher-scoring examinees "reported greater use of many evidence-supported effective test preparation and learning strategies, including discussing preparation strategies with advisors/peers, establishing baseline capabilities, practicing applying knowledge to practice questions, and evaluating readiness by taking a practice test." Tier **A** for design and sample size, but I read the abstract only (full text behind Cloudflare/paywall), so I am not claiming anything about lower-scorers beyond that sentence.

Note the mechanism buried in that sentence: what separated high from low scorers was *establishing a baseline and evaluating readiness* — i.e. measurement — not resource volume.

### 4. "I do practice questions but don't improve"

No survey quantifies this. What exists:

- **87.8% / 87.6%** of PMQ respondents (2020 / 2021) answered practice questions as they studied each topic; **89.7% / 89.4%** took a timed practice exam mimicking exam day. Tier **A**. So near-universal practice-question use is the baseline, which means "I do questions" is not a differentiator.
- **67.6% / 67.4%** reported "difficulty staying confident that I could do well on the MCAT exam." Tier **A**. This is the highest-scoring challenge item in the whole survey and is the closest instrumented signal of the plateau feeling.
- AAMC retest data: median second-attempt gain is **2 to 4 points** for examinees whose first score was 472–517, and **0 to 1 point** for 518–528, across three separate annual reports. Tier **A**. So the objective rate of improvement on a full retake cycle is genuinely modest — a student who expects a fast climb is mis-calibrated, not necessarily failing.
- Forum sentiment, tier **C**, directional only: Student Doctor Network hosts recurring threads with titles such as "No MCAT improvement studying since December" and "Advice for a plateauing MCAT score - desperate." The top-voted replies converge on error logs, categorizing *why* each miss happened, and explaining why wrong answers are wrong. That is community-generated consensus, not evidence.

### 5. CARS: "I'm too slow" and "I narrow to two and pick wrong"

No survey data exists for either. Nearest instrumented proxy: **85.0% / 83.7%** of PMQ respondents (2020 / 2021) reported that they "practiced pacing myself so I could get through all the questions in each section without running out of time," and **69.9% / 69.6%** practiced strategies for dealing with challenging questions to finish on time (tier **A** for the figures; **B** as an inference that pacing anxiety is widespread, since practicing pacing is also just standard advice).

Structural facts (AAMC, tier **A**): CARS is 53 questions, all passage-based, in 90 minutes; passages are "typically between 500 and 600 words." The 9-passage count is from prep-company sources rather than the AAMC overview page I read (tier **B**).

*[own calculation]* At 9 passages × 550 words ≈ 4,950 words, and at the 200–400 wpm normal adult range documented by Rayner et al. (2016), pure reading consumes roughly 12–25 minutes of the 90 — about 14–28% of the budget. Even at a slow 200 wpm, ~65 minutes remain for 53 questions (~74 seconds each). Reading rate is not where CARS time goes.

"Narrow to two and pick wrong" is uninstrumented for the MCAT specifically, but it is the exact paradigm of Kruger, Wirtz & Miller (2005) Study 3, which had participants narrow to two answers and mark their first instinct. See the misdiagnosis table.

### 6. Cost of materials

- PMQ, "Difficulty affording preparation courses and materials": **41.3% (2014), 42.2% (2015), 42.7% (2016), 42.5% (2017), 43.4% (2018), 38.7% (2020), 36.9% (2021)**. Tier **A**.
- Independent confirmation at scale: "Racial and Ethnic Differences in Barriers Faced by Medical College Admission Test Examinees," *JAMA Health Forum* (2023), analyzed 81,755 MCAT examinees, 2015–2018; **29,563 (42.5%)** reported difficulty affording preparation materials. Unadjusted by group it ranged from **35.9%** (Asian examinees) to **53.9%** (American Indian or Alaska Native examinees). Tier **A**, full text read.
- Sticker prices: Kaplan and Princeton Review packages **$2,100–$6,900** ("The rising cost of applying to medical school," 2024, tier **B** — a published paper citing vendor list prices, which drift).
- Free-resource use rose from **39.3% (2020) to 48.9% (2022)** per the same paper citing PMQ summary reports (tier **B**; I did not open the 2022 PMQ Excel myself).

This complaint is well-supported and roughly 4 in 10 students hold it. Do not "correct" it.

## The misdiagnosis table

| Complaint | Their diagnosis | Likely real cause per the literature | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| "I don't know what to study next" | "I need a better schedule, a better resource list, or a better plan" | They cannot rank their own topic mastery, so any self-chosen next topic is barely better than chance. Yang, Zhao, Yuan, Luo & Shanks (2022) meta-analyzed 502 effects from 115 studies (15,889 participants): weighted metacomprehension accuracy was **MC = 0.242** overall and **0.178** for non-intervention conditions — the authors compute that MC = .242 corresponds to about **56.1%** discrimination accuracy, "barely better than a coin toss (50%)." Earlier reviews (Maki 1998; Dunlosky & Lipko 2007) independently landed on ≈.27. The fix is external measurement, not a better calendar. | **A** |
| "I'm going to run out of time before test day" | "I need more hours" | **Partly right — do not overcorrect.** Time scarcity is real and instrumented (>50% report job/school/family conflict, **A**). But the hours they have are low-yield: Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan & Willingham (2013) rated rereading, highlighting/underlining and summarization **low utility** while practice testing and distributed practice were the only two rated **high utility**. So the binding constraint is yield per hour at least as much as hour count. | **B** (student partly correct) |
| "I forget everything I learned months ago" | "I have a bad memory; I need to re-review all the content" | Their schedule guarantees the forgetting. Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted & Rohrer (2006) meta-analyzed 839 assessments across 317 experiments: spaced beats massed study, and critically the optimal inter-study interval *grows with the retention interval* — so a 6-month prep window requires deliberately long gaps, not a single content pass. Compounding it, Dunlosky & Rawson (2012) showed overconfidence causes **premature termination of study**, producing lower learning during practice that persists to the delayed test. They stopped studying each topic too early, on a confidence signal that was wrong. | **A** |
| "I do practice questions but don't improve" | "I need more questions, or harder questions" | The tool is right and the loop is wrong. Practice testing is high-utility (Dunlosky et al. 2013, **A**), but only as retrieval-with-feedback; used as pure self-assessment it feeds the same faulty confidence signal (Dunlosky & Rawson 2012). Second, the expectation is mis-set: AAMC's own retest data show a median gain of only 2–4 points across a full second attempt for first scores of 472–517, so "flat over a few weeks" is often normal variation rather than failure. Mechanism is plausible and consistent, but I found **no MCAT-specific causal study** linking review quality to score gain. | **B** |
| CARS: "I'm too slow — I need to read faster" | "I should learn to speed-read or skim" | Wrong on both the method and the diagnosis. Rayner, Schotter, Masson, Potter & Treiman (2016): there is a speed–accuracy trade-off, it is "unlikely that readers will be able to double or triple their reading speeds… while still being able to understand the text as well," educated adults read 200–400 wpm, and the durable route to faster reading is greater language skill and vocabulary. They cite Calef et al. (1999): a speed-reading course raised rate from ~280 to ~400 wpm while comprehension fell from 81% to 74% correct. Separately, *[own calculation]* reading is only ~14–28% of the CARS time budget, so raw rate is the wrong lever; time is going to question deliberation and re-reading. | **A** (reading science); **B** (my time-budget arithmetic) |
| CARS: "I narrow to two and pick wrong — I should trust my first instinct" | "My gut is more reliable than my second thought" | Wrong, and studied in exactly this paradigm. Kruger, Wirtz & Miller (2005), Study 1: of 3,291 changed answers on real exams (n = 1,561 students), **51% were wrong→right, 25% right→wrong, 23% wrong→wrong** — a better-than-2:1 advantage to switching; the same students *predicted* the opposite (42% right→wrong expected vs 33% wrong→right). Replicated on a separate 1,250-exam cohort. A prior review found that in **none of 33 studies** were test takers hurt on average by changing answers. Study 3 had participants narrow to two and mark a first instinct, then found they misremembered sticking as having worked better than it did. Real cause: a regret-driven memory bias, not a reliable gut. | **A** |
| "Prep materials cost too much" | "Materials are unaffordable" | **The student is right; there is no gap to expose here.** 36.9–43.4% report it (PMQ, **A**); 42.5% of 81,755 examinees confirm it (*JAMA Health Forum* 2023, **A**). The only nuance worth stating: Swan Sein et al. (2025) found utilization of high-value free/low-cost resources was *significantly higher* among top scorers from lower-SES backgrounds — so spend is not the score-limiting variable even though the cost pain is genuine. | **A** (complaint); **B** (nuance, abstract only) |

## Quotable material

Two quotes I can source verbatim. I read both articles in full and confirmed the wording against the article text.

**1. On the expectation-versus-score gap** — Ashlynn Sandberg, Baylor senior, quoted in *The Baylor Lariat*, April 16, 2026:

> "When I first started studying for it, you have this huge expectation. I've worked so hard. I expect to be scoring a certain way. Even if you're not scoring that way, you have to have a positive mentality, and that has the biggest impact."

This is the best of the three for a demo: it is a student naming the calibration gap in her own words.

**2. On the cumulative load** — Bhuvana Vejandla, Loyola University Chicago third-year, quoted in *The Loyola Phoenix*, January 29, 2025:

> "The stress of not only doing school but getting into labs, research, presenting, publishing, making connections with professors, recommendations, the MCAT, preparing essays — it really piles up."

Limitation: this is about premedical load generally, with the MCAT as one item in the list, not about MCAT study method. Use it for stakes, not for diagnosis.

**3. On the daily study reality** — Damo Kouakou, Baylor junior, same *Lariat* article, April 16, 2026:

> "The actual time I have to actually do practice problems or even do any type of content review is after 5 p.m. I'll either be at my workplace, or I go to a library and put in at least 3 hours on the subject that I'm on."

This pairs well with the PMQ job-conflict figure (51–56%).

**What I did not include.** I found no verbatim, attributable published quote for "I do practice questions but don't improve," for CARS slowness, or for the narrow-to-two experience. Forum posts expressing all three exist, but I am not reproducing them here — thread titles on Student Doctor Network such as "No MCAT improvement studying since December" and "Advice for a plateauing MCAT score - desperate" are the honest form of that evidence, and they are tier **C** sentiment. I did not manufacture or paraphrase any quote into existence.

## Abandonment data

**Void rate: not published.** The AAMC's only published characterization I could find is in its 2015 new-exam report: "At every test administration, a small percentage of examinees decide to void their results; this report excludes data for these examinees." No number is given. Tier **A** for the statement, and **A** for the conclusion that no rate is public.

**No-show rate: not published.** The AAMC documents the *policy* extensively — a no-show counts as one of your lifetime attempts regardless of reason, the scheduling fee is not refunded, and once you check in the attempt is consumed even if you void or leave early (*MCAT Essentials for Testing Year 2026*, tier **A**) — but publishes no frequency.

**Reschedule/delay rate: not published.** Only the fee schedule is public: $55 (60+ days out), $110 (30–59 days), $210 (10–29 days), with no changes permitted inside 10 days; cancellation refunds $175 or $0 (AAMC, tier **A**). The fee ladder is itself indirect evidence that late rescheduling is common enough to price, but that is an inference, not data.

**What *is* published, and is a better abandonment story:**

- **Retesting.** About **36%** of individuals who took the MCAT in 2017–2019, and about **38%** in both 2018–2020 and 2019–2021, were retesters (AAMC, *Using MCAT Data in Medical Student Selection*, 2021/2022/2023 editions). Tier **A**.
- **Never applying.** Of **81,755** MCAT examinees from 2015–2018, **64,399 (78.8%)** applied to a US MD-granting school and **35,908 (43.9%)** matriculated (*JAMA Health Forum* 2023, tier **A**). *[own calculation]* That means **21.2% of people who sat the MCAT never applied at all**, and **56.1% never matriculated**. This is the cleanest quantified abandonment figure available.
- **Volume context.** Nearly 87,000 examinees took more than 97,000 exams in 2019; more than 88,000 examinees took nearly 100,000 exams in 2020 (AAMC, tier **A**). *[own calculation]* ≈1.12 exams per examinee in 2019. Current AAMC percentile ranks are built on 293,882 exams across 2022–2024, ≈98,000/year *[own calculation]*.

**Framing note for a demo:** do not say "students void their exams at X%." Say the true and stronger thing: about one in five people who sit the MCAT never submit a medical school application, and roughly 38% sit it more than once.

## What I could not verify

- **Void, no-show, and reschedule rates.** Searched AAMC policy pages, *MCAT Essentials 2026*, and the *Using MCAT Data* series. The AAMC confirms voids happen ("a small percentage") but publishes no figure. Treat any specific void percentage you encounter as unsourced.
- **PMQ years 2022–2025.** Those summary reports are published as Excel only. I read the 2018, 2020, and 2021 PDF reports, so every PMQ percentage above is 2014–2021. The 2024 *PMC* paper quotes 39.5% affordability difficulty for 2022 and 48.9% free-resource use, which suggests the pattern holds, but I did not open the source workbooks.
- **Swan Sein et al. (2025) full text.** Abstract only — Cloudflare/paywall blocked the *Taylor & Francis* full text. I therefore quote only its verbatim abstract findings and make no claims about its lower-scorer results.
- **A survey quantifying "practice questions but no improvement," CARS slowness, or narrow-to-two.** None exists that I could find. Everything in those rows is either a proxy PMQ item (**A**), general learning-science mechanism (**A**), or forum sentiment (**C**).
- **CARS passage count (9).** Confirmed only via prep-company pages, not the AAMC section overview I read. My reading-time arithmetic depends on it.
- **Rejected sources.** `gitnux.org/mcat-retake-statistics` and similar aggregators surfaced with precise-sounding claims ("58% of first-time takers below 510 retake within 12 months, Blueprint survey n=1,200," etc.). These are AI-generated statistics farms with no traceable underlying reports. I did not use any figure from them, and neither should the demo. `testpreppal.com` was used only as a pointer to the AAMC percentile PDF, whose figure I then confirmed against the AAMC and Magoosh renderings of the same table.
- **Reading depth caveat.** For Dunlosky et al. (2013), Cepeda et al. (2006), and Dunlosky & Lipko (2007) I read abstracts plus substantial secondary excerpts, not the full monographs. See the "how much read" column below.

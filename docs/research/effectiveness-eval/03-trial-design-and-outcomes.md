# Trial Design, Outcome Measurement, and Statistical Power for the Integrated Scheduler

**Scope.** How to run a study on real learners that could credibly support or refute the claim that the integrated scheduler produces better outcomes per hour of study than (a) practice tests alone, (b) Anki-style spaced repetition alone, (c) other adaptive prep products — and what it would cost.

**Evidence tiering used throughout.** **[A]** strong direct evidence or field consensus · **[B]** reasonable with stated limits · **[C]** weak, indirect, or single-source. Every load-bearing claim is tagged. Where I read only an abstract or a secondary citation, I say so inline and in `## Sources`.

**Reproducibility.** All power arithmetic in this document is generated by `docs/research/effectiveness-eval/power.py` (pure Python, no dependencies, ~5 s runtime). Change the assumptions at the top of each step and re-run.

## Recommendation

**Do not run the study implied by the claim. The claim as written is not testable by this team at any cost it can afford, and the most valuable thing this document can do is say so precisely.**

The claim has three comparators, all of which are *active controls* embodying practices with their own evidence bases (full-length practice testing = retrieval practice; Anki = spaced retrieval; competitor adaptive products = the same BKT/IRT machinery). Against active controls of this quality, the honest prior on the true effect is **d ≈ 0.10–0.20 on an independent achievement measure**, not d = 0.5 **[B]**. At d = 0.15 with a pretest covariate at r = .70 and 60% attrition, you need **893 enrolled learners per arm** — 2,679 across three arms — recruited from a US population of roughly 98,000 MCAT sittings per year **[C, see Sources]**, by a pre-launch company with zero users. That is not a budget problem. It is an arithmetic impossibility for the next 24 months.

What follows is a four-stage staircase. Each stage is independently fundable, produces a publishable or at least defensible artifact, and licenses a specific and narrower public claim. Stages 0 and 1 are reachable now. Stage 2 is reachable in year 1–2 *if* launch goes well. Stage 3 is descriptive forever.

### Stage 0 — Build the measurement instrument before you need it (start now, ~$0 cash, 3–6 weeks of content time). Confidence: **high**.

Nothing downstream is interpretable without this, and it is the one thing that gets *strictly harder* the longer you wait, because every item you use for instruction is permanently burned as an outcome measure.

1. **Sequester a held-out item set now.** Draw ~**300 items** (14% of the 2,108) stratified by Foundational Concept and knowledge component, and put them behind a hard technical boundary: never served by the scheduler, never in any practice queue, never in a flashcard, not present in the client bundle, not reachable by any content-authoring path. Treat exposure as an irreversible one-way door.
2. **Pre-register** the primary outcome, the analysis model (ANCOVA on the held-out set with diagnostic score as covariate), the attrition-handling plan, and the stopping rule — on OSF, before any data. Cost: zero. This single act moves you from "vendor marketing research" to "a study a skeptic can read," and it is the cheapest credibility purchase available **[B]**.
3. **Instrument dose properly**: active learning seconds, items attempted, unique KCs touched, and calendar days active, all logged per learner. "Per hour of study" is the load-bearing phrase in the claim and you currently have no defensible denominator for it (§Findings Q5).

### Stage 1 — One-week component experiments on a paid panel (months 1–3, ~$3k–$10k per experiment). Confidence: **high** that this is the right first study.

Randomise **one scheduling mechanism**, not the system. Recruit pre-med/MCAT-intending participants on a paid panel; two sessions a week apart; primary endpoint is a **delayed** retention test at day 7 on sequestered items.

At **n = 100/arm analysed** the MDE is **0.398 SD** unadjusted and **0.284 SD** with a pretest covariate at r = .70. At n = 150/arm: **0.325 / 0.232 SD**. Cost at Prolific's recommended $12/h for 2 participant-hours: **≈$3,200 for 200 participants** including platform fees, **≈$9,600 for 600** (§Power and cost table, Step 7).

This is well-powered for the effects that *mechanisms* actually produce — spaced vs. massed retrieval is g = 0.74 in meta-analysis **[A]**, distributed vs. massed in real classrooms d = 0.54 **[A]** — and badly powered for whole-system-vs-active-control effects. That asymmetry is the entire argument for component-wise evaluation (§Findings Q6).

Run three of these in the first year: (i) the FSRS interval policy vs. a fixed-interval control; (ii) the adaptive item-selection target success rate at two set points; (iii) the daily-plan interleaving/ordering rule vs. blocked-by-topic. Total ≈ **$10k–$30k**.

### Stage 2 — Internal-baseline RCT at launch, active control, held-out primary outcome (months 4–12, ~$25k–$70k). Confidence: **moderate**; contingent on reaching ~1,500–3,000 registrations.

**Two arms, both yours**: full integrated scheduler vs. a **well-implemented "sensible defaults" arm** (fixed-interval SRS + blocked practice + a static syllabus, same content, same UI polish, same 2,108-item pool minus the held-out set). Not a crippled placebo — a defensible good-practice baseline.

- **Primary outcome:** score on the sequestered held-out item set at week 8, ANCOVA-adjusted for the 140-item onboarding diagnostic.
- **Secondary:** delayed retention on a held-out subset at week 12; time-to-mastery per KC; a full-length practice test if you can license an independent one.
- **Target:** n = 150/arm analysed → MDE **0.232 SD** (r = .70). At 60% attrition that is **375/arm enrolled, 750 total**. At 40% attrition, **250/arm, 500 total**.
- **Interpretation ceiling, stated up front:** this design is powered to detect d ≥ ~0.23 and *cannot* rule out a real effect of 0.10. Pre-register that as a limitation rather than reporting a null as "no difference."

Do **not** add a competitor arm (§Findings Q5). Do **not** add a third arm unless you can fund it; splitting 750 learners three ways puts you back at MDE ≈ 0.28.

### Stage 3 — Observational score-linkage cohort (year 2+, low marginal cost). Confidence: **high that it is worth doing, high that it cannot support a causal claim.**

Consented, incentivised collection of self-reported MCAT scores with **document verification** for a verified subsample. Self-reported total test scores correlate r ≈ .82 with actual, but are systematically over-reported by ~25 points on the old 1600-scale SAT (d = 0.31), with over-reporting concentrated in low scorers **[B]** — which biases exactly the comparison you care about. Verification of a subsample is what turns this from unusable to usable.

This yields Khan Academy-class evidence: "learners who did X on our platform scored Y points higher, adjusted for baseline." That is ESSA Tier 3 at best, and its central weakness — dose is self-selected, motivated learners do more of everything — is unfixable by more data **[A]**.

### What I would explicitly not do

| Not this | Why |
|---|---|
| Months-long RCT vs. a named competitor | ToS exposure, dosage non-equivalence, cost of comparator access, and no equipoise defence (§Q5) |
| A/B testing the whole system on live users as the efficacy strategy | Needs ~100k users to be a sensible tool; you have zero **[C, practitioner source]** |
| Cluster randomisation | Requires clusters. A direct-to-consumer pre-launch product has none, and the design effect at ICC = 0.05, m = 25 inflates N by **2.20×** |
| Any in-app measure as the primary outcome | Endogenous: the system chooses what is practised, so it partly chooses the test (§Q2) |
| Claiming an MCAT-score surrogate | Surrogate validation requires trial-level meta-analysis across multiple trials measuring *both* endpoints. You will have zero such trials (§ next section) |

## The delayed-outcome problem

The outcome anyone cares about — an MCAT score 2–6 months out — is delayed past any iteration cycle, self-reported at best, confounded by everything else the learner does, and structurally missing for everyone who does not sit the exam. The instinct is to find a surrogate. **The literature's answer is that you cannot validate one, and that substituting an unvalidated one is a documented way to reach a confidently wrong conclusion.**

### What makes a surrogate defensible

The canonical operational criteria are Prentice's (1989) four **[A, via multiple secondary sources; I did not read Prentice 1989 directly]**:

1. Treatment has an effect on the true endpoint.
2. Treatment has an effect on the surrogate.
3. The surrogate is prognostic for (associated with) the true endpoint.
4. **The treatment effect on the true endpoint is fully captured by the surrogate** — formally F(T | S, Z) = F(T | S).

Criterion 4 is the one that matters and the one that is "rarely fully met in practice." It cannot be established by a significance test, because it requires demonstrating the *absence* of a treatment–outcome relationship after conditioning on the surrogate **[A]**.

Supporting quantitative machinery, and its known failure modes:

- **Freedman's proportion of treatment effect explained (PTE)** = 1 − β_S/β. Despite the name it is **not bounded in [0,1]**, is highly unstable when the treatment effect β is small, and relies on strong and often unverifiable causal-mediation assumptions **[A]**. Note the interaction with everything else in this document: your true effect *is* small, which is precisely the regime where PTE is least trustworthy.
- **Trial-level R² (Buyse–Molenberghs)** is the modern standard: correlate the treatment effects on surrogate and true endpoint **across many trials**. The conventional threshold in oncology is R² ≥ 0.7, and "for a good surrogate end point we [want] ρ² well over 0.5, ideally close to 1" **[A]**.
- **The surrogate paradox**: the treatment improves the surrogate, the surrogate correlates positively with the outcome, **and the treatment still harms the outcome** **[A]**. This is not hypothetical hand-wringing; it is why FDA-style guidance insists that pivotal trials use direct measures when a replacement endpoint has not been properly validated.
- The governing aphorism, from Fleming & DeMets via the JAMA piece: **"a correlate does not a surrogate make"** **[A]**.

### Why this team can never validate an MCAT surrogate

Trial-level validation is a **meta-analysis over trials that measured both endpoints**. To validate "held-out item score at week 8" as a surrogate for MCAT total, you would need multiple randomised trials in which you observed both the held-out score *and* official MCAT scores, then show the treatment effects track each other across trials. This team will have zero trials with the true endpoint, because it will never receive official scores. **The surrogate route is closed, permanently, for structural reasons that no budget fixes.** **[A — this is a direct application of the validation requirement, not a cited finding]**

### The reframe that actually works

Stop treating the held-out item set as a *surrogate* for MCAT score and start treating it as **the outcome of record in its own right** — a domain-referenced achievement measure over the AAMC Foundational Concepts.

This changes the burden of proof from **surrogate validity** (impossible: needs trials with both endpoints) to **construct validity and content representativeness** (achievable: needs blueprint alignment, adequate reliability, and independence from instruction). It also changes what you may claim: not "raises MCAT scores" but "produces higher performance on an independently-blueprinted MCAT-content assessment that the system never taught to." The second is defensible and the first is not.

Two consequences worth internalising:

- **Do not benchmark against the MCAT's own measurement precision.** Commercial sources put the MCAT total-score confidence band at roughly ±2 points **[C — I could only source this to commercial test-prep sites, not to AAMC technical documentation; flagged in "What I could not verify"]**. If that figure is right, a large part of the score movement a learner experiences is measurement noise, and any claim phrased in scale points is fighting that noise. Report in SD units on your own instrument.
- **Delay is not only a nuisance, it is the point.** Every mechanism in this system (FSRS, forgetting decay, mistake review) is a *retention* mechanism, and retention effects are invisible at immediate post-test and largest at delay. Distributed-practice meta-analysis finds larger effects at longer retention intervals **[A]**. An immediate post-test systematically *understates* the thing you built. Any endpoint shorter than ~7 days is measuring the wrong construct.

## Findings

### Q1 — Surrogate and intermediate outcomes: what the literature requires

Covered in full above. The reportable criteria for surrogate validity, condensed:

| Criterion | Source of standard | Can this team meet it? |
|---|---|---|
| Biological/theoretical plausibility of the link | Clinical-epidemiology consensus **[A]** | Yes — mastery of tested content plausibly mediates score |
| Epidemiologic evidence: consistent surrogate–outcome association across populations | Clinical-epidemiology consensus **[A]** | Partially — could be built from Stage 3 observational data |
| Prentice criterion 4: treatment effect fully captured by surrogate | Prentice 1989 **[A, secondary]** | **No** — requires observing the true endpoint under randomisation |
| Trial-level R² ≥ ~0.7 across trials | Buyse–Molenberghs; oncology convention **[A]** | **No** — requires multiple trials with both endpoints |
| Absence of surrogate paradox | Principal-stratification literature **[A]** | Unknowable without the above |

**Documented hazards of substituting one:** unstable and uninterpretable PTE at small effects; the surrogate paradox; and — the education-specific analogue — surrogate validity that does not extrapolate across intervention classes. The clinical literature is explicit that a surrogate validated for one drug class may not transfer to another **[A]**. The education parallel: an intermediate measure validated for, say, a content-coverage intervention may not be valid for a *scheduling* intervention, because scheduling acts through retention timing rather than content acquisition.

### Q2 — What to measure instead, and how

The dominant threat here is **endogeneity**, and it deserves stating formally because it is the deepest measurement problem in this system.

> The scheduler chooses which items the learner practises. Any in-app measure is computed over a sample of items the treatment itself selected. So the treatment influences both the numerator and the sampling frame of the denominator. A scheduler that steers practice toward easy KCs, or toward items it has already shown, will look better on every in-app metric while teaching less. Worse, the BKT mastery estimate is a *model output*, and comparing arms on mastery estimated by the treatment arm's own model is comparing a system to its own opinion of itself.

This is the education version of the researcher-made-measure problem, and that problem is enormous and well quantified: effect sizes are **roughly twice as large** on experimenter-made measures as on independent ones **[A]**; recent PreK-12 meta-analyses found researcher-developed outcomes inflated effects by **0.30 SD (math)** and **0.27 SD (science)**, with measure type a *stronger* predictor of reported effect than randomisation itself **[B, from a methods preprint citing those meta-analyses; I did not read the underlying meta-analyses]**. Kraft's benchmark data shows the same gradient: median 0.10 SD on broad measures vs. **0.17 SD on narrow** ones **[A]**.

| Candidate measure | What it can support | How it is gamed / confounded | Endogeneity |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sequestered held-out item set** | Primary causal claim about content mastery | Only by exposure leakage — which is *technically preventable*, unlike the others | **None, if truly sequestered.** The single reason to prefer it |
| **Delayed retention test at fixed interval** | The retention claim specifically; the mechanism you actually built | Differential dropout at follow-up correlates with mastery; needs delay to be *identical* across arms, not "about a week" | None if drawn from held-out pool |
| **Full-length practice test score** | Face validity, learner-legible, closest to the real thing | Third-party FLs are miscalibrated (commercial reports of ±5–15 scale points of deflation by vendor **[C]**); AAMC FLs are the good ones but are a paid, finite, exposure-limited resource; and any FL your product also *teaches from* is burned | High if your product uses the same FLs |
| **Time-to-mastery per KC** | Efficiency claims — directly relevant to "per hour" | **Definitionally circular.** "Mastery" is your BKT posterior crossing a threshold you chose. Changing the threshold changes the result. Comparing arms with different scheduling on a shared BKT model still confounds practice-order with model dynamics | **Severe** — the metric is a treatment output |
| **Transfer to novel item types** | The strongest claim available: generalisation beyond trained format | Expensive to author; low reliability if the set is small; risks measuring reading ability rather than content | None if authored independently |
| **Self-reported exam score** | Descriptive cohort claims; marketing with disclosure | r ≈ .82 with actual score; mean over-report ≈ 25 points (d = 0.31); 39% over-report vs. 10% under-report; over-reporting worst among low scorers **[B]**. Missing entirely for non-sitters, non-randomly | Not endogenous, but severely selected |

**Composite recommendation.** Primary = sequestered held-out set at week 8. Co-primary or key secondary = delayed retention on a held-out subset at week 12. Report time-to-mastery and in-app accuracy as **implementation/dose measures only** — Slavin's framing for developer-made measures, which is the right one **[B]**. Never as efficacy outcomes.

#### The held-out item set: how large, how selected, how protected

**How large.** Two constraints bind. *Reliability:* you need the set's internal consistency high enough that it is not the bottleneck on your effect estimate; for a 4-option MCAT-like set, ~120–150 scored items is a reasonable target for a total score, and you want to administer it in two or three sittings rather than one 150-item marathon. *Multiplicity:* you also want per-domain subscores across 10 Foundational Concepts, which pushes you higher. **Take ~300 items sequestered, ~150 administered per learner via two matched half-forms, ~150 held in reserve** for the week-12 delayed test and for replacing items that leak.

That is **14% of your 2,108-item bank permanently removed from instruction.** State that cost explicitly to whoever owns content: it is the price of having any interpretable outcome at all, and it is cheapest to pay now. With 422 KCs and 300 items you get sub-1 item per KC, so **do not attempt KC-level held-out scoring** — blueprint the held-out set at the Foundational Concept level (10 domains × ~30 items) and accept that KC-level claims will have to rest on in-app (endogenous) data with appropriate caveats.

**How selected.** Stratified random sampling within the crossing of Foundational Concept × KC-cluster × difficulty band, then *frozen*. Not hand-picked by anyone with an interest in the result — hand-picking is how "over-alignment with the intervention" gets in **[A]**. Match the two half-forms on blueprint and on predicted difficulty so that the week-8 and week-12 forms are exchangeable. If your difficulty predictions are cold-start estimates rather than calibrated parameters, say so; form equivalence then rests on blueprint matching alone, which is weaker.

**How protected from exposure.** The CAT literature is unambiguous that exposure destroys the measurement property you need: **"When an item has been overexposed, the probability of a correct response no longer depends on the examinee's ability level, but rather on prior familiarity with the item"** **[A]**. Also note the distinction the licensure-testing literature draws: **item *exposure* is not the same as item *compromise*** — exposure controls are preventive, and they do not fix an item that has already leaked **[A]**. So:

1. **Hard technical boundary.** Held-out items live in a separate store the scheduler has no read path to. Enforce it with an architectural test, not a code-review convention — you already have `tests/architecture/boundaries.test.ts`, which is the right place for it.
2. **Administer only in proctored-ish sessions** (one-time tokens, single session, no review-after, no explanations shown, no copy affordances). Showing explanations converts your outcome measure into instruction.
3. **Zero reuse across learners in different arms? No — the opposite.** Everyone gets the same form; that is what makes scores comparable. The exposure risk you are managing is *leakage to future cohorts*, not within-study contamination.
4. **Monitor for compromise.** Track per-item p-values over calendar time. A held-out item whose difficulty drifts easier for later cohorts is a leaked item; retire it and document the retirement before unblinding. This is a cheap version of the compromised-item-detection idea and it is the only defence that survives contact with a motivated pre-med forum **[B — the monitoring principle is standard; this specific lightweight implementation is my construction]**.
5. **Budget for burn.** Assume ~10–20% of the set is unusable per year in a population that actively shares materials. The 150-item reserve is that budget.

### Q3 — Trial designs, ranked for this situation

Scored in full in `## Design comparison`. The narrative:

1. **Short-horizon randomised component experiment on a paid panel — best available.** Feasible with zero users, cheap, fast, and adequately powered for mechanism-sized effects. Threats: external validity (paid one-week participants are not learners 4 months out from a life-defining exam), and demand characteristics.
2. **Between-subjects RCT with an internal active-control arm — the target for year 1–2.** The only design that can support a causal efficacy claim. Threats, all live: **differential attrition** (learners assigned to the deliberately-plainer baseline arm quit more, and they are not a random subset); **self-selection** into the study (volunteers are more motivated than the market); **novelty effects**; and **contamination** (nothing stops the baseline arm from also buying UWorld and running Anki — and nothing should).
3. **A/B on system variants — right instrument, wrong company size.** This is what mature platforms do: Duolingo runs ~100s concurrently, ~500 per quarter, 16,000 lifetime, gated by *guardrail metrics* that may not be harmed without senior review **[B, company/press sources]**. The practitioner threshold quoted for A/B testing making sense is **~100,000 users** **[C, single practitioner source]**. Adopt the *discipline* now (pre-specified metrics, guardrails, an experiment registry) and the *practice* later.
4. **Within-subjects / crossover — attractive, mostly invalid here.** Each learner as own control kills between-person variance, which is exactly what you want at small N. But learning is **irreversible**: you cannot wash out having learned amino-acid structures, so the carryover assumption fails outright. Narrow legitimate use: crossover on *within-learner, item-level* assignment (this KC scheduled by rule A, that KC by rule B) where the units are KCs rather than people. That is a genuinely good design for Stage 1 mechanisms and it is how platform experiments are usually run.
5. **Matched-pair — useful as an *adjunct*.** Pane et al. matched schools into similar pairs *then* randomised, which is variance reduction inside an RCT, not an alternative to it **[A]**. Matching without randomisation (matched QED) caps you at ESSA Tier 2 and inherits selection bias; effect sizes are systematically *higher* in quasi-experiments than RCTs **[A]**, so a matched design that finds a positive effect will be discounted by exactly the readers you are trying to persuade.
6. **Stepped-wedge / staged rollout — appealing for the wrong reason.** Genuine advantages: feasible when simultaneous rollout is impossible, everyone eventually receives the intervention (which helps recruitment and equipoise), each cluster serves as its own control **[A]**. But: it needs **clusters**, which you lack; sample-size calculation must absorb **calendar-time confounding**, which *degrades* precision relative to a parallel design; and it is **less** powerful than a parallel design when ICC is small **[A]**. Given that a consumer app's natural "clusters" are tiny or nonexistent and ICC is unknown, this is the wrong tool. The BMJ guidance is blunt that stepped-wedge should be avoided when another rigorous design is feasible **[A]**.
7. **Cluster randomisation — not applicable.** No clusters. If you later partner with post-bacc programmes or university pre-med advising offices, revisit: at ICC = 0.05 and m = 25 learners per site the design effect is **2.20×**, at ICC = 0.10 it is **3.40×**. Cluster designs also import the WWC's cluster-specific attrition machinery (cluster-level attrition, individual-level attrition, cluster representativeness, joiners and leavers) **[A]**.

**Blinding is impossible, and here is what that costs.** Learners see which product they are using; the team knows the allocation; there is no plausible sham scheduler. Concretely: (i) you cannot separate the scheduler's instructional effect from motivation/placebo, so the estimand is "the effect of *being given* this system," which is arguably the commercially relevant quantity but is not a mechanism claim; (ii) **novelty effects are on the same timescale as your study** — a 14-week longitudinal study found gamification effects began decaying after **four weeks**, with the decay lasting two to six weeks and reducing a moderate effect to null in the worst case, before a partial "familiarisation" recovery at 6–10 weeks **[B, single study, gamification rather than scheduling]**. A study with an 8-week endpoint therefore lands squarely in the novelty-decay trough, which is an argument for the 12-week secondary endpoint being the more honest one; (iii) unblinded outcome collection with individual participant data creates differential-selection risk into the analytic sample **[A]**; (iv) mitigations that are actually available: automated and identical outcome administration in both arms, allocation concealed until after the diagnostic, analyst blinded to arm labels during the pre-specified analysis, and an active control with equal UI investment so the contrast is *scheduling* rather than *polish*.

### Q4 — Statistical power

Full arithmetic in `## Power and cost table`. The three sentences that should change the team's plans:

- **The effect you are hunting is d ≈ 0.10–0.20, not 0.5.** Kraft's benchmarks from **1,942 effect sizes across 747 RCTs** of education interventions with standardised achievement outcomes: **median 0.10 SD**; proposed benchmarks <0.05 small, 0.05–0.20 medium, ≥0.20 large. Pre-registered US-DoE-funded RCTs have a **median of 0.03 SD** across 139 effect sizes from 49 RCTs **[A, read in the author's PDF]**. Your comparators are stronger than "business as usual," which pushes the plausible effect toward the bottom of that range.
- **At d = 0.15 you need 699 analysed per arm** unadjusted, 357 with a pretest covariate at r = .70, and **893 enrolled per arm at 60% attrition**.
- **The real-world existence proof cuts both ways.** Duolingo's Video Call RCT — the closest published analogue to what you would run — sent **57,254 invitation emails**, randomised **658**, analysed **567**, and detected a raw post-test difference of **d ≈ 0.087** **[A, read in full]**. Their planning assumption was 42% attrition (from a prior in-house study I could not access). They achieved that N by inviting 57,254 people. You do not have 57,254 people.

### Q5 — Comparing against a competitor product, specifically

**Recommendation: don't. Use a well-implemented internal baseline instead.** Five reasons, in descending order of decisiveness.

**1. Dosage equivalence is not merely hard, it is ill-defined — and it is the whole claim.** The claim is "better outcomes *per hour*." A competitor hour and your hour are not the same object: different active-vs-passive ratios, different item difficulty targets (yours are explicitly *differentiated* target success rates), different idle-time accounting, different definitions of a "session." Duolingo hit this exact wall and handled it by *equalising design-time*: they required 2 Video Calls/day against 1 regular lesson/day, "selected to make learning time comparable... as the average Video Call length was approximately half the length of a regular lesson" **[A, read in full]**. You can do that arithmetic on *your own two arms* because you instrument both. You cannot do it on a competitor whose telemetry you will never see. With a competitor arm you are reduced to **self-reported hours**, which reintroduces exactly the reporting bias documented for self-reported achievement data **[B, by analogy]**.

There is a further trap: even if you match hours, the two arms are not matched on *what an hour costs the learner*. Duolingo's own data shows how weakly time predicts outcome in self-directed app use — median 203 hours to finish seven units, IQR 114–332, and **no significant positive correlation between time spent and proficiency** (a *negative* one in Spanish) **[A]**. If time-on-task barely predicts outcome within a single product, "outcomes per hour" across two products is a very fragile denominator.

**2. Terms of service are a real and asymmetric risk.** Commercial software terms routinely prohibit using the product for benchmarking, competitive analysis, or building competing products, and prohibit disclosing benchmark results without written permission. Representative clauses: *"may not distribute or disclose to third parties the results of benchmarking, competitive analysis... without the prior written permission"*; *"must not use... to inspect or analyze the Technology or the outputs for benchmarking or comparison purposes... to acquire any technical specifications and gain a competitive technological or business advantage"* **[A, as examples of drafting practice — these are from NVIDIA and UiPath, not from MCAT-prep vendors, and I did not read any MCAT competitor's ToS]**. Critically, these restrictions are **contractual and enforceable independently of copyright**: *Bowers v. Baystate* held a contractual reverse-engineering ban enforceable and not preempted by copyright, so the contract can reach further than copyright's fair-use carve-outs **[B, single law-firm secondary source; treat as a flag to raise with counsel, not a legal conclusion]**. The asymmetry: you are a pre-launch company and they are an incumbent with lawyers. **Not legal advice — have counsel read the specific ToS of any product before a single learner is enrolled.**

**3. You would be paying to give your learners a competitor's product.** Providing comparator access means buying subscriptions for an entire arm for the study duration. At consumer med-ed prices (AMBOSS + UWorld together are cited at ~$750/year **[C, review site]**; MCAT-prep pricing I did not verify), a 250-learner comparator arm for six months is plausibly $25k–$60k in pure comparator cost — likely exceeding the entire rest of the study budget, and spent on making a competitor's revenue line look good.

**4. There is no equipoise defence for the comparator arm.** See Q7. You cannot honestly tell a learner "expert opinion is genuinely divided about whether our unlaunched product or the market-leading one is better for you," because there *is* no expert community holding an opinion about your unlaunched product.

**5. An active internal baseline gets you almost all the inferential value.** The comparator that makes the claim meaningful is "a sensible, well-implemented alternative use of the same hours." You can *build* that: fixed-interval SRS, blocked practice, static syllabus, same content, same polish. This is the design Kulik & Fletcher's meta-analysis implicitly warns about and thereby validates as the honest test — ITS effects were **0.66 SD** median against conventional instruction across 50 evaluations, but in evaluations with **non-conventional control groups** the effects "were small" **[A, abstract + ResearchGate full text of results section]**. That single contrast is the most important number in this entire document: **the difference between a 0.66 and a ~0.1 effect is almost entirely the choice of control group.** Your claim is explicitly framed against strong controls, so you must expect and plan for the small number.

**When a head-to-head *would* be justified:** after Stage 2 shows a positive internal-baseline effect; with counsel's written sign-off on the specific ToS; with funded comparator access; and ideally executed by an independent third-party evaluator so the vendor-funding criticism does not land on you.

### Q6 — Cheaper designs that yield real information

**a) Short-horizon single-mechanism randomised experiments — the highest-value tool you have.** One week, one rule, delayed endpoint. $3k–$10k. Powered for real mechanism effects (MDE 0.28 SD at n = 100/arm with covariate). The ASSISTments/E-TRIALS programme is the proof of concept that this is a legitimate research modality and not a compromise: a free infrastructure explicitly built so researchers can run "low-cost, small-scale randomized control trials that assess pedagogical interventions" inside a live platform, running as few as three supported experiment types on single problem sets **[B]**. Note also their methods response to the small-N problem: **variance-reducing estimators** (LOOP / ReLOOP) that exploit baseline covariates and historical auxiliary data to gain precision over a naïve t-test **[B]**. That is the same lever as the ANCOVA design factor below, and it is free power — use it.

**b) Single-case / N-of-1 with replication — legitimate, and there is an actual standard.** These are "the only designs without a comparison group eligible for a WWC review" **[A]**, which means a well-run series can in principle enter the formal evidence base. The bar is specific: for multiple-baseline/multiple-probe, treatment-reversal, and changing-criterion designs, **at least six data points in the initial baseline phase** for *Meets WWC Standards Without Reservations* (raised from five in v5.0), five or more per subsequent phase, at least **three attempts to demonstrate an effect at three different points in time**, and — for a WWC intervention report — **at least 20 cases across studies** with design-comparable effect sizes **[A, read in the Handbook]**. For alternating-treatment designs, at least five data points per condition. Where this fits: it is a strong fit for *within-learner scheduling* questions where the dependent variable is repeatable (e.g., retention of matched KC sets under two interval policies), and a poor fit for whole-system evaluation, because you cannot withdraw "having a study plan."

**c) Paid-panel lab studies with a one-week retention endpoint — see Stage 1.** The honest limitation is population validity: a Prolific participant paid $24 is not a learner four months from an exam that determines their career, and motivation is the moderator most likely to interact with a *scheduling* intervention (a scheduler's value is largely that it removes planning burden from someone who is overwhelmed — a state you cannot induce in a one-week study). Treat these as mechanism screens, not efficacy trials, and say so.

**d) Component-wise vs. whole-system evaluation — the explicit argument.**

**I argue for component-wise, and against whole-system, for this team at this stage — with one qualification.**

*For component-wise:*
- **Power.** Component effects are large (spaced vs. massed retrieval g = 0.74; distributed vs. massed in classrooms d = 0.54; retrieval vs. restudy g ≈ 0.50, rising to ≈0.73 with feedback **[A, the g = 0.50/0.73 figures via a peer-reviewed meta-analysis citing Rowland 2014, not read directly]**). Integration effects against an active control are small. Power scales with d², so a 0.5 effect needs **64/arm** and a 0.15 effect needs **699/arm** — an 11× difference in cost for the same confidence.
- **Diagnosticity.** A whole-system null tells you nothing actionable: five mechanisms, any of which could be helping, hurting, or cancelling. A component result tells you what to change on Monday.
- **The components already have independent evidence bases; the integration does not.** So the marginal information is concentrated in the integration *decisions* — the interval policy, the target success rate, the ordering rule, the time-budget allocation across four activity types. Those are exactly what component experiments test.
- **Bundling is a known interpretive hazard.** The WWC's position is that a bundled intervention can yield a valid impact estimate for "the package," but that the bundle remains problematic for synthesis if components are not separately eligible **[A]**. Translated: a whole-system result is a claim about *your specific bundle*, non-transferable and non-decomposable.

*Against component-wise (the honest counter-case):* the claim on the table is explicitly about the **integration** — "the integrated scheduler produces better outcomes per hour" — and component experiments cannot test an integration claim. A system can be a set of individually-good mechanisms that fight each other over a fixed time budget; that failure mode is *only* visible at the system level. There is real precedent for integration mattering: Pane et al. found **no effect in year 1 and ~0.20 SD in year 2** for the same curriculum, which is an implementation/integration effect, not a mechanism effect **[A]**.

*Resolution:* components first, because they are affordable and diagnostic; **one** whole-system internal-baseline RCT once you have users, because it is the only thing that tests the actual claim; and never a whole-system trial as your *first* study, because a null from an underpowered whole-system trial is the worst possible outcome — expensive, uninformative, and easy to misread as disproof.

### Q7 — Ethics and consent

**Not legal advice. Get counsel and, for anything you intend to publish, an IRB.**

**Does equipoise hold?** Clinical equipoise, per Freedman (1987), is satisfied when there is "genuine uncertainty within the expert medical community — not necessarily on the part of the individual investigator — about the preferred treatment"; the underlying norm is that **no participant should be randomised to something the expert community believes inferior to the established standard of care** **[A, via multiple secondary sources; I did not read Freedman 1987 directly]**.

Applied honestly, arm by arm:

- **Integrated scheduler vs. internal sensible-defaults baseline: equipoise plausibly holds.** [B] Both arms deliver the full 2,108-item bank, all 114 lessons, spaced repetition, and adaptive practice. The contrast is *scheduling policy*, and there is genuine uncertainty — including among the team — about whether the integrated policy beats sensible defaults. Nobody is denied content. This is the design that is *both* the most informative and the most ethically comfortable, which is a rare alignment and a strong reason to prefer it.
- **Integrated scheduler vs. a deliberately degraded arm: equipoise fails.** Do not build a straw-man control.
- **Your unlaunched product vs. a market-leading competitor: equipoise fails in the other direction.** There is no expert community with an opinion on your unlaunched product, so you cannot claim community-level uncertainty; and a learner randomised *away* from your product toward the incumbent is arguably fine, while one randomised *toward* your unvalidated product and away from the incumbent is the ethically exposed case. That asymmetry is a second, independent reason to drop the competitor arm.
- **Withholding a feature you already believe works: equipoise degrades over time.** Freedman's point is that the obligation attaches to the *community's* uncertainty, but the practical implication for you is that once a Stage 1 experiment establishes a mechanism, continuing to randomise learners away from it needs a fresh justification.

**Does an IRB apply to a commercial product?** As a private entity not receiving federal research funds, you are generally **outside the Common Rule** — this is precisely the gap the Facebook emotional-contagion episode exposed: Facebook manipulated 689,003 users' news feeds; Cornell's IRB reviewed only the downstream data analysis as a "pre-existing dataset"; and **no institution reviewed the ethically significant element, the manipulation itself** **[A, multiple peer-reviewed sources]**. Two lessons that transfer directly:

1. **A terms-of-service "we may use your data for research" clause is legal permission, not ethical consent.** Grimmelmann's critique was that the study "did not follow professional standards for informed consent" and that the ToS argument "conflated legal permission with ethical consent" **[A]**. Do not rely on your ToS.
2. **The defensible middle path exists and was described contemporaneously**: even where full Common Rule consent would bias the study, an IRB could reasonably require *some* study-specific information plus an opportunity to decline this particular study — i.e., "alteration" of consent elements, not a blanket waiver **[B, legal-academic blog]**. That is exactly the standard to hold yourselves to.

**Practical consent posture.**
- **Stage 1 paid-panel studies:** full written consent, standard. If you want to publish, use an independent IRB (commercial IRBs review company-sponsored protocols routinely). Budget a low four figures.
- **Stage 2 in-product RCT:** study-specific consent at enrolment, not buried in the ToS. Disclose: that there are two versions, that assignment is random, that you do not know which is better, what data is collected, that declining costs nothing, and that they may withdraw and receive the other version. Duolingo's model is instructive and worth copying: participants were **pre-consented to research contact**, invited to a *specific* study, compensated ($20 plus their official score report), **compensated even on partial completion or exclusion**, and the control arm received the paid feature at the end "for parity" **[A, read in full]**. That last detail — the control arm gets the withheld thing afterwards — is cheap, and it is the single most effective equipoise and goodwill mechanism available.
- **Never randomise anything that plausibly *reduces* study effectiveness** for a high-stakes candidate. That includes "no spaced repetition" arms, artificial delays, or withholding mistake review. Your control arm should be something you would be comfortable shipping.

**Reputational risk, stated plainly.** Pre-med forums are dense, fast, and adversarial toward prep vendors. The specific failure mode is not "they ran a study"; it is **"they ran a secret study on paying exam candidates and the losers paid full price."** The mitigations are all cheap and all must be decided *before* launch: pre-registration (so you cannot be accused of fishing); consent that a hostile reader would call fair; compensation for the baseline arm and post-study access to the full system; publishing nulls as well as positives; and never using an in-app measure as the headline number. The LSE critique names the failure modes you must avoid by name — research "instrumentalised to recruit users under the guise of pilots or trials," findings that are not educationally meaningful, and certification as a "pay-to-play credibility mechanism" **[B, expert blog]**. Assume you will be read by someone looking for exactly those three things.

### Q9 — Beyond the brief

**a) The "per hour" claim needs a pre-specified estimand, and it is not obvious.** "Outcomes per hour" is a ratio, and ratio estimands behave badly: they are undefined at zero hours, unstable at low hours, and their treatment effect is not the ratio of treatment effects. Two defensible alternatives: (i) fix dose by design and compare outcomes (Duolingo's approach — require equal-ish study time in both arms, then compare scores; clean but tests a different question than the marketing claim); (ii) estimate outcome-on-dose slopes per arm and compare the slopes, accepting that dose is post-randomisation and therefore only descriptive. **Pre-specify which one.** Deciding after seeing the data is the single most likely way this study loses credibility.

**b) Compliance-based exclusion is attrition, and the WWC will treat it that way.** Duolingo excluded participants who missed weekly usage requirements. That is defensible for a mechanism question but it converts the estimand from intent-to-treat to something closer to a per-protocol effect. WWC v5.0 has explicit machinery for complier-average-causal-effect estimates, including complier-specific attrition rates (equations G.6–G.8) and the requirement that attrition for *compliers* be assessed against the same Table C.1 boundaries **[A, read in the Handbook]**. Report ITT as primary. If you report a per-protocol or CACE estimate, label it as such.

**c) Guardrail metrics belong in the design now, not later.** Duolingo's practice — a set of metrics that "can never be hurt without a very deliberate and senior-level review," and deliberately *not* including monetisation among them **[B]** — is the mechanism that prevents an experimentation programme from optimising engagement at the expense of learning. For an exam-prep product, the guardrail should be the delayed-retention measure, and the person who can override it should not be the person who owns growth.

**d) You have a Goodhart problem baked into the architecture.** BKT mastery and FSRS retrievability are *simultaneously* the scheduler's control signal and the most natural progress metric to show learners and to report internally. Any drift in the model, any change to a threshold, moves the metric without moving learning. Keep at least one measure structurally outside the model's reach — which is a second, independent argument for the sequestered item set.

**e) The reachable-evidence ceiling has a name, and it is not WWC.** ESSA Tier 1 and Tier 2 both require a multi-site sample of **at least 350 students across sites** **[A]**. Tier 1 additionally requires an experimental study meeting **WWC standards without reservations** plus population *and* setting overlap. Two things follow. First, the 350 threshold is *not* the binding constraint for you — power is; you need more than 350 for d = 0.15 regardless. Second, ESSA is a **K-12 procurement** framework and your buyer is an individual adult consumer, so no one will ever *require* you to hold a tier. It remains the only shared vocabulary for "how good is this evidence," which is why the claim table below is organised by it.

**f) Attrition has a formal cliff, and you should know where it is.** WWC Table C.1 gives the maximum *differential* attrition that still counts as low attrition, as a function of *overall* attrition. Selected values **[A, read in the Handbook]**:

| Overall attrition | Max differential (cautious) | Max differential (optimistic) |
|---|---|---|
| 10% | 6.3 pp | 10.9 pp |
| 20% | 5.4 pp | 10.0 pp |
| 30% | 4.1 pp | 8.2 pp |
| 40% | 2.6 pp | 6.0 pp |
| 50% | 1.0 pp | 3.7 pp |
| 57% | 0.0 pp | 2.1 pp |
| **≥58%** | **not attainable** | 1.9 pp |

Read the last row carefully: **at overall attrition of 58% or higher, no differential attrition rate can achieve low attrition under the cautious boundary.** A voluntary months-long consumer study will plausibly exceed 58%. Two implications: (i) your realistic attrition planning assumption and the WWC's low-attrition threshold are in direct conflict, so a WWC "without reservations" rating on a long consumer trial is likely out of reach on attrition grounds alone; (ii) v5.0 chose to *relax* the consequence — high-attrition RCTs no longer need to demonstrate baseline equivalence to reach *Meets WWC Standards With Reservations*, provided the authors use an acceptable adjustment strategy **[A]**. **So pre-specify the adjustment strategy.** Also note: for interventions "delivered outside of regular school hours" and "postsecondary interventions," the Handbook says the **cautious** boundary typically *should* be used **[A]** — which describes your product exactly.

**g) Retention is the differentiator; measure it like you mean it.** Distributed-practice benefits grow with retention interval **[A]**, and your learners study 2–6 months for one exam. That is the longest, most favourable retention interval in consumer ed-tech, and it is the one place where your architecture has a genuine theoretical edge over practice-tests-alone. It also means a 7-day delayed test *understates* your case. If you can afford exactly one methodological luxury, buy a **longer delay** rather than a bigger N.

**h) Expanding vs. uniform spacing is a settled question you may be over-engineering.** Meta-analysis found **no significant difference** between expanding and uniform spacing schedules under retrieval practice (g = 0.034, 95% CI [−0.10, 0.17], I² = 0%), with the moderator being number of exposures **[A]**. If FSRS complexity is being justified on expanding-schedule grounds, that justification is weak — which makes "FSRS vs. fixed interval" an excellent, genuinely uncertain, cheap Stage 1 experiment.

## Power and cost table

### Formula and tool

Pure Python, no dependencies: `docs/research/effectiveness-eval/power.py`. Two-sample comparison of means, two-sided α = .05, power = .80.

**Normal approximation (the one to reason with):**

```
n per group = 2 (z_{1-α/2} + z_{1-β})² / d²

z_{1-α/2} = 1.959964     (α = .05, two-sided)
z_{1-β}   = 0.841621     (power = .80)
2 (1.959964 + 0.841621)² = 15.6978

  =>  n per group = 15.6978 / d²
```

**Exact (noncentral t), used for the table:** power = 1 − F_{T′}(t_crit; df = 2n−2, ncp = d·√(n/2)), with F_{T′} computed by integrating Φ(t√(u/df) − ncp) against the χ²_df density (Simpson's rule, 4,000 nodes). Verified: each tabled n achieves power in [0.800, 0.808].

## Design comparison

Scored 1 (poor) to 5 (excellent) for *this* team: pre-launch, zero users, cost-sensitive, small. Cost is time-and-money to execute; Credibility is how a skeptical third party would rate the resulting evidence.

| Design | Feasibility now | Feasibility post-launch | Credibility | Cost | Time | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Short-horizon single-mechanism RCT (paid panel, 1 wk, delayed endpoint)** | **5** | 5 | 3 | **5** ($3k–$10k) | **5** (4–6 wks) | **Do first.** Only design that is both affordable and adequately powered for the effects it targets |
| **Within-learner / item-level crossover on KC sets** | 4 | **5** | 3 | **5** | **5** | **Do.** Kills between-person variance. Valid only where units are KCs, never whole-system |
| **Between-subjects RCT, internal active-control arm, sequestered outcome** | 1 | 3 | **5** | 2 ($25k–$70k) | 2 (6–9 mo) | **The target.** The only design that tests the actual claim. Gate on reaching ~1,500–3,000 registrations |
| **Single-case / N-of-1 with replication (≥3 demonstrations, ≥6 baseline points)** | 4 | 4 | 3 | 4 | 3 | **Adjunct.** WWC-eligible, which is unusual for a no-control design. Fits repeatable retention DVs, not whole-system |
| **A/B on system variants** | **1** | 4 | 2 | **5** | **5** | **Adopt the discipline now, the practice at scale.** ~100k users is the practitioner threshold; guardrail metrics from day one |
| **Matched-pair (as randomisation-blocking inside an RCT)** | 2 | 3 | **5** | 3 | 2 | **Yes, as a component.** Precisely what Pane et al. did |
| **Matched QED (matching *instead of* randomising)** | 3 | 4 | 2 | 3 | 3 | **Avoid as primary.** ESSA Tier 2 ceiling; QED effect sizes run systematically higher than RCTs, so a positive finding gets discounted |
| **Stepped-wedge / staged rollout** | 1 | 2 | 4 | 2 | 1 | **No.** Needs clusters; time-confounding degrades precision; less powerful than parallel at low ICC; BMJ guidance says avoid when another rigorous design is feasible |
| **Cluster randomisation** | **1** | 2 | **5** | 1 | 1 | **No.** No clusters exist. DE of 2.2–3.4× at plausible ICC on top of an already-infeasible N |
| **Head-to-head vs. commercial competitor** | 1 | 1 | 4 | **1** | 1 | **No.** ToS exposure, no equipoise, unfixable dosage non-equivalence, comparator cost likely exceeds the study |
| **Observational score-linkage cohort (self-report + verified subsample)** | 1 | **5** | 1 | 4 | 2 | **Do, as description only.** Khan-class evidence; ESSA Tier 3 ceiling; dose is self-selected |

**Threat-by-design summary.**

| Threat | Where it bites hardest | Cheapest real mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| **Contamination** | Any long consumer trial — nothing stops either arm from also using UWorld and Anki | Measure it (outside-resource survey at baseline and endline), report it, do not try to prevent it. Duolingo handled this by *screening on it* (excluding learners using other apps/tutors) — effective, but it narrows generalisability sharply |
| **Differential attrition** | Between-subjects RCT with a plainer baseline arm | Equal-polish active control; incentives paid on partial completion; post-study access to the full system for the control arm; pre-specified adjustment strategy (required for *With Reservations* at high attrition) |
| **Self-selection** | Every voluntary study | Report the funnel completely (invited → consented → eligible → randomised → analysed), as both Duolingo studies do. Do not claim market-level generalisability |
| **Hawthorne / novelty** | 8-week endpoints land in the documented novelty-decay trough (onset ~4 weeks) | 12-week secondary endpoint; equal-attention active control; report both endpoints |
| **No blinding** | All designs | Automated identical outcome administration; analyst blinded to arm labels; estimand honestly stated as "effect of being given the system" |
| **Endogenous outcome** | Any in-app measure | Sequestered held-out item set. This is the only real fix |

## What you can honestly claim, by evidence tier

Mapping reachable designs to defensible public claims. ESSA tiers are the shared vocabulary even though ESSA is a K-12 procurement rule that does not bind a consumer product. **Not legal advice — advertising-substantiation rules are a separate question I did not research; have counsel review any comparative claim before it ships.**

| Study you can actually run | Formal tier | **You may say** | **You may not say** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Nothing yet** — logic model + citations to the component literature | ESSA **Tier 4** ("Demonstrates a Rationale"). Requires a well-specified logic model based on rigorous research *and* a study planned or under way | "Built on established findings in spaced retrieval practice and knowledge tracing." "Designed around evidence-based learning science." Publish the logic model and cite the primary meta-analyses | Anything with a number attached to *your* product. No "students improve X%." No comparison to any competitor |
| **Stage 1** — one-week randomised mechanism experiment, delayed endpoint, paid panel, pre-registered | Below Tier 3 formally (single-site, small, short, not the whole product). Genuine peer-reviewable evidence about a *mechanism* | "In a pre-registered randomised experiment, our scheduling rule produced better one-week retention than a fixed-interval schedule (d = X, 95% CI)." Name the mechanism, the delay, the N | "Our app raises MCAT scores." "Better than Anki." Do not generalise a one-week panel result to a 4-month exam preparation |
| **Stage 1 × 3–5 replicated mechanisms** | Still not an ESSA tier, but a real internal evidence base | "Each of the following components has been individually tested in randomised experiments on our platform: [list, with effect sizes and CIs, nulls included]" | Anything about the *integration*. Component evidence does not aggregate into a system claim |
| **Stage 2** — internal-baseline RCT, n ≈ 150/arm, sequestered outcome, pre-registered, ITT primary | Likely **Tier 3 / borderline Tier 2**. Falls short of Tier 1–2 on multi-site (single platform) and near-certainly on WWC low-attrition | "In a randomised trial of N learners, those using the integrated scheduler scored X SD higher on an independently-blueprinted assessment of MCAT content that the system never used for teaching, than learners using a conventional plan." | **Stage 2 with a positive result and full disclosure** | Tier 3, credibly reported | "The only MCAT-prep product with a published randomised trial using an independent outcome measure" — *if* you publish it in full, including the funnel and any nulls. Given that the category has **no** RCTs, this is a genuine and defensible differentiator | Do not imply peer review you do not have. ## DOK 1 — established facts

1. **n per group = 2(z_{1−α/2} + z_{1−β})²/d²**; at α = .05 two-sided and 80% power the numerator constant is **15.6978**, so n = 15.6978/d². Exact noncentral-t values: **394 / 64 / 26** per arm for d = 0.20 / 0.50 / 0.80. **[A]**
2. **ANCOVA design factor is (1 − r²)**: multiply the t-test n by (1 − r²) and add one, where r is the pretest–posttest correlation. **[A]**
3. **Cluster design effect is 1 + (m − 1)·ICC**; at ICC = 0.05, m = 25 this is 2.20×. **[A]**
4. **Kraft (2020)**: from 1,942 effect sizes across 747 RCTs of education interventions with standardised achievement outcomes, the **median is 0.10 SD**; proposed benchmarks are <0.05 Small, 0.05–<0.20 Medium, **≥0.20 Large**. US-DoE-funded pre-registered RCTs have a median of **0.03 SD** (139 ES, 49 RCTs). Broad measures median 0.10 vs. narrow 0.17. **[A]**
5. **Kulik & Fletcher (2016)**: median ITS effect **0.66 SD** across 50 controlled evaluations vs. conventional instruction — but effects were **small** in evaluations with non-conventional control groups or flawed implementation. **[A]**
6. **Cheung & Slavin (2016)**: across 645 studies, effect sizes were **roughly twice as large** for experimenter-made vs. independent measures, for small-scale vs. large-scale trials, and for published vs. unpublished reports; and significantly higher in QEDs than RCTs. **[A]**
7. **Prentice's four criteria** define a statistical surrogate; criterion 4 (treatment effect fully captured by the surrogate) is the demanding one and is rarely met. **Freedman's PTE is not bounded in [0,1]** and is unstable when the treatment effect is small. Modern practice requires **trial-level R²** (conventionally ≥0.7). The **surrogate paradox** is a documented failure mode. **[A]**
8. **WWC v5.0 attrition boundaries** (Table C.1): at 20% overall attrition, max differential is 5.4 pp (cautious) / 10.0 pp (optimistic); at 40%, 2.6 / 6.0 pp; at 50%, 1.0 / 3.7 pp; **at ≥58% overall, no differential rate yields low attrition under the cautious boundary**. Cautious is the default when a team cannot defensibly choose, and typically applies to postsecondary and outside-school-hours interventions. **[A]**
9. **WWC v5.0** raised the SCD baseline requirement to **six data points** in initial baseline phases (from five) for *Without Reservations*, requires **three demonstrations at three different time points**, and requires **≥20 cases** across studies for an intervention report. SCDs are the only comparison-group-free designs eligible for WWC review. **[A]**
10. **ESSA Tiers 1 and 2** both require a multi-site sample of **≥350 students across sites**; Tier 1 requires an experimental study meeting WWC standards **without reservations** plus population *and* setting overlap; Tier 3 permits correlational designs with controls for selection bias; Tier 4 requires a logic model plus a planned or ongoing study. **[A]**
11. **Roschelle et al. (2016), *AERA Open***: cluster RCT, 43 schools, **2,850** 7th-graders, TerraNova outcome, **g = 0.18** (0.22 in a three-level re-analysis, 95% CI [0.15, 0.30]); **0.29** for low-prior-achievement students. IES-funded. **[A, abstract + the follow-up effect-size report]**
12. **Pane et al. (2014), *EEPA***: matched-pair cluster RCT, ~20,000 students, 146 schools, 7 states. **No effect in year 1**; **~0.20 SD in year 2**, significant for high schools, not middle schools. **[A, abstract + ResearchGate text]**
13. **Duolingo Video Call RCT (DRR-25-06, 2025)**: **57,254** invitation emails → **658** randomised (stratified on pre-test quartiles) → **567** analysed (304 control / 263 treatment) = 13.8% post-randomisation loss against a **42%** planning assumption. 30-day intervention, Versant by Pearson outcome, $20 + official score report compensation, control arm given the paid feature afterwards for parity. Post-test speaking: 24.32 (SD 10.60) vs. 25.26 (SD 10.94). **[A, read in full]**
14. **Jiang et al. (2021)**: recruitment funnel of **2,175 emails → 296 surveys → 171 qualified → 135 participants** (6.21%); $100/participant; ACTFL tests via LTI; **no control group**. Companion whitepaper: n = 340, **median 203 hours** to complete seven units (IQR 114–332), and **no significant positive correlation between hours and proficiency** (negative in Spanish). **[A]**
15. **Khan Academy / College Board Official SAT Practice**: observational, ~250,000 test-takers; 6 h associated with **+21 points (ES 0.11)**; 6 h + one best-practice behaviour with **+39 points (ES 0.20)**; marketed headline "20 hours → 115 points." Co-authored by Khan Academy and College Board. **[A for the figures; the design is correlational]**
16. **Spacing and retrieval effect sizes**: spaced vs. massed **retrieval** g = 0.74 (29 studies); **expanding vs. uniform spacing g = 0.034**, ns, I² = 0% (54 ES, 16 studies); distributed vs. massed in **classroom** settings d = 0.54, 95% CI [0.31, 0.77] (22 reports, 31 ES, N > 3,000), with larger effects at longer retention intervals; retrieval vs. restudy g ≈ 0.50, rising to ≈0.73 with feedback. **[A; the g = 0.50/0.73 figures come via a peer-reviewed meta-analysis citing Rowland 2014, which I did not read directly]**
17. **Self-reported test scores**: self-reported vs. actual SAT-Total r ≈ **.82**; mean over-report ≈ **25 points, d = 0.31**; 39% over-report, 51% accurate, 10% under-report; over-reporting greatest among **low scorers**. Only ~36% of SAT-Total scores reported exactly. **[B]**
18. **Test-prep coaching effects are small**: SAT-V **0.09 SD**, SAT-M **0.16 SD** in published comparison studies; controlled meta-analyses estimate 9–15 points verbal and 15–18 points math; one experimental meta-analysis of test preparation overall found **g = 0.26**. Studies comparing coached gains to national norms yield effects **4–5× larger** than matched or randomised evaluations. **[A]**
19. **Clinical equipoise** (Freedman 1987): satisfied by genuine uncertainty **within the expert community**, not the individual investigator; the underlying norm is that no participant is randomised to something the expert community believes inferior to standard care. **[A, secondary]**
20. **Facebook emotional contagion**: 689,003 users' feeds manipulated; Facebook, as a private entity without federal research funds, was **exempt from the Common Rule**; Cornell's IRB reviewed only the analysis as a "pre-existing dataset"; **no institution reviewed the manipulation**. The ToS argument "conflated legal permission with ethical consent." **[A]**
21. **Item exposure**: once an item is overexposed, "the probability of a correct response no longer depends on the examinee's ability level, but rather on prior familiarity with the item." Exposure control is **preventive** and distinct from item **compromise**, which it does not remedy. **[A]**
22. **Duolingo experimentation scale**: ~100s of concurrent A/B tests, ~30 new per week, ~500 per quarter, 16,000 lifetime; results assessed across engagement, monetisation, and **learning outcomes**; **guardrail metrics** cannot be harmed without senior review and deliberately exclude monetisation. **[B, company/press sources]**
23. **Prolific pricing**: absolute minimum **£6/$8 per hour**, recommended **£9/$12 per hour**, with the effective rate computed from **median actual** completion time. **[A]**
24. **Novelty effect timing**: in a 14-week longitudinal study, gamification effects began decaying after **four weeks**, decay lasted two to six weeks and reduced a moderate effect to null in the worst case; a familiarisation recovery began at 6–10 weeks but did not restore the original effect. **[B, single study, gamification]**
25. **Longitudinal online RCT attrition**: in a trial of >13,000 prospective homeowners invited to up to seven biweekly surveys over ~14 weeks, **60% completed** the study (40% attrition), and attrition occurred primarily **soon after enrolment**. MOOCs: ~22% watched ≥20% of lectures and **<6% earned certificates**. **[B]**
26. **Stepped-wedge**: sample-size calculation must absorb calendar-time confounding, which degrades precision; the design is **less** powerful per measurement than a parallel design at low ICC and more powerful at high ICC or large clusters; unblinded individual-level data collection creates differential-selection risk. **[A]**
27. **The ed-tech evidence base is thin**: ~**85%** of the 2,135 products in the ISTE EdTech Index hold **no** evidence-based certification of any kind (~40% among the ~100 most-used US K-12 tools). **[B]**
28. **No RCTs compare commercial medical-education question banks.** The literature is retrospective cohorts and surveys: total questions completed r = 0.30 with USMLE Step 1; USMLE-targeted banks r = 0.38; ~1 Step 1 point per **445 practice questions** or per **1,700 unique Anki flashcards**. A systematic review notes the apparent dose-response "may partly reflect self-selection." **[B]**

## DOK 2 — insights and implications

1. **The single most consequential number in this document is not a sample size — it is Kulik & Fletcher's contrast between 0.66 SD against conventional instruction and "small" against non-conventional controls.** The claim on the table is explicitly framed against three *strong* comparators. That framing choice, made in a sentence, multiplies the required sample size by roughly 20×. **If the team wants a study it can afford, the lever is not the budget, it is the comparator.**
2. **The delayed-outcome problem is unsolvable as stated and dissolvable as reframed.** No surrogate for MCAT score can be validated here, because validation requires trials measuring both endpoints and this team will never observe the true endpoint under randomisation. But nothing forces the outcome to be a *surrogate*. A sequestered, blueprint-aligned, independently-authored item set is a legitimate **outcome of record**, defensible on construct-validity grounds. The reframe converts an impossible task into a merely expensive one — and it changes the marketing claim from "raises MCAT scores" (indefensible) to "produces higher performance on MCAT-blueprinted content the system never taught to" (defensible).
3. **Endogeneity, not power, is the deepest measurement problem — and it is the one with a clean engineering fix.** Every in-app metric is computed over items the treatment selected, and BKT mastery is the treatment's own opinion of itself. Power problems are solved with money you do not have; endogeneity is solved with an architectural boundary you can build in a sprint. **The 300-item sequestration is the highest-leverage action available, and its cost rises monotonically with delay** — every item served to a learner is permanently burned.
4. **The 140-item diagnostic is a power asset that is currently being under-valued.** At r = .70 it halves the required sample; at r = .80 it cuts it by 64%. The Duolingo reality-check suggests their trial's feasibility rested largely on high pretest precision. **Empirically estimating the diagnostic-to-held-out correlation should be a Stage 0 deliverable**, because every downstream sample-size number in this document is a linear function of (1 − r²) and r is currently my least-supported assumption.
5. **Component-wise evaluation wins on an argument the team may not have made explicitly: power scales with d², and components have effects 3–5× larger than integrations.** A mechanism at d = 0.5 needs 64/arm; a system at d = 0.15 needs 699/arm. That 11× cost difference, multiplied by the fact that a component result is *diagnostic* and a system null is not, makes the sequencing decision straightforward. But the counter-argument is real and should be conceded in writing: components cannot test an integration claim, and integration failure — five good mechanisms competing over one time budget — is invisible at the component level. **The resolution is sequence, not substitution.**
6. **The WWC attrition cliff and realistic consumer attrition are in direct, unresolvable conflict, and this should be planned for rather than discovered.** At ≥58% overall attrition no differential rate qualifies as low under the cautious boundary — which the Handbook says typically applies to postsecondary, outside-school-hours interventions. A voluntary months-long consumer study will plausibly land there. **A WWC "without reservations" rating is therefore likely unreachable on attrition grounds alone**, independent of budget. The productive response is to pre-specify the adjustment strategy that v5.0 accepts for *With Reservations*, and to stop treating Tier 1 as a goal.
7. **Blinding's impossibility interacts badly with your natural endpoint, and the fix is a longer delay rather than a bigger N.** Novelty decay starts at ~4 weeks; an 8-week endpoint sits in the trough. Meanwhile retention effects — the thing your architecture is actually for — grow with the retention interval. These point the same way: **the 12-week endpoint is the more honest primary, and buying delay is cheaper than buying sample size.**
8. **A competitor head-to-head fails on four independent grounds, which is unusual and decisive.** Any one of ToS exposure, absent equipoise, undefinable dosage equivalence, or comparator cost exceeding the study budget would be enough to defer it. All four holding simultaneously means this is not a "later, with more money" item — it is a **structurally bad design** for a small challenger, and the internal active-control arm captures nearly all the inferential value at a fraction of the risk.
9. **"Per hour" is the most dangerous phrase in the claim.** It is a ratio estimand, undefined at zero dose, unstable at low dose, and Duolingo's own data shows time-on-task barely predicting outcomes *within* a single product. The claim must be re-specified as either equal-dose-by-design (test the outcome) or slope comparison (descriptive only) **before** data collection. Choosing after seeing the data is the fastest route to a result nobody believes.
10. **The category's total absence of randomised evidence is the strategic opportunity, and its own market signal is the warning.** No RCTs compare medical-education question banks, and 85% of ed-tech products carry no evidence at all. **One competent Stage 2 RCT with an independent outcome measure would make this the best-evidenced product in MCAT prep** — a real, defensible differentiator. But the same facts show the market has not been rewarding evidence, so the investment must be justified as credibility-and-truth infrastructure rather than as a growth lever with a modelled return.
11. **Vendor-funding is survivable; design choices determine whether it is disqualifying.** Duolingo's vendor-funded, non-peer-reviewed RCT is more credible than Khan's 250,000-participant observational study, because it randomised and used an independent instrument. **Independence of the *measure* buys more credibility than independence of the *funder*, and it is far cheaper.** Licensing a third-party assessment, or authoring the held-out set under blueprint discipline with a documented freeze, is the affordable version of independence.
12. **The 2,108-item bank is smaller than it looks once measurement is accounted for.** 300 items sequestered (14%) plus reserve for leak replacement, against 422 KCs, means **KC-level held-out scoring is arithmetically impossible** (<1 item/KC) and the held-out instrument must be blueprinted at the 10-Foundational-Concept level. This is a real constraint on what "time-to-mastery per knowledge component" claims can ever be supported by non-endogenous data, and it argues for prioritising item generation capacity as a *measurement* investment, not only a content one.
13. **Adopt the experimentation discipline now even though the scale is absent.** Duolingo's guardrail-metric practice — a protected set of metrics that growth cannot override, deliberately excluding monetisation — is free to institute at zero users and nearly impossible to retrofit at scale. For an exam-prep product the guardrail must be delayed retention, and its owner must not be the person who owns growth.
14. **Equipoise and study quality happen to align here, which is rare and should be exploited.** The internal-baseline design (integrated scheduler vs. sensible defaults, same content, equal polish) is simultaneously the most informative available design *and* the only one where you can honestly tell a high-stakes candidate that nobody knows which arm is better. Designs that fail the ethics test — degraded controls, competitor arms — also fail the inference test. **When those two signals agree this strongly, treat it as confirmation that the internal-baseline RCT is the right target rather than a compromise.**

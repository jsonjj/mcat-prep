# Comparators and the Bar: Practice Tests, Interleaving, Competitor Claims, and Where the Advantage Actually Is

Scope: literature review answering four questions — (1) test-enhanced learning and high-volume question-bank practice as exam prep, (2) interleaving effect sizes and failure modes, (3) credibility of competitor efficacy claims, (4) which dimension of advantage is actually defensible and measurable. Sibling reviews already established the target effect size against strong active controls (d ≈ 0.10–0.20), the Doroudi/Aleven/Brunskill null result for adaptive sequencing of interdependent content, the Duolingo half-life-regression misreading, the absence of any FSRS learning-outcome RCT, and the impracticality of whole-system evaluation. Those are not re-derived here.

---

## Headline

**The realistically available advantage is on the efficiency dimension — equal exam outcome for materially less learner time — not on learning rate. It is the only claim with a plausible mechanism, a credible effect magnitude, an existing precedent in the tutoring-systems literature, and a measurement path that a pre-launch team can actually execute.**

The reasoning, compressed:

1. **The learning-rate ceiling against an active comparator is near zero, and this is now directly measured, not inferred.** Yang et al. (2021, *Psychological Bulletin*), the largest classroom meta-analysis of test-enhanced learning (222 studies, 48,478 students), reports the testing effect as **g = 0.610 against no-activity/filler controls, g = 0.330 against restudying, and g = 0.095, 95% CI [−0.005, 0.194], p = .062 — not statistically significant — against other elaborative strategies.** The active-control penalty is not a theoretical caveat; it is the empirical result. A diligent Anki-plus-question-bank user *is* the elaborative-strategy control condition. Beating them on learning rate means winning a comparison where the field's best-evidenced mechanism has a confidence interval spanning zero.

2. **The two mechanisms the system relies on most are already saturated in the comparator.** Retrieval practice and spacing are not this product's differentiators — they are Anki's and UWorld's. Every counterfactual learner in the target market already does thousands of retrieval trials with spacing. What remains proprietary is *scheduling, sequencing, selection, and orchestration*, and the sibling review's Doroudi et al. finding is that adaptive sequencing of interdependent content has zero clean wins across eight studies. The mechanisms with large effect sizes are the ones the comparator shares; the mechanisms that are proprietary are the ones with null results.

3. **The market's own causal record is a null, and it is a well-powered null.** Werner & Bull (2003, *Medical Education*): 468 students, commercial 3–4 week Step 1 coaching courses (n = 99) versus self-study (n = 369), regression of actual Step 1 on NBME-CBSE-predicted score. The regression lines were statistically indistinguishable, in an analysis powered to detect a difference of ~1% — about 2 questions out of 350. McGaghie et al. (2004, *Teaching and Learning in Medicine*) systematically reviewed commercial test prep in medical education and concluded the incremental validity, "if any, is extremely small," with supporting evidence "weak or nonexistent." SAT coaching, the most-studied analogue, converges: Becker's (1990) meta-analysis of published comparison-group studies found coached groups exceeded controls by **0.09 SD on SAT-Verbal and 0.16 SD on SAT-Math** — landing precisely in the sibling review's 0.10–0.20 band, which is reassuring for calibration and sobering for ambition.

4. **Efficiency, by contrast, has the largest and most replicated advantage in the entire adjacent literature.** The LISP Tutor evaluations (Anderson, Conrad & Corbett, 1989, *Cognitive Science*) found students completed identical exercises in **one-third to three-quarters the time** of controls while scoring **at least as well**, and in one study about a letter grade higher. The ITS literature's honest summary of its own best case is "the same level of proficiency as conventional instruction in one third the time." The pattern across that literature is consistent: time savings are large and robust; achievement gains are smaller and comparison-dependent. That is the same structural shape as this product's plausible advantage.

5. **Efficiency is also the only dimension this team can measure at pre-launch scale.** Time-to-mastery is a continuous, high-frequency, within-subject-measurable outcome available from instrumentation the system already needs. An exam-score effect of d = 0.15 requires roughly 1,400 learners for 80% power; a 20% reduction in time-to-criterion on a knowledge-component-level outcome is detectable with two to three orders of magnitude fewer subjects, because the outcome is measured per component rather than once per learner per lifetime.

**What this means for the three claims the team wants to make.** Against practice tests alone (a): defensible, because full-length-tests-only prep lacks a spacing and error-remediation mechanism, though note that this control barely exists in the wild — 89.7% of AAMC Post-MCAT Questionnaire respondents already took timed practice exams *and* used other resources. Against Anki-style spaced repetition alone (b): **very hard, and the honest position is that a diligent Anki user with a good deck and a question bank is close to the practical ceiling of self-study.** The best available claim against them is coverage and coordination, not learning rate. Against other adaptive prep products (c): **easy to claim and nearly impossible to substantiate**, because no competitor has published a credible causal estimate — which means there is no benchmark to beat, and also no benchmark to hide behind if challenged.

**Two hard numbers to internalize before writing marketing copy.** The AAMC's own retest data: the median score gain from first to second MCAT attempt is **+2 to +4 total points** for first-attempt scores of 472–517, and **0 to +1 points** for 518–528. That is the observed gain from *everything a motivated retaker does* — more study, more materials, more time, plus practice effects and regression to the mean. Meanwhile Kaplan and Princeton Review both advertise a **"515+ or +15 points"** guarantee. The guaranteed increment is roughly four to seven times the population median retest gain. That gap is not evidence the products work; it is evidence about how the guarantee is engineered — measured from a proctored in-house diagnostic rather than an official score, conditioned on completing prescribed work, and remedied with a refund or free retake rather than a score.

## Practice tests alone

### The core effect: strong, but the number depends entirely on what you compare to

Three meta-analyses anchor this. Every one of them finds that the effect size is dominated by the choice of control condition, and all three authors say so explicitly.

**Rowland (2014, *Psychological Bulletin*)** — 61 laboratory studies, 159 effect sizes, 1975–2013. Testing versus **restudy** of the same material, final retention test.
- Overall **g = 0.50**; 81% of comparisons favored testing.
- Retention interval **< 1 day: g = 0.41 [0.31, 0.51]**, k = 103. Retention interval **≥ 1 day: g = 0.69 [0.56, 0.81]**, k = 56. Heterogeneity between levels significant.
- Feedback provided **g = 0.73 [0.61, 0.86]** versus no feedback **g = 0.39 [0.29, 0.49]**.
- On a "high-exposure" subset (feedback given or initial test accuracy > 75%, which removes the bias against the test condition from unreexposed failed retrievals): < 1 day **g = 0.58**, ≥ 1 day **g = 0.78**.

**Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan (2017, *Review of Educational Research*)** — 118 articles, 272 independent effect sizes, N = 15,427.
- Overall fixed-effects **g = 0.61** (random-effects g = 0.70).
- Versus **restudying: g = 0.51** (k = 195). Versus **filler activity or no activity: g = 0.93** (k = 58).
- **Multiple-choice practice tests g = 0.70**; short-answer g = 0.48; mixed formats g = 0.80. Practice/final format identical g = 0.63 versus dissimilar g = 0.53.
- Retention interval **< 1 day g = 0.56** (the majority of studies, k = 117); **1–6 days g = 0.82** (k = 58). Between-levels difference significant, Q_B(4) = 69.07, p < .001.
- Single practice test **g = 0.70** versus two or more **g = 0.51**.
- Classroom **g = 0.67** (k = 30) versus laboratory **g = 0.62** (k = 223) — the effect survives leaving the lab.
- High implementation fidelity **g = 0.65** versus low fidelity **g = 0.46**.
- **The authors' own verdict on which number to use:** because the no-activity comparison confounds testing with re-exposure, "the effect size of g = 0.51 is arguably the most accurate" estimate of the testing effect. Cite 0.51, not 0.61 or 0.93.

**Yang, Luo, Vadillo, Yu & Shanks (2021, *Psychological Bulletin*)** — 222 independent studies in authentic classrooms, 48,478 students. This is the most externally valid of the three and the most important for this product.
- Overall **g = 0.499 [0.442, 0.557]**; median g = 0.446. Right-skewed; ~82% of effects positive, ~6% null.
- **Control-condition moderator, Q(3) = 153, p < .001:**
  - versus no activity / filler: **g = 0.610 [0.547, 0.673]**
  - versus being tested on **fewer** questions: **g = 0.465**
  - versus **restudying**: **g = 0.330 [0.256, …]**
  - versus **other elaborative strategies** (concept mapping, note-taking, summarizing): **g = 0.095 [−0.005, 0.194], p = .062**
- Corrective feedback **g = 0.537** versus none **g = 0.374**. Matched test format **g = 0.531** versus mismatched **g = 0.399**. Instructor consistency: no significant modulation.

**That g = 0.095 is the single most decision-relevant number in this review.** It is the testing effect measured against learners who were already doing something cognitively active. It is not significant. The MCAT market's counterfactual learner is doing something active — thousands of UWorld-style questions and Anki reviews. The system's practice engine is competing in the g ≈ 0.095 regime, not the g ≈ 0.61 regime.

**Reading across the three:** the testing effect is genuinely one of the best-replicated findings in learning science (Tier A), it survives the move to classrooms, it grows with retention interval, and it is substantially amplified by corrective feedback and format match. Those last three facts do favor design choices this system makes — long horizons (2–6 months), feedback on every item, MCAT-format four-option items. But the effect is measured against *inactive* controls, and it shrinks toward zero against active ones. Nothing here licenses a claim of superiority over another retrieval-based product.

### The applied question: does high-volume question-bank practice cause higher exam scores?

This is where the prep-industry literature is at its worst, so the correlational/causal separation has to be done explicitly.

#### What is correlational

**Deng, Gluckstein & Larsen (2015, *Perspectives on Medical Education*)** is the best-executed study in this genre and the most-cited "question volume works" source. N = 72 students at one institution, survey-reported resource use, multivariate regression on Step 1 score.
- All students used practice MCQs; mean **3,870** questions (SD 1,472).
- Independent predictors: MCQs completed (B = 2.2 × 10⁻³, p < .001), unique Anki cards seen (B = 5.9 × 10⁻⁴, p = .024), second-year honours (B = 1.198, p = .002), MCAT score (B = 1.078, p = .003). Test anxiety was a significant *negative* predictor (B = −1.986, p < .001). Firecracker (commercial flashcards) did **not** predict score.
- Translated: **each additional ~445 boards-style questions, or ~1,700 unique Anki cards, was associated with one additional Step 1 point**, controlling for MCAT, preclinical grades, and psychological factors.

Three things about this. First, it is the most useful number in the applied literature, because it gives a *slope* rather than a dichotomy, and because the authors adjusted for prior ability (MCAT) and prior achievement (honours) — which is more than almost anyone else does. Second, it is still correlational, N = 72, single site, self-reported exposure. Third, and most important: **read as a causal slope it is brutal about the returns to volume.** 445 questions for one point. A 15-point gain would require ~6,700 additional questions on top of the 3,870 mean. On a system with 2,108 items — roughly half the mean question count of this cohort — the volume lever is arithmetically small even if you grant the slope full causal status, which you should not.

Other correlational findings, all with the same confounding structure:
- **UWorld percent-correct correlates r = 0.62 with Step 1** (N = 81, Cureus predictive model). In the same model, **NBME CBSE r = 0.71**, MCAT r = 0.18 (ns), and **days studied r = 0.02, p = .78 (ns)**. The null for days-studied inside a model where question performance predicts strongly is a clean illustration of the confound: what predicts is *how well you answer*, which is ability, not *how long you sat there*.
- **N = 164 at West Virginia University**: UWorld users M = 229 (SE 1.4) versus non-users M = 215 (SE 4.2). In the same study, **commercial preparation courses were not associated with improved scores**. Note the group sizes implied by the standard errors — the non-user group is small and unusual, since not using the dominant question bank is itself a marker.
- **Anki**: a 2026 systematic review in *Medical Science Educator* found three studies with consistent positive associations between regular Anki use and Step 1, high-frequency users outperforming minimal users by **4–13 points** (Lu et al.: 241.1 ± 13.2 versus 235.5 ± 17.7, p = .012; Wothe et al.: median 238 versus 233.5, p = .039; Deng et al.: dose-response). Only one study looked at Step 2 CK and found **no** significant benefit. Evidence for university-administered exams was mixed. *(Abstract and summary text only.)*

**The confounding is not incidental, it is structural.** Question-bank volume is a *behavioral output* of exactly the traits that cause high scores: baseline knowledge, conscientiousness, available study time, financial capacity to buy the bank, and low test anxiety. Deng et al.'s own model shows test anxiety predicting score negatively — anxiety also suppresses question volume, so volume partly proxies for equanimity. Adjusting for MCAT and preclinical grades removes some ability confounding and none of the motivation confounding. And there is a reverse-causal channel: students who find questions rewarding because they are getting them right do more of them.

Selection is also severe in the other direction. In one Step 2 cohort, **UWorld was used by 98.1% of participants**. When a resource is universal, the non-users are a residual category — people who could not afford it, ran out of time, or were struggling — and any comparison against them is a comparison against adversity, not against a control condition.

#### What is causal, or close to it

Very little, and what exists is unflattering to the industry.

- **Werner & Bull (2003, *Medical Education*)**: 468 students; 99 took a 3–4 week live commercial coaching course, 369 self-studied. Actual Step 1 regressed on NBME-CBSE-predicted Step 1. **Regression lines statistically indistinguishable**, in an analysis the authors state was powered to detect ~1% ≈ 2 of 350 questions. No benefit for above-average or below-average CBSE performers. This is not randomized, but conditioning on an NBME-authored predicted score is a strong prognostic adjustment, and the power statement makes the null informative rather than merely inconclusive. **Tier B, and the most decision-relevant causal-ish estimate in the medical prep market.** *(Abstract only.)*
- **McGaghie et al. (2004, *Teaching and Learning in Medicine* 16(2))**: systematic review of commercial test prep for MCAT, NBME Part 1, and USMLE Step 1, including grey literature. Conclusion: research "lacks control and rigor"; incremental validity "extremely small"; evidence "weak or nonexistent"; no cost-benefit analyses exist; almost no details of course content are ever reported. *(Abstract only.)*
- **Spaced repetition in medical education**: a 2026 *The Clinical Teacher* systematic review and meta-analysis (14 studies, 13 meta-analyzed, **21,415 learners**) reports **SMD = 0.78 [0.56, 0.99], p < .0001** for spaced repetition versus "standard studying techniques." This is the largest quantitative estimate available in this domain and it is tempting to lean on. Do not lean hard: the comparator is heterogeneous and vaguely specified ("standard studying techniques" spans no-intervention, massed study, and business-as-usual), the interventions pool faculty-made flashcards, third-party decks, emailed MCQs, and classroom quizzes, and I read only the abstract. It is best read as confirming that spacing beats *unstructured* study — a g ≈ 0.61-style comparison, not a g ≈ 0.095-style one. **Tier B/C.**
- **No RCT of high-volume question-bank practice versus an equal-time active alternative exists in this market**, as far as I could find. This is the central evidentiary hole and it is not this team's fault.

#### What this means for claim (a), "beats practice tests alone"

Defensible in principle but weaker than it looks, for three reasons.

First, the control barely exists. AAMC Post-MCAT Questionnaire data show **85.4%** of respondents used Official MCAT Practice Exams, **72.6%** used commercial prep books, and **89.7%** took a timed online practice exam mimicking test day. Practice-test-only prep is not the counterfactual; multi-resource prep is. A trial against "practice tests alone" would be a trial against a straw comparator, and sophisticated readers will say so.

Second, the plausible mechanism for winning is not the testing effect — the comparator gets that too — but **spacing, error remediation, and coverage**. Full-length practice tests deliver retrieval but distribute it by test-blueprint frequency, not by the learner's forgetting curve, and they surface errors without scheduling their re-exposure. That is a real gap and it is the honest basis for the claim.

Third, the retention-interval moderators are on your side and should be stated as such: testing effects are larger at ≥ 1 day than < 1 day (Rowland: 0.69 versus 0.41), larger at 1–6 days than < 1 day (Adesope: 0.82 versus 0.56), larger with corrective feedback (Rowland: 0.73 versus 0.39; Yang: 0.537 versus 0.374), and larger with format match (Yang: 0.531 versus 0.399). A 2–6 month, feedback-rich, MCAT-format system sits in the favorable cell of every one of those moderators. That is a legitimate design-quality argument. It is not an effect size.

## Interleaving

### The headline number and why it does not transfer to this system

**Brunmair & Richter (2019, *Psychological Bulletin*)** — 59 papers, 238 effect sizes nested in 158 samples, N = 8,466. Interleaved versus blocked presentation, final test typically immediate or short-delay.
- Overall **g = 0.42 [0.34, 0.50]**; g = 0.43 [0.35, 0.51] restricted to independent effects.
- By material: **paintings g = 0.67** and other visual materials best; **mathematical tasks g = 0.34**; **expository texts and tastes non-significant**; **words g = −0.39 — blocking beat interleaving.**
- Metaregression: interleaving effects are stronger when material is **more similar between categories**, **less similar within categories**, and **more complex**.
- Authors' conclusion: interleaving "can effectively foster inductive learning but the setting and type of learning material must be considered," and "should be used with caution in certain conditions, especially for expository texts and words."

**Rohrer, Dedrick, Hartwig & Cheung (2020, *Journal of Educational Psychology*)** is the strongest applied demonstration and worth stating precisely, because it is the best-case number people will quote at you. Preregistered cluster RCT, **54 grade-7 mathematics classes, 15 teachers, 4 months**. Both arms received **the same practice problems**; only the order differed (interleaved arms arranged so no two consecutive problems share a strategy). Both then completed an interleaved review assignment. **One month later, an unannounced test: interleaved 61% versus blocked 38%, d = 0.83 [0.68, 0.97]**, positive for all 15 teachers (per-teacher ds 0.23–1.48).

That is a large, well-designed, preregistered effect on a delayed unannounced test with time-on-task held constant — genuinely impressive. But notice what was manipulated: **which discrimination the learner must make on each problem.** Blocked practice tells the student the strategy by context; interleaved practice forces strategy *selection*. The effect is about learning to discriminate between confusable problem types.

### The boundary conditions, which are the operative part here

The mechanism literature is now fairly clear that there is **no globally optimal sequence**, and that the moderator is category structure:

- **Carvalho & Goldstone (2013, *Memory & Cognition*)**: interleaving produces better generalization for categories with **high** within- and between-category similarity; **blocked presentation produces better generalization for categories with low within- and between-category similarity.** Interleaving emphasizes differences *between* categories; blocking emphasizes discovery of commonalities *within* one.
- The **discriminative-contrast** versus **commonality-detection** framing (Sequential Attention Theory; Carvalho & Goldstone 2015/2017): blocking wins when the hard part is finding what exemplars of one concept share; interleaving wins when the hard part is telling two confusable concepts apart.
- **Noh et al. (2016, *Cognition*)**: schedule × category-structure interaction — **rule-based** category learning benefited from **blocking**; information-integration category learning benefited from interleaving. Held with both simple and complex stimuli. *(Abstract only.)*
- Learners' metacognition points the wrong way: they judge blocking to be more effective than interleaving, and restudy more effective than testing, even when the reverse holds. Yang et al. (2021) summarize Kirk-Johnson, Galla & Fraundorf (2019): a majority chose to restudy rather than test, and those who chose testing recalled better. *(I read this via Yang et al.'s account, not the original.)*

### What this means for the scheduler

**The scheduler enforces activity variety. Activity variety is not interleaving in the technical sense, and the g = 0.42 / d = 0.83 numbers do not transfer to it.** This distinction is load-bearing and easy to get wrong:

- Interleaving in this literature means **alternating exemplars of confusable categories within a single inductive-learning task**, so that each trial demands a discrimination. The effect arises from the discrimination.
- Alternating a lesson, then flashcards, then a mistake-review block is **task alternation across activity types**. Nothing in Brunmair & Richter or Rohrer et al. speaks to that. Its plausible benefits (attention, fatigue management, engagement) are real but are a different literature with different and weaker evidence.

Where the system *does* have a genuine interleaving mechanism is **adaptive item selection mixing knowledge components within a practice block**, and there the boundary conditions are directly actionable:

1. **Interleave confusable KCs; block on introduction.** With 422 knowledge components, many pairs are high-between-similarity and confusable — amino acid properties, hormone axes, acid-base cases, reasoning-question archetypes. Those are the high-similarity cell where interleaving wins. Conversely, when a learner is first acquiring a KC whose exemplars look superficially unalike, blocking helps them find the commonality. A defensible policy is blocked on first acquisition, interleaved once a minimum competence threshold is reached — and that policy is itself a clean A/B test.
2. **Expect nothing, or harm, from interleaving prose-heavy material.** Expository texts were non-significant and word materials were **negative (g = −0.39)** in Brunmair & Richter. CARS passages are expository prose. Interleaving passage-level reading practice has no meta-analytic support and one adjacent negative signal.
3. **Interleaving degrades in-session performance while improving delayed retention.** This is the classic desirable-difficulty signature and it has a product consequence the team should plan for: interleaved schedules will make the dashboard look worse and learners feel worse in the moment. Given that learners systematically mis-attribute effort to poor learning, a mechanism that raises delayed retention while lowering in-session accuracy is an adherence risk. If the system also targets differentiated success rates, note that interleaving and success-rate targeting push in opposite directions on observed accuracy, and the controller needs to know which signal it is reacting to.
4. **Interleaving is cheap and time-neutral, which makes it a good bet regardless.** Rohrer et al. held problem sets identical and changed only order. That is close to free. Even a small expected effect has excellent cost-effectiveness, and unlike most mechanisms here it is testable within-product at low cost. It is a good candidate for an early mechanism-level experiment — with the outcome measured on a **delayed** assessment, since the effect is defined by delayed retention.

## Competitor claims and the market bar

### What is actually claimed

**Kaplan MCAT** advertises: score **515+ or increase by 15+ points**, depending on baseline — if baseline ≥ 500, guaranteed 515+; if baseline < 500, guaranteed +15. Conditions: complete the recommended work, test **within 60 days** of the last course session, submit the official score report **within 90 days** of the test date. Remedy: refund **or** free retake of the prep. Marketing copy states the guarantee is derived from having "analyzed the work of thousands of students to identify what work they completed to achieve a 15-point score improvement" — i.e., the design is explicitly a **conditional-on-completers analysis of past customers**, which is the survivorship structure named as a flaw below. The course page also advertises instructors averaging 526 on the MCAT, which is an input credential, not an outcome.

**The Princeton Review** advertises the same structure — 515+ or +15 depending on starting score — with a **tiered partial-refund** table: an increase of 10–14 points yields 50% of tuition, a final score under 510 yields full tuition, less materials fees. Learners with a starting score of 515 or higher are **ineligible**.

**Others**: Blueprint advertises a score-increase guarantee at a lower price point. Guarantees across the market are uniformly conditioned on completing a minimum proportion of the course and testing within a window.

### The standard methodological problems, and specifically how each one operates here

**1. The baseline is a diagnostic, not a score.** Both guarantees are measured from an in-house baseline/diagnostic exam. A cold diagnostic taken before any content review, under low stakes, with no stamina training, is a downward-biased estimate of true ability. Every point of that bias converts directly into "improvement." This is the single largest lever and it is entirely within the vendor's control.

**2. Regression to the mean.** Guarantee eligibility is defined by a *low* baseline (Princeton Review excludes starting scores ≥ 515; Kaplan's +15 tier applies to baselines < 500). Selecting on a low observed score and re-measuring guarantees mean-reverting gains with no instruction at all. AAMC's own data show the pattern in the population: median retest gain **+2 to +4 points for first attempts of 472–517**, versus **0 to +1 for 518–528**, and the AAMC notes gains are larger when more time elapses between attempts. The gain is largest exactly where the guarantee applies.

**3. No control group, and the counterfactual is not zero.** The canonical demonstration is from the SAT literature: at Deerfield Academy, 19 students who completed a commercial prep course gained an average of **80 SAT points** — and the 119 uncoached students at the same school gained **80 points** as well. Without a comparison group, the entire 80 points reads as a program effect. DerSimonian & Laird's synthesis found coaching effects **four to five times larger** in studies without control groups than in controlled ones.

**4. Survivorship and compliance conditioning.** Guarantees require completing prescribed work; the "average +N points" statistics are computed on students who completed it. Completion is caused by the same conscientiousness and available time that cause score gains. Conditioning the estimate on a post-treatment variable is a textbook collider adjustment and it manufactures effect. The industry does this by construction: the completion requirement is simultaneously the legal defense against payouts and the selection filter that generates the marketing number.

**5. Self-report and voluntary response.** Powers & Rock, writing for ETS, note that coaching companies documented their claims "only by surveying previous customers to ascertain score changes after coaching," and that these — "although sometimes verified by prestigious accounting firms" — do not constitute scientific studies. An accounting firm can verify that the arithmetic on the returned surveys is correct. It cannot verify that the non-responders had the same outcomes.

**6. Claim asymmetry between marketing and remedy.** The guarantee is advertised as a score outcome and remedied as a **refund or free retake**. It is priced as an insurance product with a deductible: the vendor's expected liability is the refund rate times tuition, and the completion conditions and testing windows are the underwriting. Princeton Review's tiered table is explicit about this — partial credit for partial shortfalls. Nothing about the structure requires the course to work.

### Regulatory reality: the bar is low for marketing and high the moment someone looks

The empirical bar for a *published* efficacy claim in this market is near zero: no MCAT or USMLE prep product appears to have published a randomized trial with exam-score outcomes, and the two systematic assessments that exist (McGaghie et al. 2004; Werner & Bull 2003) find no demonstrated effect.

The *legal* bar is different and much higher, and there is a directly relevant precedent. In January 2016 the FTC settled with **Lumos Labs (Lumosity) for $2 million** over claims that its program improved performance "on everyday tasks, in school, at work, and in athletics" and delayed cognitive decline. The FTC's position was that the company lacked "competent and reliable scientific evidence"; the order **prohibits representations that the product improves performance in school, at work, or in athletics** absent substantiation, and the substantiation standard imposed was **randomized, blinded human clinical trials**. Lumosity is the closest available analogue: a subscription cognitive-training product making school and work performance claims. The regulator's answer was that internal studies and testimonials are not substantiation.

The practical read for a pre-launch, zero-user, cost-sensitive team:

- **Do not make a quantified score claim.** "+N points" or "beats X" invites the Lumosity standard, and the team has no RCT.
- **A guarantee is a pricing instrument, not an efficacy claim, and it is legally safer.** "Score 515+ or get your money back" is a conditional refund promise. The exposure is a refund rate, not a substantiation burden. This is presumably why the incumbents do it that way.
- **The strongest honest positioning is mechanism transparency plus a measured, non-score outcome.** "Every item is scheduled by a forgetting model and every error is re-tested on a spaced schedule" is a description of the product, verifiable and non-actionable. "Learners reach mastery on 422 knowledge components in X% less study time than a fixed-schedule control" is an efficiency claim you can actually run an experiment to support.
- **Being the only product with a real internal experiment is itself the differentiator against (c).** The market bar is so low that a single well-run, preregistered mechanism-level experiment with honest reporting would make this the best-evidenced product in the category — without needing a large effect. That is a marketing asset available to a team with no users and no budget, because the comparison class has nothing.

## Where the advantage probably is

Ranked by expected defensibility × measurability. For each: the mechanism, whether it is measurable pre-launch, and whether there is evidence it affects exam outcomes.

### 1. Time efficiency — same outcome, less time. Strongest available claim.

**Mechanism.** Mastery-based item selection plus forgetting-aware scheduling should reduce wasted repetitions in two directions: not re-testing what is already retrievable, and not re-teaching what was never forgotten. The counterfactual learner over-reviews easy material (Anki's default parameters are conservative and users rarely tune them) and under-reviews material they have wrongly judged as known.

**Evidence it affects outcomes.** Indirect but the best in this review. The LISP Tutor evaluations (Anderson, Conrad & Corbett, 1989, *Cognitive Science* 13(4)) found students completed identical exercises in **one-third to three-quarters of control time while scoring at least as well**, and about a letter grade higher in one of two studies. A secondary review reports the three-arm comparison as **11.4 hours (human tutor), 15.0 (LISP Tutor), 26.5 (traditional)** for identical exercises with **equal outcome-test performance** — those specific hours come from Shute's (1991) technical-report summary of Anderson, Boyle & Reiser (1985), not the primary source, so treat the exact figures as **Tier C** and the qualitative pattern as Tier B. The ITS literature's own best-case summary is "at least the same level of proficiency as conventional instruction in one third the time," and it labels this **best case** — which is the right way to read it. Kulik & Fletcher's asymmetry, already established by the sibling review (0.66 SD versus conventional instruction, "small" versus non-conventional controls), is the same shape: these systems' robust win is efficiency, and their achievement win depends on a weak comparator.

**Measurability: high, and this is the decisive point.** Time-to-criterion on a knowledge component is measured per component per learner, not once per learner per exam. With 422 KCs and hundreds of learners, a within-subject or KC-randomized design yields thousands of observations, whereas an exam-score endpoint yields one. An exam-score effect of d = 0.15 needs on the order of 1,400 learners for 80% power; a 20% reduction in reviews-to-criterion is detectable with a small fraction of that. Instrumentation required — timestamps, per-KC mastery trajectories, review counts — is already needed for BKT and FSRS to function.

**Honest caveats.** Time saved is only valuable if it is either reallocated to more learning or genuinely returned to the learner. If the system saves review time and the learner refills it with more study, exam scores may rise slightly and the efficiency claim becomes an intensity claim. If the learner banks the time, exam scores are flat by construction and the claim is about cost, not performance — which is a legitimate and honestly stated claim, but it is a claim about the learner's life, not their score. Decide which one you are making before designing the trial, because the two require different primary outcomes. Also: efficiency measured against the *system's own* non-adaptive control is a clean internal comparison; efficiency measured against Anki requires knowing the counterfactual learner's actual review load, which is unobservable without instrumenting a comparison arm.

### 2. Content coverage and coordination — real, measurable, weakest causal link.

**Mechanism.** 2,108 items across 422 KCs, 114 lessons, with a 140-item diagnostic routing attention, guarantees that no KC is silently skipped. The self-study failure mode is not inefficiency, it is **blind spots** — a learner at 45% bank completion with a high percent-correct has never practiced entire content areas. Coverage is the one thing an integrated system can guarantee and an unstructured learner cannot.

**Evidence.** Correlational only, but consistent. Deng et al.: MCQ volume and unique-Anki-card volume both independently predicted Step 1 controlling for MCAT and preclinical grades — and the fact that the *unique-card* count (a coverage measure) predicted while the commercial-flashcard count did not is at least suggestive that breadth rather than repetition carries signal. The Step 2 CK finding that completing **> 2,500 unique UWorld questions** was associated with higher scores (256 versus 252, p = .04) is a coverage-flavored dose-response, though from a single institution where UWorld usage was 98.1%, and I have it via secondary summary only.

**Measurability: high as a process metric, low as an outcome.** Percent-KC-coverage is trivially measurable and you will beat unstructured self-study on it by construction. That is exactly why it is weak evidence — a metric you win by definition is not an efficacy result. It becomes meaningful only if you can show coverage completeness predicts exam outcome *within* your own population, controlling for engagement, which requires real users and a plausible identification strategy.

**Hard caveat on scale.** 2,108 items is roughly **half** the mean question count (3,870) reported by Deng et al.'s Step 1 cohort. Under that study's slope of 445 questions per point, exhausting the entire bank corresponds to under 5 points — on a different exam, from a correlational slope that should not be read causally. The honest version of the coverage claim is about **distribution** — every KC gets attention proportional to its mastery deficit and exam weight — not about **volume**, where the system is not competitive with a large commercial bank and should not pretend to be. (I did not verify competitor bank sizes; that is a gap.)

### 3. Reduced planning burden — genuinely differentiated, plausibly the real user value, hard to convert into an outcome claim.

**Mechanism.** A learner facing 2–6 months, four MCAT sections, a full-length-test calendar, flashcards, and mistake review under a fixed daily time budget is being asked to solve a scheduling problem every single day. That is a real cognitive and emotional cost, and it is the cost the daily-plan scheduler eliminates. Note that this benefit does **not** require the schedule to be optimal — it requires it to be *good enough that the learner stops deliberating*. That is a much lower bar than beating Anki on learning rate, and it is worth recognizing that the scheduler's value may be almost entirely about decision elimination rather than sequence quality. If so, the Doroudi et al. null for adaptive sequencing is not fatal to the product; it is fatal only to the *learning-rate* rationale for the product.

**Evidence.** Weak, correlational, but pointed. The AAMC Post-MCAT Questionnaire analysis of 3,240 lower-SES examinees (*Teaching and Learning in Medicine*, 2025) found lower-scoring examinees were significantly more likely to report difficulty **"determining how to begin studying"** and accessing concrete information about the exam, while higher scorers reported more use of evidence-supported strategies including establishing baseline capabilities, practicing application, and taking a practice test to evaluate readiness. That is the best available evidence that planning burden is a real barrier tied to outcomes — and it is cross-sectional survey data with obvious confounding by prior preparation, advising access, and SES itself. *(Abstract only.)* It also identifies a segment where the value proposition is strongest: learners without advising infrastructure.

**Measurability: medium.** Measurable as self-reported planning burden, decision latency, plan-adherence rate, and time-to-first-productive-action. Converting to exam outcome is the hard part and probably requires a randomized comparison of scheduled versus self-directed use of identical content — a design that is feasible, ethically fine, and directly informative about whether the scheduler earns its complexity.

### 4. Adherence and completion — attractive, and the evidence is the most discouraging in this review.

**Mechanism.** Daily plans, streaks, and time budgets should raise completion; completion should raise outcomes.

**Evidence, and it is bad news.** The causal literature on scalable engagement interventions in education is a wall of nulls. Oreopoulos & Petronijevic (2018, *Journal of Human Resources* 53(2)) randomized **> 4,000** undergraduates to control, a one-time online goal-affirmation exercise, a full-year text-message advice-and-motivation campaign, or one-on-one coaching. **One-on-one coaching: +0.3 SD average grades, +0.35 SD GPA (~4.92 percentage points).** Online exercise: **no effect**. Text-message campaign: **no effect on any academic outcome**, in the full sample and across subgroups. Their follow-up (NBER w24992; *Economics of Education Review* 2020, N > 3,000) found **precisely estimated nulls** on GPA and credit accumulation from an online module plus text coaching, alongside **positive effects on self-reported study behavior, satisfaction, belonging, and mental health**. The authors' own diagnosis is that texting campaigns are effective for nudging **one-time simple actions** (college enrollment, aid renewal) and ineffective for outcomes requiring **sustained behavior change**.

Read that carefully, because it is the exact shape of the trap: **the intervention moved self-reported study behavior and well-being and did not move grades.** A dashboard showing improved adherence and happier users is fully consistent with zero learning effect. That is the outcome to expect, and if the team's success metric is engagement it will report a win that the exam does not corroborate.

There is one supportive signal from the other direction: Adesope et al. found practice-test effects of **g = 0.65 with high implementation fidelity versus g = 0.46 with low fidelity**. So adherence does plausibly moderate how much of the mechanism's benefit is realized. But moderating a g ≈ 0.10-versus-active-control effect yields very little in absolute terms.

**Measurability: high for the behavior, and that is the danger.** Adherence is the easiest thing to measure and the least connected to exam outcome. Treat completion as a **necessary intermediate**, never as an outcome claim.

### 5. Superior learning rate per unit time — least defensible. Do not build the strategy on it.

Established by sibling reviews: the target against strong active controls is d ≈ 0.10–0.20; Kraft's median across 747 education RCTs is 0.10 SD and 0.03 for pre-registered federally-funded ones. Added here: against *other elaborative strategies*, the testing effect itself is **g = 0.095, p = .062** (Yang et al. 2021), and SAT coaching against controls is **0.09 SD verbal / 0.16 SD math** (Becker 1990). The adaptive-sequencing mechanism has **zero clean wins in eight studies** of interdependent content (Doroudi et al.). No FSRS learning-outcome RCT exists.

**Assume the true learning-rate effect against a diligent Anki-plus-question-bank user is somewhere between 0.00 and 0.15, and that the team cannot distinguish it from zero at any sample size it will have before launch.** On an MCAT total-score scale with a standard deviation of roughly 10–11 points (I did not verify a published AAMC SD figure), d = 0.15 is about **1.5 points** — inside the noise of a single administration and well under the +2 to +4 median that retakers achieve by doing nothing special. Detecting it would require thousands of learners with official score outcomes.

### Cross-cutting: the diligent-Anki-user problem

The team should sit with this. A learner using a well-curated MCAT Anki deck plus a large commercial question bank is already getting: retrieval practice with corrective feedback (the highest-effect cell of every meta-analysis here), spacing with a working scheduler, format-matched practice, mistake review via lapse handling, and item volume exceeding this system's 2,108. The mechanisms they lack are: orchestration across activity types, mastery-model-driven selection at calibrated difficulty, coverage guarantees, and not having to plan. Every one of those is a **coordination** benefit, not a **memory** benefit — and the memory mechanisms are where the large, replicated effect sizes live.

The corollary is uncomfortable but clarifying: **the system is probably not competing with Anki on learning. It is competing with the cost of assembling and running an Anki-plus-qbank-plus-full-length regimen yourself.** That is a real product with a real market. It is a claim about the learner's time, decisions, and coverage — not their learning rate. Every measurement decision should follow from accepting that.

## DOK 1 — established facts

1. Rowland (2014) meta-analyzed 61 studies / 159 effect sizes: testing versus restudy, **g = 0.50**; **g = 0.41** at retention intervals < 1 day and **g = 0.69** at ≥ 1 day; **g = 0.73** with feedback versus **g = 0.39** without.
2. Adesope et al. (2017) meta-analyzed 118 articles / 272 effect sizes / N = 15,427: overall **g = 0.61** fixed; **g = 0.51 versus restudy**, **g = 0.93 versus filler/no activity**. The authors state g = 0.51 is "arguably the most accurate" estimate of the testing effect.
3. In Adesope et al., multiple-choice practice tests yielded **g = 0.70**; the highest interval-specific effect was **g = 0.82 at 1–6 days**; classroom studies (g = 0.67) matched laboratory studies (g = 0.62); high implementation fidelity yielded g = 0.65 versus 0.46 for low.
4. Yang et al. (2021) meta-analyzed 222 classroom studies / 48,478 students: overall **g = 0.499**. Control condition significantly moderated the effect: **no activity g = 0.610; fewer questions g = 0.465; restudying g = 0.330; other elaborative strategies g = 0.095 [−0.005, 0.194], p = .062, non-significant.**
5. Yang et al. also found feedback (g = 0.537 versus 0.374) and matched test format (g = 0.531 versus 0.399) significantly amplified the classroom testing effect; instructor consistency did not moderate it.
6. Brunmair & Richter (2019) meta-analyzed 59 papers / 238 effect sizes / N = 8,466: interleaved versus blocked **g = 0.42**; **paintings g = 0.67; math g = 0.34; expository texts and tastes non-significant; words g = −0.39 (blocking superior)**. Interleaving effects were larger for material more similar between categories, less similar within categories, and more complex.
7. Rohrer et al. (2020) ran a preregistered cluster RCT in 54 grade-7 math classes over 4 months, holding practice problems identical and varying only order: on an unannounced test **one month later**, interleaved 61% versus blocked 38%, **d = 0.83 [0.68, 0.97]**, positive for all 15 teachers.
8. Carvalho & Goldstone (2013) found interleaving superior for categories with high within- and between-category similarity and **blocking superior for categories with low within- and between-category similarity**; Noh et al. (2016) found **blocking superior for rule-based** and interleaving superior for information-integration category structures.
9. Deng et al. (2015), N = 72, controlling for MCAT, preclinical honours, and psychological factors, found **~445 additional boards-style questions or ~1,700 additional unique Anki cards was associated with one additional Step 1 point**; commercial flashcards (Firecracker) did not predict score; test anxiety predicted negatively.
10. Werner & Bull (2003), N = 468, found regression lines for actual-on-CBSE-predicted Step 1 **statistically indistinguishable** between students who took a 3–4 week commercial coaching course (n = 99) and self-studiers (n = 369), in an analysis powered to detect ~1% (≈ 2 of 350 questions).
11. McGaghie et al. (2004) systematically reviewed commercial test prep for medical examinations and concluded incremental validity is "extremely small" and supporting evidence "weak or nonexistent," with no cost-benefit analyses performed.
12. Becker (1990) meta-analyzed SAT coaching: published comparison-group studies showed coached groups exceeding controls by **0.09 SD on SAT-V and 0.16 SD on SAT-M**. DerSimonian & Laird (1983) found coaching effects **4–5× larger** in uncontrolled than controlled studies.
13. AAMC's own retest analysis: **median gain of +2 to +4 total points** from first to second attempt for first-attempt scores of 472–517, and **0 to +1 points** for 518–528, with substantial variance in both directions; gains are larger when more time elapses between attempts.
14. Kaplan and The Princeton Review both advertise a **"515+ or +15 points"** MCAT guarantee, conditioned on completing prescribed coursework and testing within a defined window (Kaplan: 60 days after the last session; score report within 90 days), remedied by refund (Princeton Review: tiered/partial) or free retake.
15. In January 2016 the FTC settled with Lumos Labs (Lumosity) for **$2 million**; the order bars unsubstantiated claims that the product improves performance in school, at work, or in athletics, and requires **randomized, blinded human clinical trials** as substantiation.
16. Anderson, Conrad & Corbett (1989) reported LISP Tutor students completing identical exercises in **one-third to three-quarters** of control time while scoring at least as well, and about a letter grade higher in one of two evaluations.
17. Oreopoulos & Petronijevic (2018), randomizing > 4,000 undergraduates, found one-on-one coaching produced **+0.30 SD** on average grades and **+0.35 SD** on GPA, while an online exercise and a year-long text-message campaign produced **no effect on any academic outcome** in any subgroup.
18. Their follow-up (N > 3,000) found **precisely estimated nulls** on GPA and credit accumulation from an online module plus text coaching, alongside **positive effects on self-reported well-being, belonging, and study behavior**.
19. AAMC Post-MCAT Questionnaire data: **85.4%** of respondents used Official MCAT Practice Exams, **72.6%** used commercial prep books, and **89.7%** took a timed online practice exam mimicking exam day.
20. In one Step 2 CK cohort, **UWorld was used by 98.1%** of study participants.

## DOK 2 — insights and implications

1. **The active-control penalty is measured, not hypothesized, and it lands almost exactly on the sibling reviews' target band.** Yang et al.'s g = 0.095 against elaborative strategies and Becker's 0.09/0.16 SD for SAT coaching against controls were derived from completely different literatures with different methods, and they agree. Treat d ≈ 0.10 as the *modal* honest expectation against a diligent active comparator, with 0.20 as an optimistic ceiling.

2. **The mechanisms with big effect sizes are the ones the comparator already has; the mechanisms that are proprietary are the ones with null results.** Retrieval practice (g ≈ 0.50), spacing, feedback, and format match are all present in an Anki-plus-qbank regimen. What is proprietary is orchestration, sequencing, and selection — and adaptive sequencing of interdependent content has zero clean wins in eight studies. This inversion is the strategic fact of the whole review, and it implies the product's value proposition should be written in the language of coordination, not memory.

3. **The scheduler's activity variety is not the interleaving literature's interleaving, and conflating them would be a real error.** The g = 0.42 and d = 0.83 findings come from alternating *confusable exemplars within an inductive-learning task*; the effect arises from forcing a discrimination. Alternating lesson → flashcards → mistake review is task alternation, which that literature does not address. The system's real interleaving mechanism is KC mixing inside practice blocks, and *that* is where the boundary conditions apply.

4. **Interleaving's boundary conditions convert directly into a testable scheduler policy.** Block on first acquisition of a KC (learner needs to find commonalities); interleave once minimally competent, preferentially among confusable KCs (learner needs discriminative contrast). Do not interleave CARS passage practice — expository text was non-significant and word material negative. This is a concrete, cheap, order-only manipulation with a preregistered RCT precedent, and it belongs near the front of the mechanism-experiment queue.

5. **Interleaving fights the success-rate controller, and the controller will win unless told not to.** Interleaving lowers in-session accuracy while raising delayed retention. A system targeting differentiated success rates reads lower accuracy as "too hard" and will compensate by making items easier — silently undoing the manipulation. Any interleaving experiment must therefore either freeze the difficulty controller or model the interaction, and the primary outcome must be **delayed**, since the effect is defined on delayed tests.

6. **The Deng et al. slope is the best argument against a volume-based strategy, and it comes from the paper most often cited to support one.** At 445 questions per Step 1 point, a 2,108-item bank is arithmetically incapable of a large volume-driven effect even taking the correlational slope at face value — and it should not be taken at face value. Reframe the coverage claim from volume to **distribution**: every KC receives attention proportional to mastery deficit and exam weight. That is a claim the system can actually keep.

7. **Correlational question-bank findings are contaminated in a specific and predictable direction, and the Cureus model shows it cleanly.** In one model, UWorld percent-correct correlated r = 0.62 with Step 1 while **days studied correlated r = 0.02**. What predicts is how well you answer, i.e. ability — not how long you worked. Any observational analysis of the team's own usage data will reproduce this artifact, and it will look like proof that the product works.

8. **Firecracker's null in Deng et al. is the most useful negative result for this product's positioning.** Commercial pre-made spaced flashcards did not predict Step 1; user-generated Anki cards did. The most parsimonious reading is that the act of generating and selecting cards carries the signal, not the delivery mechanism. This is uncomfortable for any product that hands learners pre-authored items and a scheduler — it is precisely the Firecracker architecture — and it should temper confidence that a well-scheduled pre-made corpus reproduces what diligent Anki users get. It is one coefficient in one N = 72 study, but it points at the product's weakest structural assumption and deserves a mechanism experiment rather than a dismissal.

9. **The guarantee is an insurance product and should be understood as one.** "515+ or +15" is priced off the refund rate, underwritten by completion conditions and testing windows, and measured from a vendor-controlled diagnostic. It generates a marketing number through survivorship (Kaplan's copy openly describes analyzing "the work of thousands of students" who *achieved* a 15-point improvement) and through regression to the mean (eligibility requires a low baseline). None of it requires the course to work — which is consistent with Werner & Bull's null and McGaghie et al.'s review.

10. **The +15 guarantee versus AAMC's +2-to-4 median retest gain is the number to keep in mind whenever anyone proposes a score claim.** The population-median gain from doing everything a motivated retaker does is 2–4 points. Any claim materially above that is either measured from a depressed baseline, conditioned on completers, or both.

11. **Being the only product with a real experiment is a viable differentiator against competitors precisely because the market bar is so low.** No MCAT or USMLE prep product appears to have published a credible causal estimate. One preregistered mechanism-level experiment, honestly reported, would make this the best-evidenced product in the category without requiring a large effect. That is unusually cheap differentiation for a team with no users.

12. **The Lumosity order draws the line the marketing must stay behind.** Performance claims about school or work outcomes require randomized human trials. Mechanism-transparency claims ("scheduled by a forgetting model," "every error re-tested on a spaced schedule") are descriptions of the product and are safe. Conditional refund promises are pricing. Quantified score claims are the one category that invites the substantiation standard the team cannot meet.

13. **Time efficiency is the only dimension where the claim, the mechanism, the precedent, and the measurement all line up.** ITS efficiency results (one-third to three-quarters the time at equal outcomes) are the most robust finding in the adjacent literature; the mechanism (avoid unnecessary reviews) is a direct consequence of BKT + FSRS working; and the outcome is measurable per knowledge component rather than once per learner, which changes the required sample size by orders of magnitude. This should be the primary claim and the primary experimental endpoint.

14. **Decide now whether the efficiency claim is about score or about cost, because they need different trials.** If saved time is reallocated to more study, the claim becomes an intensity claim and the endpoint is exam performance. If saved time is returned to the learner, the endpoint is time-to-criterion at matched mastery and scores are flat *by design* — which must be stated up front rather than discovered and spun later.

15. **Adherence is the trap, and Oreopoulos & Petronijevic show exactly how it closes.** Their interventions moved self-reported study behavior, satisfaction, and belonging while producing precisely estimated nulls on grades. A product dashboard showing better adherence and happier users is fully consistent with zero learning effect. Adherence is a necessary intermediate and must never be reported as an outcome.

16. **Planning-burden reduction may be the true value proposition, and it survives the Doroudi et al. null.** The scheduler's benefit may be almost entirely that the learner stops deliberating — which requires the schedule to be *good enough*, not *optimal*. If so, the absence of evidence for adaptive sequencing gains is not fatal to the product; it is fatal only to the learning-rate rationale for it. The clean test is randomizing scheduled versus self-directed use of identical content, and it is worth running because it tells you whether the scheduler earns its engineering complexity.

17. **The lower-SES PMQ finding identifies the segment where the value proposition is strongest.** Lower-scoring examinees from lower-SES backgrounds were more likely to report difficulty "determining how to begin studying" and accessing concrete exam information. Learners without advising infrastructure are where a good default plan plausibly has the largest counterfactual value — the comparator is not a diligent Anki user, it is someone who does not know where to start. This is also the segment where a small-N efficiency or completion trial has the best chance of detecting something, and where the product's social value is highest.

18. **Practice-tests-alone is a straw comparator and the claim should be narrowed accordingly.** With ~90% of examinees already taking timed practice exams and ~73% using commercial books, nobody is doing practice tests alone. The honest version of claim (a) is mechanism-specific: full-length tests deliver retrieval but distribute it by blueprint frequency rather than by the learner's forgetting curve, and surface errors without scheduling their re-exposure.

19. **The retention-interval and feedback moderators are the strongest design-quality argument available, and they should be framed as design quality rather than as effect size.** A 2–6 month horizon, corrective feedback on every item, and MCAT-format four-option items place the system in the favorable cell of every moderator in Rowland, Adesope, and Yang. That is a legitimate claim that the implementation is well-aligned to the evidence. It is not a claim about superiority over another well-implemented product.

20. **The honest overall position: this is probably not a better way to learn, it is a cheaper way to run a good study regimen.** The measurable deliverables are less time, fewer decisions, and no blind spots. That is a real product with a real market, and it is defensible. Claiming a superior learning rate is not, and building the evaluation strategy around detecting one would consume the budget on the one thing least likely to be found.

## What I could not verify

1. **The precise Anderson, Boyle & Reiser (1985) hours (11.4 / 15.0 / 26.5).** These come from Shute's (1991) technical-report summary, not the primary source. I did not read Anderson, Boyle & Reiser (1985) directly. The qualitative claim (equal outcomes, substantially less time) is corroborated by Anderson, Conrad & Corbett (1989), which I did access; the specific hour figures should be treated as secondary.
2. **The Sherlock claim** ("20 hours of tutoring ≈ skills of technicians with nearly 4 additional years of experience") and the Smithtown and PASCAL-tutor time-savings figures. All from the same secondary review, none traced to primary sources, and all read as best-case selections. I would not use them.
3. **The 2026 *Clinical Teacher* spaced-repetition meta-analysis (SMD = 0.78, 21,415 learners): abstract only.** I could not inspect the comparison conditions, retention intervals, per-study effect sizes, MERSQI quality distribution, or the publication-bias analysis. Given that "standard studying techniques" plausibly spans no-intervention through business-as-usual, this number may sit in the g ≈ 0.61 (inactive-control) regime rather than the g ≈ 0.10 (active-control) regime. It is the largest available medical-education estimate and also the one I trust least relative to its size.
4. **The 2026 *Medical Science Educator* Anki systematic review: abstract and results summary only.** The "+4 to +13 points" range and the Lu et al. and Wothe et al. figures come from that summary rather than the primary studies.
5. **Werner & Bull (2003) and McGaghie et al. (2004): abstracts only.** For Werner & Bull I could not inspect the regression specification, whether course-takers differed systematically on unmeasured variables, or the exact power calculation. The power statement is the abstract's own claim.
6. **Noh et al. (2016): abstract only.** Direction of the schedule × structure interaction is from the abstract; I did not see effect sizes.
7. **Becker (1990) full text**: I read the abstract and summary passages, plus secondary characterizations from College Board and ETS reports. The 0.09 / 0.16 SD figures are the abstract's own. The "~9 points SAT-V / ~19 points SAT-M" restatement is from a College Board summary, i.e. an interested party, though one arguing *against* coaching efficacy.
8. **Whether any MCAT or medical-exam prep company has ever published a randomized trial with exam-score outcomes.** I found none, and McGaghie et al. found none as of 2004. Absence of evidence in my searches is not proof of absence — this is a claim about the searchable published record, and vendors may hold unpublished internal trials.
9. **Competitor question-bank sizes.** I did not verify item counts for UWorld MCAT, AAMC official materials, Blueprint, or Kaplan. The comparison of this system's 2,108 items against Deng et al.'s mean of 3,870 questions is against a *Step 1* cohort's total consumption across resources, not against a specific competitor's MCAT bank. Treat the coverage-scale argument as directional.
10. **The MCAT total-score standard deviation.** I used ~10–11 points to translate effect sizes into score points and did not verify a published AAMC SD. Any score-point translation in this document inherits that uncertainty. Verify before using in planning.
11. **Blueprint's specific guarantee terms.** Sourced only from a third-party comparison site, not from Blueprint's own terms and conditions. Kaplan's and Princeton Review's terms I read on their own pages.
12. **Several SEO-content sources returned by search — `residencyadvisor.com`, `nbmescore.com`, `testpreppal.com`, `gradpilot.com` — present specific-looking statistics ("survey data from 510+ test takers," hours-to-score tables, "median 290 hours, IQR 230–380") with no methodology, no sample definition, and no traceable source.** I have deliberately used none of their numbers. They are worth knowing about as a competitive-landscape observation: much of what an MCAT learner finds when searching for evidence is unfalsifiable content marketing, which is part of why the market bar for truth is as low as it is. One such page cites a real study (Rao et al., r = 0.622) correctly and then surrounds it with invented-looking tables — that mixture is the hardest kind to filter.
13. **The Kirk-Johnson, Galla & Fraundorf (2019) metacognition finding and the Kornell & Bjork (2008) blocking-preference finding**: both read via secondary accounts (Yang et al. 2021 and the Carvalho/Frontiers reviews respectively), not the originals.
14. **The FTC "Word Smart" learning-claims case**, referenced by an advertising-law blog as a prior FTC action on learning claims. Not verified; potentially a useful precedent if someone wants to map the regulatory boundary more carefully.
15. **The "> 2,500 unique UWorld questions → 256 vs 252, p = .04" Step 2 finding**: from a secondary summary page, not the primary paper. Flagged Tier C accordingly.
16. **A reported RCT of a peer-led Step 1 preparation program finding no significant difference** appeared in a search synthesis. I could not identify the underlying citation and have not relied on it.

**1. Yang, C., Luo, L., Vadillo, M. A., Yu, R., & Shanks, D. R. (2021).

# Off-policy evaluation, experiment infrastructure, and pre-launch instrumentation

Scope: what must be true of this system's logging and decision-making so that its effectiveness can be measured later, and what is irreversibly lost if it is not built before the first learner arrives.

Evidence tiers used inline: **A** = strong direct evidence or field consensus; **B** = reasonable with stated limits; **C** = weak, indirect, or single-source. Where a number comes from my own arithmetic rather than a paper, it is labelled *[own calculation]*. Where I read only an abstract, it says so.

---

## Recommendation

**Minimum viable evaluation infrastructure, in one sentence:** every scheduling decision must be *sampled from an explicit probability distribution over an explicit candidate set*, and the candidate set, the per-candidate score, the per-candidate probability, the sampled action, the exact state values the decision consumed, the policy version, and the RNG seed must be written to an append-only log at decision time.

**The single most important thing to do before launch:** replace the top-level `argmax` in Today's Path and in adaptive practice item selection with **tolerance-band sampling** — deterministic filtering and scoring as today, then uniform (or softmax) sampling among candidates whose score is within a tolerance δ of the best score — and log the resulting propensity. Everything else in this document is either downstream of that change or can be retrofitted later. Propensity logging cannot be retrofitted; A/B test infrastructure can.

Why this specific change rather than ε-greedy: it bounds the cost to learners analytically. If you only randomise among candidates whose score is within δ of the maximum, the worst-case per-decision regret *in your own score units* is δ, chosen by you, and it is zero when the band contains only one candidate. It also produces *lower*-variance off-policy estimates than ε-greedy at the same cost, because the minimum propensity is 1/|band| (order 1/2 to 1/4) rather than ε/K (order 1/60). The price is that you can only evaluate policies that differ from production within the band — which is precisely the "restrict the policy space" remedy that Sachdeva, Su & Joachims (KDD 2020) found to be the most effective of the three known approaches to support-deficient logs, because it gives explicit risk control, is robust, and is cheap to implement (**A**).

**Confidence:** High (**A**) that off-policy estimators of the IPS family are unusable — not merely noisy, but unidentified — on logs from a deterministic policy, and that propensities, candidate sets, and decision-time state snapshots are not reconstructable after the fact. High (**A**) that exam-score outcomes will be unmeasurable at launch cohort scale. Moderate (**B**) on the specific δ and band-size values, which are product judgements with no literature to fix them. Moderate (**B**) that a proxy reward can be made defensible here; low (**C**) that any within-app proxy you pick at launch will survive validation without adjustment.

**The five things that are irreversible** (full ordering in the checklist at the end):

1. Decision logs with propensities and candidate sets — cannot be backfilled at all.
2. Non-degenerate action selection — a deterministic launch cohort is permanently uninformative about alternatives.
3. Append-only (event-sourced) learner state — once a BKT posterior or FSRS card row is overwritten, the decision that consumed it cannot be reconstructed or replayed.
4. Reserved probe items per knowledge component, never scheduled by the normal pipeline — exposure contamination is permanent, and at 2,108 items / 422 KCs (≈5 items/KC) you have very little slack.
5. Consent and a mechanism to collect the real MCAT score and test date — without at least one cohort that has both the within-app proxies *and* the exam score, no proxy can ever be validated, and the surrogate-index machinery (Athey et al., *Review of Economic Studies*) has nothing to calibrate against.

**What you should not build:** a contextual bandit over instructional content, a high-confidence-off-policy-evaluation safety layer, a switchback design, or a bespoke experimentation platform. Justification for each is in Findings; the short version is that the sample sizes required are 10²–10⁴× what a launch cohort provides.

## The determinism problem

### How binding it is

The requirement is *positivity* (also called full support, or common support). Sachdeva, Su & Joachims state it as: the logging policy π₀ has full support for target policy π when π₀(a|x) > 0 for every action a and context x with π(a|x) > 0 (**A**, full text read). IPS is unbiased if and only if this holds. For a rich policy class — anything expressible as a softmax over scores, which is exactly what a re-tuned Today's Path would be — this reduces to requiring π₀ to assign non-zero probability to *every* candidate in *every* state.

A deterministic scheduler assigns probability 1 to its own choice and 0 to everything else. This is not a mild violation. It is the extreme point of what the literature calls **deficient support**, and the consequence is bias, not variance:

- The bias of IPS equals the expected reward on the unsupported actions, weighted by the target policy's probability on them (Sachdeva et al., Prop. 1) (**A**).
- Define the *support divergence* D_X(π|π₀) as the expected probability mass the target policy places on actions with zero logging probability. Their Theorem 1: with a deterministic logging policy, ERM using IPS can return a policy that is at least (r_max − r_min)·max_π D_X(π|π₀) worse than optimal **in the limit of infinite data** (**A**).
- Their worked illustration: rewards in [−1, 0], a good policy with true value −0.1 and a bad policy with true value −0.7. If the bad policy has support divergence ≥ 0.6, IPS-based ERM may return the bad policy over the good one no matter how much data you collect (**A**).
- Under a fully deterministic logger, D_X(π|π₀) = 1 − (fraction of decisions where π agrees with π₀). So for a target policy that disagrees with production half the time, the guaranteed-suboptimality bound is half the full reward range. There is no data-collection fix. Clipping, self-normalisation, and variance regularisation do not help, because the problem is a missing term, not a heavy tail (**A**, explicit in Sachdeva et al. §3).

Practically, what IPS computes on a deterministic log is: the average logged reward over the subset of decisions where the target policy happens to agree with production, and nothing at all about the disagreements. That is the direct method restricted to the agreement set, with zero counterfactual content. A recent preprint on ranking OPE under deterministic logging states the same conclusion for the ranking case and describes deterministic logging as "a strict special case of deficient support, eliminating all stochasticity, which makes existing methods severely ineffective" (arXiv 2603.21485; **not peer reviewed** as far as I could verify, though slides appear on the ICLR 2026 site, which suggests acceptance there — I did not confirm; **C**).

**Note on the recently added seeded randomisation in diagnostic item assembly.** Tie-breaking randomisation is exactly the right *shape* of intervention but, as described, it is almost certainly insufficient in two ways: (a) it applies only to diagnostic assembly, not to Today's Path or adaptive practice, which are the policies you actually want to evaluate; (b) "seeded tie-breaking" is only useful for OPE if the *probability* assigned to each tied candidate is computed and logged. A seed alone lets you replay; it does not give you a propensity. If the tie set is genuinely a tie set of size m and you sample uniformly, the propensity is 1/m — but you must log m, the members, and 1/m, because the tie set depends on inventory and learner state at that instant and cannot be recomputed later. Verify whether m and 1/m are being persisted; if only the seed is persisted, you have replayability without evaluability.

### What "sufficient exploration" means quantitatively

Four operational quantities, in increasing usefulness:

1. **Positivity floor.** π₀(a|x) ≥ p_min > 0 for every action the target policy might take. Importance weights are then bounded by w = π/π₀ ≤ 1/p_min.
2. **Second moment, not the mean.** IPS variance is driven by E[w²r²]/n. The mean weight is 1 by construction, so "average weight looks fine" tells you nothing.
3. **Effective sample size.** ESS = (Σᵢwᵢ)² / Σᵢwᵢ² is the standard importance-sampling diagnostic; it reports, on the natural scale of samples, how many independent draws from the target your reweighted logs are worth (**A** for the diagnostic; its standard statement and known failure mode — ESS can look healthy if the proposal never visits the target's high-mass region — are well documented). Ship this. Refuse to act on any off-policy estimate whose ESS is below a pre-agreed floor.
4. **The identity Σᵢwᵢ/n → 1.** Swaminathan & Joachims (NeurIPS 2015) show that the weight-sum control variate has expectation exactly 1 for *every* policy in the class (**A**). This is a free, decisive unit test on your propensity logging: if the empirical weight average departs materially from 1 for a policy you believe you can evaluate, your logged propensities are wrong. Make it a CI assertion.

Two concrete replay/efficiency facts worth internalising:

- With uniform-random logging over K arms, the replay estimator of Li, Chu, Langford & Wang (WSDM 2011) retains exactly 1/K of logged events for a deterministic target policy, i.e. it needs O(KT) logged events to yield T usable ones (**A**, full text read). Randomising does not merely cost reward; it costs data efficiency, and the cost scales with the size of the candidate set.
- Trajectory-level importance sampling is hopeless at this system's horizon. Jiang & Li (ICML 2016) state that IS variance "easily grows exponentially in horizon", and they prove a Cramér–Rao lower bound establishing this as inherent hardness rather than an estimator defect (**A**, abstract plus quoted passages). Today's Path over eight weeks at ~6 decisions/day is roughly 330 sequential decisions. **Therefore: frame all evaluation as a decision-level contextual-bandit problem with a short-horizon reward, never as an RL trajectory problem.** This is forced by variance mathematics, not a preference.

### The quantified remedy and its cost

| Remedy | Propensity | Max weight | Cost to learners | Verdict here |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ε-greedy over full candidate set | ε/K for non-greedy, 1−ε+ε/K for greedy | K/ε | ε × (mean regret of a uniformly random pick) | Too expensive; a uniformly random pick from all eligible activities can be very bad |
| Boltzmann / softmax over existing scores at temperature τ | softmax(score/τ) | unbounded in principle, controllable via floor | grows smoothly with τ | Good second choice; needs a propensity floor to bound weights |
| **Tolerance-band sampling: uniform (or softmax) among candidates within δ of the top score** | 1/\|band\| | \|band\| | **≤ δ per decision, in your own score units, by construction** | **Recommended** |
| Explicit exploration budget (a fixed fraction of learner-days run under a randomised policy) | as chosen | as chosen | concentrated on a minority of learners | Useful complement for evaluating policies outside the band |
| Restrict the target policy class to what the log supports | n/a | n/a | zero | Mandatory discipline regardless of which of the above you pick (**A**, Sachdeva et al.) |

**Arithmetic for this system** *[own calculation]*, assuming 200 launch learners × 56 study days × 6 scheduling decisions/day ≈ 67,000 logged decisions, a reward scaled to [0,1], and a target policy that disagrees with production on half of decisions:

- ε-greedy with ε = 0.10 and K = 6 candidates: p_min = 0.017, max weight 60. Informative disagreement observations ≈ 67,000 × 0.5 × 0.017 ≈ 560, each amplified 60×. E[w²] ≈ 0.5 × K/ε = 30, so with r² ≈ 0.5 the IPS standard error is ≈ √(15/67,000) ≈ 0.015, i.e. roughly ±0.03 at 95%.
- Tolerance-band with a typical band of 3 near-ties: p_min = 1/3, max weight 3, E[w²] ≤ 9, standard error ≈ √(4.5/67,000) ≈ 0.008, i.e. roughly ±0.017 — about half the width, at a fraction of the cost, **but only for target policies that reorder within the band**.

The general lesson from that comparison: variance and learner cost both scale with how far the exploration distribution is spread from the production choice, while the *set of evaluable alternatives* also scales with spread. You buy evaluability with both reward and precision. Band randomisation buys the cheapest useful slice.

**What exploration actually costs, empirically.** This is where the team's objection should be answered with numbers rather than reassurance.

- **Bing ad placement** (Bottou et al., *JMLR* 2013; **A**, full text read of the relevant passage). Mainline reserve prices were multiplied by a log-normal random multiplier with mean 1 and σ = 0.3, so 95% of multipliers fell in [0.52, 1.74] — a substantial live perturbation on 22 million search result pages over five days. Measured against an unrandomised control bucket: a "small but statistically significant increase in the number of mainline ads per page", with click-yield and average-revenue differences **not significant**. Their conclusion: accurate counterfactual estimates at affordable randomisation cost.
- **Educational game concept sequencing** (Mandel, Liu, Levine, Brunskill & Popović, AAMAS 2014; **B** — single study, engagement/achievement proxy, a fractions game rather than exam prep; full text read). Data was collected under a *uniformly random* exploration policy. In the subsequent 2,000-student deployment, the expert-designed policy had a **slightly lower** mean reward than the random policy (p = 0.389), and the offline-selected adaptive policy beat both by **32%** (p = 0.002 vs random, p < 0.0001 vs expert). In that setting, the measured cost of uniform random sequencing relative to a human-expert curriculum was statistically indistinguishable from zero.
- **Fractions intelligent tutoring system** (Doroudi, Aleven & Brunskill, *IJAIED* 2019, Appendix B; **A** for the described practice, full text read). Initial data collection from >1,000 students used a *semi-randomised* order as an explicit compromise between learner benefit and evaluability: the first 26 activities were constrained to follow a prerequisite graph with random choice among admissible candidates, then activities were drawn uniformly at random without replacement from a pool of 130. This is essentially the tolerance-band design applied at curriculum scale, and it is the closest published precedent for what I am recommending.
- **Adaptive assignment is not a free alternative** (Rafferty, Williams & Ying, *JEDM* 2019; **A**, full text read). Thompson-sampling assignment raised student reward but cut power from a nominal 0.80 to **0.57** for binary outcomes and **0.49** for normally-distributed outcomes; doubling n recovered only 0.79 and 0.69 respectively; **at least twice as many participants** are needed for acceptable power. It also inflated false positives when student characteristics drifted over time, and overestimated effect sizes (true d = 0.2 estimated as 0.30; true d = 0.8 estimated as 0.93). An optimistic prior mitigated the power loss without materially reducing student benefit.

**Bottom line on cost.** Two large-scale precedents (one industrial, one educational) measured the cost of substantial deliberate randomisation and found it small or nil on the metrics they cared about. Neither licenses the claim that randomisation is free here — an MCAT learner has a hard deadline and a hard time budget, which the Bing users and the fractions students did not. But the tolerance-band design makes the cost a parameter you set, which converts the argument from a values dispute into a product decision: *how many score points of ordering quality is a measurable product worth?* If δ is set so that band members differ by less than the score noise implied by your own BKT posterior uncertainty, the honest answer is that you cannot tell which band member is better anyway — and randomising among candidates you cannot rank is not degrading service, it is admitting ignorance in a machine-readable way.

### The non-randomised escape hatch

There is one line of work that estimates counterfactual policy values under *fully deterministic* loggers, and it is directly applicable to this system's thresholds. Narita, Okumura, Shimizu & Yata ("Off-Policy Evaluation with General Logging Policies", KDD 2021 BCIRWIS workshop; **B/C** — workshop paper, full text read; the related peer-reviewed estimator is Narita, Yasui & Yata, RecSys 2021) define an **Approximate Propensity Score**: the average probability the logging policy chooses an action over a shrinking neighbourhood around the context. When the logger is deterministic, the sub-sample with APS strictly between 0 and 1 consists of learners *near a decision boundary*, and local reward differences there identify counterfactual values; the estimator is consistent whether the logger is stochastic or deterministic. This is regression discontinuity wearing OPE clothing, and it is why §7 matters more here than it would for a stochastic system. It is also strictly weaker: it identifies effects locally at boundaries, needs a large mass of learners near each boundary, and requires you to be able to *recompute the logging policy's decision function* offline — which again requires the decision-time state snapshot and policy version.

## Findings

### 1. Off-policy / counterfactual policy evaluation

All estimators below assume, at minimum, that logged decisions are the unit of analysis, rewards are attributable to individual decisions, and the environment is stationary between logging and evaluation. Non-stationarity is a first-order concern for a product whose content and models are changing weekly (**A**; Agarwal et al.'s Decision Service explicitly resets or holds learning rates to cope, e.g. a daily reset for MSN).

| Estimator | Form (informal) | Data required at decision time | Assumption that can break it | Variance behaviour |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Direct method / model-based** | fit r̂(x,a), then average r̂(x, π(x)) | context features, action, reward; **no propensity** | reward-model misspecification → bias that does not vanish with data | low variance, uncontrolled bias (**A**, Dudík et al. ICML 2011) |
| **IPS / IPW** | mean of r·π(a\|x)/π₀(a\|x) | context, action, reward, **exact logging propensity** | positivity; correct propensities | unbiased; variance ∝ E[w²r²]; unbounded if p_min → 0 |
| **Capped / clipped IPS** | clip w at M | as IPS, plus a chosen M | clipping is **always a downward bias** with non-negative rewards, i.e. a lower bound on true value (**A**, Lichtenberg et al.) | variance falls with M; Ionides (*JCGS* 2008) shows truncation lowers MSE and is less sensitive to a bad proposal, with truncation at rate n^(1/2) "a good general choice" and an adaptive threshold via minimum unbiased risk (**A**, abstract + quoted passages) |
| **SNIPS / self-normalised IPS** | Σwr / Σw | as IPS | still needs positivity | biased but consistent, typically lower variance, equivariant; **required** if you ever *learn* a policy from logs, because plain IPS ERM suffers "propensity overfitting" and degenerates (**A**, Swaminathan & Joachims NeurIPS 2015) |
| **Doubly robust** | r̂ + w·(r − r̂) | context, action, reward, propensity, **plus** a reward model | unbiased if *either* the reward model or the propensity model is good | Dudík, Langford & Li report DR reduces RMSE by **10–20%** vs IPS, **13.6% on average**, with the gain coming from variance (**A**, abstract + results passage; I did not read the full experimental section) |
| **DR with shrinkage** | shrink weights to minimise an MSE bound | as DR | choice of shrinkage | Su et al. (ICML 2020) report adaptivity and typically better than prior art (**A** for existence, abstract only) |
| **Trajectory IS / sequential DR** | product of per-step weights | full trajectory with per-step propensities | positivity at every step | variance exponential in horizon (**A**, Jiang & Li) — **do not use at 330 decisions** |
| **APS / boundary-local** | local comparison at decision boundaries | context, action, reward, **and the ability to recompute π₀'s decision rule** | smoothness at the boundary, mass near boundaries | consistent under deterministic logging; local estimand only (**B/C**) |

The practical reading for a small team: **log what IPS needs, report SNIPS and clipped IPS side by side as a bias-direction sandwich, add DR once you have a decent reward model, and treat the direct method alone as a hypothesis generator, never as evidence.** Clipped IPS gives you a defensible *lower* bound on a candidate policy's value; the direct method gives you an optimistic point estimate; if they disagree, you have learned that you cannot answer the question with the data you have.

### 2. The determinism problem

Covered in its own section above. One additional consequence deserves its own line, because it bites in a way teams do not anticipate: **off-policy policy *selection* is unfair even when the estimator is unbiased.** Doroudi, Thomas & Brunskill (UAI 2017; abridged in IJCAI 2018) show that IS-based policy selection can return the worse of two policies more than half the time, and that it is *systematically* biased toward policies producing shorter trajectories; they give sufficient conditions for fairness and a per-horizon weighted IS estimator to mitigate the trajectory-length source (**A**, full text of abstract and introduction read). Direct implication here: a policy that ends sessions sooner, or that pushes learners through fewer activities, will be flattered by naive IS relative to one that keeps learners working longer. Since Today's Path operates under a learner time budget and different policies will produce different daily throughput, **you must compare policies at matched horizons or matched time budgets, and you must log actual time-on-task per activity.**

### 3. Propensity logging

Field-by-field schema is in §"Log schema". The conceptual points:

- The Decision Service paper (Agarwal et al., 2016/2017; **A**, full text read) names the failure modes from real production deployments, and they are exactly the ones this codebase is structurally exposed to. Their logged tuple is (x, a, r, p) and their observation is blunt: "Recording p enables unbiased policy learning." Their enumerated corruptions:
  - **(F1) partial feedback and bias** — a system that does not explore reinforces its own choices and "is unable to evaluate actions [it does] not pick"; delayed rewards bias toward actions with faster feedback (hence their uniform release delay before data reaches the learner).
  - **(F2) incorrect data collection** — features stored as *references to database rows updated by a separate process*, so values at learning time differ from decision time; downstream business logic overriding the chosen action so that "the probabilities p correspond to the choice of a, not the recorded action, and are thus incorrect"; propensities accidentally leaking into the context and being used as a feature.
  - **(F3) non-stationarity**; **(F4) weak monitoring** — "the ability to fully reproduce an online run offline is essential".
- Their production deployments delivered 14–25% (elsewhere stated 25–30%) lifts on target metrics against strong baselines with this discipline in place (**A** for the reported figures; non-education domains, so **C** for transfer).

Fields that **cannot** be reconstructed after the fact, and why, ranked by how badly their absence hurts:

1. **The candidate set.** It is a function of item inventory, FSRS due dates, variety-constraint counters, remaining time budget, and eligibility filters *at that instant*. Every one of those mutates. Without it you cannot compute a propensity for any action, and you cannot even tell whether an alternative was available.
2. **The propensity of the chosen action** and the full distribution over the candidate set. Recomputing it later requires the exact scoring function, exact model parameters, and exact state — i.e. it requires all of 1, 3, 5, 6 to have been logged anyway.
3. **The decision-time state snapshot** (BKT posterior per relevant KC, FSRS stability/difficulty/due, elapsed-time and decay inputs, budget remaining, constraint counters). If learner state is stored as mutable current-value rows, this is gone the moment the next event lands.
4. **Delivered vs decided.** Whether the activity was actually surfaced, started, abandoned, or overridden by the learner or by a downstream rule. This is (F2) and it silently inverts the meaning of every propensity.
5. **The RNG seed and algorithm**, plus the draw index. Without it you cannot replay; with it, replay becomes a CI test.
6. **The policy and model version identity** — code SHA plus a content hash of the scoring config, BKT parameter set, FSRS parameter set, item bank, and KC map. Git history is not enough if any of these live in a mutable table.
7. **Time-on-task per activity** (wall-clock and active time distinguished). See §6 for why this is load-bearing rather than nice-to-have.

Fields that *are* reconstructable and therefore lower priority: aggregate session metadata, cohort/segment labels derivable from stable attributes, anything you can recompute from an immutable event stream.

### 4. Experiment infrastructure for a learning product

**Unit of randomisation.** Randomise at the *learner* level for anything that changes the trajectory. A spaced-repetition scheduler's whole mechanism is carryover: today's choice changes tomorrow's due dates and tomorrow's candidate set. Session-level or decision-level randomisation therefore contaminates both arms within the same learner.

**Why switchback designs do not rescue this.** Bojinov, Simchi-Levi & Zhao (*Management Science* 2023) derive optimal switchback designs as a function of the *order of the carryover effect* — how long a treatment persists in affecting outcomes — and show the optimal randomisation probability is 1/2 given carryover of order m (**A** for the framework, abstract and quoted passages read; I did not read the full derivations). The design requires switching intervals longer than the carryover horizon. For spaced repetition the carryover horizon *is* the retention interval — days to weeks — so over an 8-week prep window you get perhaps 2–4 periods per learner. That is not an experiment; it is a before/after comparison with seasonality. Switchback is the right tool for ride-hailing pricing and the wrong tool here (**B**, my inference from their stated requirement).

**Per-decision randomisation.** Attractive because the unit count explodes (≈67,000 decisions vs 200 learners). Three problems: (a) the estimand changes — you are measuring a per-decision effect on a short-horizon proxy, not a per-learner effect on exam performance; (b) standard errors must be clustered at the learner level, which claws back most of the apparent n; (c) under a fixed daily time budget, giving a learner more of X necessarily gives them less of Y, so the "control" condition is contaminated by design — treatment arms compete for the same finite inventory of learner minutes. Use per-decision randomisation for *within-state ordering* questions ("given identical state, is item A or item B the better next probe?") and learner-level randomisation for *policy* questions.

**Interleaving.** The information-retrieval analogue of per-decision randomisation, and the sensitivity numbers are genuinely striking: Schuth et al. (SIGIR 2015; 38 large-scale experiments, >3 billion clicks) report team-draft interleaving sensitivity **one to two orders of magnitude** above A/B tests (**A**, abstract read). Airbnb engineering reports ~50× sensitivity and 82% agreement with A/B outcomes, with one ranker reaching the same conclusion using 4% of A/B traffic (**C**, company blog). But the same SIGIR paper reports that agreement between interleaving and user-satisfaction A/B metrics ranges "from random up to 76%" before their credit-function fix — high sensitivity, questionable validity. In an education product with a fixed time budget and multi-day carryover, validity would be worse than in search. Verdict: an interleaving-style design is defensible for narrow item-selection questions where the two policies' choices are exchangeable within a single session, and indefensible as a substitute for policy-level evaluation (**B**).

**Sample ratio mismatch.** Fabijan et al. (KDD 2019) is the reference taxonomy: a χ² test on variant counts, with an SRM in most cases *completely invalidating* results, and root-causing typically much harder than detection (**A**, full text read). Their illustration of the test's power: a 50.2/49.8 split on 821,588 vs 815,482 users has probability under 1 in 500,000 of arising by chance. Their worked failures are instructive and directly transferable: a bucketing bug that surfaced as an SRM in an *A/A* test; a bot-detection filter that removed the treatment group's most engaged users during log processing, flipping the sign of the result. **The uncomfortable implication for a 200-learner cohort is that the χ² test has essentially no power** — a 55/45 split at n = 200 is unremarkable. So SRM protection here cannot be statistical. It must be structural: log the assignment event separately from the exposure event, assert assignment invariants in code, run an A/A test before the first real test, and never filter learners out of an analysis with a rule you have not applied identically to both arms.

**Sequential and anytime-valid testing.** A small team will peek; plan for it rather than moralising about it.
- *Always-valid p-values* (Johari, Pekelis & Walsh; KDD 2017, journal version in *Operations Research*; deployed as Optimizely's Stats Engine) give p-values and confidence intervals valid at any stopping time, constructed from a mixture sequential probability ratio test, with reported improvements in detection performance over fixed-horizon methods on Optimizely's own data (**A**, abstract and method section read; I did not read the simulation study).
- *Group-sequential designs* with O'Brien–Fleming boundaries are the cheaper, more conventional option: early boundaries are very conservative (critical z ≈ 4 at 25% information), the final boundary is near 1.96, and the maximum sample size inflation over a fixed design is small — reported as ≈1.03 by Tsiatis, and tabulated (Jennison & Turnbull) as 1.008, 1.017, 1.024, 1.028 for 2, 3, 4, 5 looks respectively (**B** — the exact table came to me via secondary sources citing Jennison & Turnbull rather than the book itself).
- **Recommendation:** pre-register one primary metric and a guardrail set; use O'Brien–Fleming with three planned looks (1.7% inflation) for any decision test; publish an always-valid interval on the internal dashboard for continuous monitoring. Note honestly that at launch scale you are operating a *"did we break something"* guardrail regime, not a discovery regime.

**The power arithmetic that governs everything else** *[own calculation]*, using the standard two-sample formula n_per_arm ≈ 2(z_{0.975} + z_{0.80})²/d² = 15.7/d², and AAMC's published MCAT total-score dispersion: mean 500.6, SD 11.2 for all test takers (percentile ranks effective May 2026, **A**); SD 9.9 for applicants and 6.7 for matriculants (AAMC Table A-16, **A**).

| True effect on MCAT total | Cohen's d (SD 9.9) | n per arm, 80% power | n per arm if only 40% report scores |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 scaled point | 0.101 | ≈1,540 | ≈3,850 |
| 2 points | 0.202 | ≈385 | ≈960 |
| 3 points | 0.303 | ≈171 | ≈430 |
| 5 points | 0.505 | ≈62 | ≈155 |

Read this table as the strategic constraint: **the launch cohort will not measure exam-score effects of a plausible size.** A scheduler improvement worth 1–2 MCAT points — which would be a commercially transformative product claim — requires thousands of learners per arm plus near-complete score reporting. This is not an argument against building the instrumentation; it is the argument *for* it, because logs accumulate across cohorts and can be re-analysed when n eventually arrives, whereas the decisions themselves cannot be reconstructed.

### 5. Bandits and adaptive experimentation

**Does adaptive instructional-policy selection work?** The best available synthesis is Doroudi, Aleven & Brunskill, *IJAIED* 2019 (**A**, full text read). Over half of the empirical studies comparing RL-induced instructional policies to baselines found significant improvements, and among the significant ones Cohen's d was often ≥ 0.8. But success clustered in *constrained* settings — paired-associate and concept learning — and the authors conclude RL has been most successful "where it has been constrained with ideas and theories from cognitive psychology and the learning sciences", complemented by robust offline analyses that do not lean on one model's assumptions. Their own Fractions Tutor case study attempted the ambitious version — choosing among 155 activities per step — and produced null results twice, with n > 1,000 for data collection and n = 220 for the second experiment.

**The tension between earning reward and gathering evaluable data** is quantified by Rafferty, Williams & Ying (*JEDM* 2019), whose numbers are in the determinism section: bandit assignment gets more students into the better condition (in 93% of large-effect simulations, at least two-thirds of participants went to the better arm, vs 1% under uniform) at the cost of roughly halving power and inflating false positives under temporal drift.

**The statistical hazard of adaptively collected data**, and the standard corrections:
- Ordinary least squares is **not** asymptotically normal on data collected by standard bandit algorithms when there is no unique optimal arm, which inflates Type I error and produces below-nominal coverage (**A**; Zhang, Janson & Murphy, "Inference for Batched Bandits", NeurIPS 2020, abstract and discussion read). Villar et al. (2015) and Rafferty et al. (2019) demonstrated the inflated Type I error empirically.
- Corrections all share the theme of *adaptive weighting*: W-decorrelation, which converts bias into variance and needs only coarse-grained knowledge of the collection policy rather than propensities (Deshpande, Mackey, Syrgkanis & Taddy, ICML 2018, **A**, abstract read); Batched OLS, asymptotically normal for multi-arm and contextual bandits and robust to baseline non-stationarity (Zhang et al. 2020, **A**); adaptively weighted M-estimators, extending validity to logistic regression and robust losses (Zhang, Janson & Murphy, NeurIPS 2021, **A**, full text of introduction read); Hadad et al.'s adaptive weighting.
- **Recommendation:** at 200 learners, do not run a bandit over instructional content. Fixed randomisation probabilities with logged propensities are strictly better for a small team, because they give clean inference *and* clean off-policy evaluation, and the only thing you give up is faster reward accrual on a proxy metric whose validity you have not yet established. Earning reward faster on an unvalidated proxy is not a benefit.
- If you later want adaptivity, the ed-tech tooling exists and is worth reusing rather than rebuilding: the MOOClet framework (Reza, Kim, Bhattacharjee, Rafferty & Williams, L@S 2021) implements versioned content components with pluggable assignment policies including weighted randomisation and Thompson sampling, and has been used with thousands of students (**B**, full text read; the deployments optimised student *helpfulness ratings*, which is precisely the proxy hazard in §6).

### 6. What the reward signal can be

**The problem in one line:** the true outcome is a scaled exam score months later, observed for a minority of learners who choose to report it, and no within-app signal is known to predict it.

**The hazard is not hypothetical, and the direction is not benign.**
- Soderstrom & Bjork (*Perspectives on Psychological Science* 2015; **A**, abstract read plus a secondary summary) review the learning-versus-performance dissociation: performance measured during instruction is an unreliable index of durable learning, improvements in performance can fail to yield learning, and *certain manipulations have opposite effects on learning and performance*. The entire "desirable difficulties" literature says that the manipulations which make within-session performance look best are frequently the ones that damage retention.
- **The documented case you asked for.** Lomas, Patel, Forlizzi & Koedinger (CHI 2013; **A**, abstract and results passages read) ran two large factorial experiments in an online maths game (10,000 and 70,000 subjects). Easier conditions were *more* engaging — subjects played longer in almost all cases, contradicting the inverted-U hypothesis — and, in the authors' own word, "troublingly", **the most engaging design conditions produced the slowest rates of learning.** A system that optimised the engagement proxy in that game would have systematically selected the worse instructional configurations. This is the clearest published instance of proxy optimisation in a learning system pointing away from learning.
- The same failure mode is documented industrially in non-education products: Dmitriev, Frasca, Gupta, Kohavi & Vaz (IEEE BigData 2016; **A**, full text of the discussion read) note that degrading search results *increases* short-term query share while increasing abandonment and reducing long-term value, and that short-term metric changes may not predict long-term impact. Their catalogue of long-term-experiment pitfalls — cookie/identity instability, survivorship bias, selection bias, perceived trends that reverse under segmentation even at 30M users, and side effects from personalisation state that persist after the experiment ends — applies to any attempt here to measure a scheduler over a multi-week horizon.
- **Next-item correctness is the worst available proxy for this system specifically**, because the adaptive practice selector already has explicit target success rates. Any policy can raise next-item correctness by choosing easier items; the metric is trivially gameable in the exact direction the pedagogy says is wrong. Session completion and streak metrics are only marginally better. (**A** by combination of the above.)

**What is defensible.**
- **Surrogate-outcome theory gives the formal condition.** Athey, Chetty, Imbens & Kang (*Review of Economic Studies*; **A**, abstract read, plus the working paper's statement of assumptions) show that under the Prentice surrogacy assumption — the long-term outcome is independent of treatment conditional on the surrogates — the average treatment effect on a *surrogate index* (the predicted long-term outcome given short-term outcomes) equals the effect on the long-term outcome. They characterise the bias from violations and give validation procedures using additional observed outcomes. In their job-training application, six quarters of short-term surrogates recovered nine-year employment impacts with a **35% reduction in standard errors**. The critical operational requirement: **building a surrogate index requires a cohort for which you observe both the surrogates and the primary outcome.** If no launch cohort ever yields exam scores, this machinery is permanently unavailable.
- **A defensible reward hierarchy for this product**, in descending order of validity and ascending order of availability:
  1. Self-reported real MCAT total and section scores with test date (few learners, highest validity — the anchor for any surrogate index).
  2. Full-length practice-exam scaled-score deltas, ideally on forms reserved from the practice pool.
  3. **Delayed retention probes**: correctness on a KC-tagged, previously-unseen item after a controlled gap of ≥ N days since last exposure to that KC. This is within-app, cheap, and — unlike next-item correctness — it measures the thing spaced repetition claims to produce. To make it valid you must *reserve* probe items and *control* the gap, which means the probe scheduler must be partly randomised too.
  4. Learning-curve slope per KC (error rate as a function of practice opportunities), with the well-known caveat that it is model-dependent.
  5. Time-on-task and throughput, treated as an outcome *dimension*, not a reward.
  6. Never as primary: next-item correctness, session completion, streaks, DAU, time-in-app.
- **The industry precedent.** Duolingo built "Time Spent Learning Well" precisely because formal efficacy studies are too slow for daily A/B testing, and pairs it with a guardrail regime in which negative movement on long-term engagement metrics requires senior review (**C**, company blog/LinkedIn posts). Their CTO's stated soft threshold for A/B testing being meaningful at all is around 100,000 users (**C**, secondary write-up of a talk). Take the *structure* from this — a deliberately constructed proxy, validated against slower efficacy research, with guardrails — and not the specific metric.
- **The reserved-item cost is real and must be decided now.** With 2,108 items across 422 KCs (≈5 items/KC), reserving even one probe item per KC costs 20% of the item bank, and reserving two costs 40%. Once an item is used in normal practice its difficulty statistics and its value as a clean probe are contaminated. This is an irreversible content decision, not a logging decision, and it is the least reversible item on the checklist that is not about logging.

### 7. Non-experimental alternatives

**Regression discontinuity.** The 0.95 mastery threshold and item-count cutoffs are genuine discontinuities, and the APS approach in §"determinism problem" is essentially RD applied to OPE. Four honest caveats:
- **Cost in sample size.** The RD design effect — how many times the RCT sample you need for equal precision — is 2.75 for a normally distributed score around the cutoff and 4 for a uniform one, rising to ~5 for bimodal scores (Goldberger 1972; Schochet 2008 for education; **A**). Deke & Dragoset, using four education studies covering >25,000 students, find that once optimal bandwidth selection and adjustment for random misspecification error are accounted for, an RD study needs **9 to 17 times** as many students or schools as an RCT, driven primarily by bandwidth selection discarding roughly half the observations (**A**, abstract and results passage read). Composed with the power table in §4, RD on exam outcomes is out of reach by roughly two orders of magnitude.
- **The assignment variable is your own model output.** Mastery is a BKT posterior computed from the learner's response history — exactly the history that predicts the outcome. This makes it a *fuzzy* RD at best, and manipulable in the sense that matters: the scheduler itself piles learners at the threshold, so a McCrary density test on the forcing variable will very likely detect a discontinuity in density and correctly reject the design.
- **The estimand is local.** RD at 0.95 identifies the effect of crossing the mastery threshold *for learners at 0.95*, which is a useful question ("is 0.95 the right threshold?") but is not the policy question ("is this scheduler better?").
- **Where it is genuinely worth doing:** threshold-calibration questions. "Should the mastery threshold be 0.92 or 0.95?" is exactly an RD question, it is decision-relevant, and the discontinuity is already in your code. Log the forcing variable at full precision so this remains possible.

**Instrumental variables.** Requires something that shifts exposure to the treatment without affecting outcomes through any other path. Plausible candidates here are all randomised anyway (notification timing, nudge assignment, staged feature availability), which means IV is really a way of *rescuing a randomised design with imperfect compliance* rather than an alternative to randomisation. Weak-instrument bias is the standard failure and it is severe at small n. Honest verdict: not a substitute.

**Difference-in-differences on staged rollouts.** Requires a pre-period and a parallel-trends assumption. A pre-launch product has no pre-period, so this is only available for post-launch changes, and even then the learner population at launch will be changing composition faster than any plausible parallel-trends assumption tolerates. I did not search the staggered-adoption econometrics literature; the standard cautions about two-way fixed-effects estimators under staggered timing are in Goodman-Bacon (2021) and Callaway & Sant'Anna (2021) and should be consulted before relying on a staged-rollout DiD (**C** — flagged as unverified, see "What I could not verify").

**How much weaker are these, in one sentence?** RD is credible but needs 9–17× the sample and answers a narrower question; IV is really randomisation-with-compliance in disguise; DiD needs a pre-period you do not have. None of them substitute for logging propensities, and two of the three (RD-as-OPE, IV-with-compliance) *depend* on the same decision-time logs.

### 8. Beyond the brief

**8a. The most likely failure mode is not missing logs; it is a confident model-based evaluation.** This team has a fitted student model (BKT with decay) and a fitted memory model (FSRS). The natural move is to simulate learners under those models and compare candidate schedulers. The literature is unusually direct that this fails:
- Mandel et al. (AAMAS 2014) prove by counterexample that **expected cumulative reward under a fitted model is a biased and inconsistent estimator of policy value** when used to select across state-space representations — wrong even with infinite data (**A**, full text of the appendix counterexample read).
- Doroudi et al.'s remedy is the **robust evaluation matrix**: simulate each candidate policy under *multiple* plausible student models fit to the same data, and only trust a policy that wins under all of them (**A**).
- And then their own case study shows even REM failing, in a way that maps onto this system exactly. REM predicted their new adaptive policy would beat the baseline by **0.31 to 2.23 points on a 16-point posttest**, with most models predicting ≥1 point. The actual experiment (n = 220) found the baseline did as well or better. The diagnosed cause: the models mis-predicted **time per problem**, so baseline students completed ~48 problems while other policies' students completed ≤28. More throughput compensated for less adaptivity (**A**, full text read).
- **The engineering requirement that follows is specific and unobvious: log actual per-activity time-on-task, and treat throughput under the time budget as a first-class evaluation dimension.** Today's Path is explicitly a time-budgeted planner. Any offline comparison of scheduling policies that does not model how many activities each policy actually gets completed within the budget will mis-rank policies, and the published precedent for this exact error is an ITS with a time-budget-free simulator. This is, in my judgement, the highest-value non-obvious finding in this review.

**8b. Safety guarantees are out of reach; say so now rather than promising them.** Thomas, Theocharous & Ghavamzadeh (AAAI 2015) compute high-confidence lower bounds on a new policy's value from logged data. On a website with several hundred thousand daily visitors, they needed **2 million trajectories** for a 95% lower bound of 0.077 against a behaviour policy at 0.0765 — a bound so tight to the baseline that they judged it might not warrant deployment — then 5 million for 0.0803, then 6 million to support continued deployment (**A**, full text of the experiments read). A safe-policy-improvement guarantee is a 10⁶-trajectory instrument. Do not scope one.

**8c. Two cheap artefacts that pay for themselves immediately.**
- **Decision replay test in CI.** Given a logged state snapshot, seed, and policy version, re-running the scheduler must reproduce the logged decision exactly. This is the operational definition of the reproducibility that Agarwal et al. call "essential to effectively debug learning" (**A**), it catches mutable-state leakage (their F2) at commit time, and it is the precondition for every offline analysis in this document.
- **The Σw/n → 1 invariant.** For any candidate policy, the mean importance weight over the logs must be ≈1 (**A**, Swaminathan & Joachims). Assert it. A team that ships this one check will discover broken propensity logging in days rather than in a year, when the logs are the only thing left.

**8d. Guard against the reverse of the exploration objection.** The strongest form of the "don't give learners worse activities" argument is not statistical, it is that a paying pre-med with an exam date has no tolerance for experimentation. The correct response is not to abandon randomisation but to *scope it by stakes*: randomise freely among near-tied candidates and among probe scheduling gaps; never randomise the prerequisite ordering, the diagnostic's measurement properties, or anything a learner would recognise as a degraded product. Doroudi et al.'s semi-randomised design is precisely this compromise, and it is the published precedent (**A**).

**8e. A note on what the diagnostic's seeded randomisation is worth.** 140 items at onboarding, multistage, with seeded tie-breaking, is a nearly ideal OPE substrate *if* the propensities are logged: the horizon is short (so trajectory IS is actually viable at this one place in the product), the reward (measurement accuracy against a later full-length practice exam) is comparatively well defined, and the action space is small at each stage. If you want one place to demonstrate the whole off-policy evaluation pipeline end to end before betting on it, this is it.

## Log schema

One row per scheduling decision, append-only, written *at decision time* — not derived later from state. `R?` = reconstructable after the fact.

| Field | What it is | What breaks if absent | R? |
|---|---|---|---|
| `decision_id` | UUID, stable | cannot join reward to decision; cannot deduplicate | No |
| `learner_id` | stable pseudonymous ID, never recycled | no clustering of standard errors; no per-learner outcome join; identity churn silently biases long-horizon analysis (Dmitriev et al.) | No |
| `decided_at` | server timestamp, monotonic | cannot order decisions; cannot compute FSRS gaps or reward-delay bias | No |
| `session_id`, `decision_index_in_day` | position within the day's plan | cannot control for slot effects or fatigue; cannot match horizons for fair IS comparison (Doroudi et al. 2017) | Partly |
| `policy_id` + `policy_version` | code SHA + content hash of scoring config | estimates pool incompatible policies; you cannot say *which* policy the log describes | No |
| `model_versions` | hashes for BKT params, FSRS params, item bank, KC map | scores/propensities cannot be recomputed; replay impossible | No |
| `candidate_set` | ordered list of eligible action IDs with type (lesson / practice / flashcard / mistake-review) | **propensity undefined for every action; cannot tell whether an alternative even existed** — the single most damaging omission | No |
| `filtered_out` | candidates excluded pre-scoring, each with a reason code | you cannot distinguish "scored low" from "structurally ineligible", which changes the target policy class you may legitimately evaluate | No |
| `scores[]` | raw score per candidate, full precision | cannot reproduce the ranking; cannot define a tolerance band retrospectively; RD forcing variable lost | No |
| `probabilities[]` | sampling probability per candidate, summing to 1 | **IPS/SNIPS/DR all undefined**; Σw/n invariant untestable | No |
| `chosen_action`, `chosen_propensity` | the sampled action and its probability | no importance weight; the estimator degenerates to the direct method | No |
| `sampling_rule` | `argmax` \| `softmax(τ)` \| `band(δ, k)`, with parameters | cannot audit whether support existed; cannot interpret weights | No |
| `rng_seed`, `rng_algorithm`, `draw_index` | exact randomness provenance | no replay; no CI determinism test; cannot debug a suspected assignment bug | No |
| `state_snapshot` | the exact values consumed: BKT posterior per candidate KC, decay elapsed-time inputs, FSRS stability/difficulty/due per card, minutes of budget remaining, variety-constraint counters, mistake-queue depth | propensities cannot be recomputed; the direct-method reward model has no features; Agarwal et al.'s F2 (mutable feature references) realised in full | **No** — gone the moment state is overwritten |
| `binding_constraints` | which variety/budget constraints were active and binding | a policy comparison confounded by constraints will look like a scoring difference | No |
| `delivered` | was the activity actually surfaced | logged propensity attaches to a decision that never happened | No |
| `started_at`, `completed_at`, `active_seconds` | time-on-task, wall-clock and active distinguished | **throughput mis-modelled → policies mis-ranked**, the documented Fractions Tutor failure | No |
| `override` | learner navigated elsewhere, or a downstream rule replaced the action | propensity corresponds to the intended action, not the recorded one — Agarwal et al.'s canonical F2 corruption | No |
| `outcome_ids`, `outcome_observed_at` | the responses/events attributed to this decision, with delay | cannot join reward; variable reward delay biases toward fast-feedback actions (F1) | Partly |
| `experiment_assignments` | arm memberships at decision time, logged separately from exposure | SRM undiagnosable; contamination invisible | No |
| `probe_flag`, `probe_gap_days` | whether this was a reserved retention probe and the realised gap | the only retention-valid within-app reward becomes unusable | No |

Non-negotiable properties of the log itself: append-only; no updates or deletes; written in the same transaction as the decision (or via an outbox, never best-effort fire-and-forget); schema-versioned; and retained for at least the length of the longest plausible outcome horizon plus one year.

One row per **outcome report**, separately: `learner_id`, `exam_date`, `total_score`, `section_scores`, `source` (self-report / verified), `reported_at`, `consent_version`. Without this table nothing else in the document can ever be validated.

## Pre-launch checklist

Ordered by irreversibility of absence — how much is permanently lost if it is missing on day one.

**Tier 1 — permanently unrecoverable.**

1. **Append-only decision log** with candidate set, per-candidate scores and probabilities, chosen action and propensity, sampling rule, seed, policy and model version hashes. Cannot be backfilled by any means.
2. **Non-degenerate action selection** on Today's Path and adaptive practice: tolerance-band sampling with an explicit δ, or softmax with a propensity floor. A deterministic launch cohort produces logs with support divergence up to 1 for every alternative policy, and the guaranteed-suboptimality bound of Sachdeva et al.'s Theorem 1 does not shrink with data.
3. **Event-sourced learner state.** BKT posteriors, FSRS card fields, budget and variety counters stored as an append-only series with the decision-time values snapshotted into the decision row. Overwriting is the single most common way teams destroy this data without noticing.
4. **Stable, never-recycled learner ID**, with assignment and exposure logged as separate events.
5. **Reserved probe items per KC**, excluded from normal scheduling, plus controlled probe gaps. At ≈5 items/KC this is a costly and irreversible content decision — decide it before content is in front of learners.
6. **Outcome-collection consent and mechanism**: exam date and score self-report, requested at onboarding and prompted after the test date. Without one cohort that has both proxies and exam scores, no proxy is ever validatable and the surrogate-index approach is unavailable forever.
7. **Per-activity time-on-task**, wall-clock and active time distinguished.

**Tier 2 — recoverable only by discarding the affected cohort.**

8. **Delivered-vs-decided and override logging.** Retrofittable in code, but every decision logged before the fix has ambiguous semantics.
9. **Full-precision forcing variables** (mastery posterior, item counts) at every threshold, so RD-style and APS-style analyses stay possible.
10. **Reward-delay discipline**: a uniform release delay before joined data is used for any learning or evaluation, per Agarwal et al.'s Join Service, so fast-feedback actions are not systematically favoured.
11. **Model and config version registry** with content hashes, not just git history, for anything stored in a mutable table.

**Tier 3 — cheap now, cheap later; build now because it is hours of work.**

12. **Decision replay test in CI**: state snapshot + seed + policy version reproduces the logged decision exactly.
13. **Σw/n ≈ 1 invariant check** plus an ESS report, run over the logs for any candidate policy.
14. **A/A test** before the first real experiment, and structural assignment invariants asserted in code (χ² SRM detection has no power at this cohort size).
15. **Pre-registered primary metric and guardrail set**, with O'Brien–Fleming boundaries at three looks (≈1.7% sample-size inflation) or an always-valid interval on the dashboard.
16. **An A/B assignment framework.** Genuinely last. It is fully retrofittable, and prioritising it over items 1–7 is the most common and most costly sequencing error available to this team.

**Explicitly not on the list:** contextual bandit infrastructure, high-confidence off-policy evaluation, switchback designs, an interleaving framework, a bespoke experimentation platform. Each requires 10²–10⁶× the launch cohort's data to earn its keep.

## DOK 1 — established facts

- IPS is unbiased if and only if the logging policy has full support for the target policy: π₀(a|x) > 0 wherever π(a|x) > 0 (**A**, Sachdeva et al. KDD 2020, Def. 1).
- Under support deficiency the IPS bias equals the expected reward on unsupported actions; ERM using IPS can return a policy at least (r_max − r_min)·max_π D_X(π|π₀) suboptimal *in the infinite-data limit* (**A**, ibid., Prop. 1 and Thm. 1).
- Deterministic logging is the extreme case of deficient support (**A**).
- Of three remedies — restricting the action space, reward extrapolation, restricting the policy space — the authors conclude policy-space restriction is most effective: explicit risk control, robust performance, computationally cheap (**A**).
- Clipped IPS with non-negative rewards is *always* downward biased, hence a lower bound on true policy value (**A**, Lichtenberg et al.).
- Truncating importance weights lowers MSE and reduces sensitivity to a poor proposal; truncation at rate n^(1/2) is a good general default (**A**, Ionides, *JCGS* 2008).
- SNIPS is biased, consistent, equivariant, usually lower variance, and necessary to avoid propensity overfitting when *learning* from logs (**A**, Swaminathan & Joachims, NeurIPS 2015). The weight-sum control variate has expectation 1 for every policy in the class.
- Doubly robust is unbiased if either the reward model or the propensity model is good; DR reduced RMSE 10–20% versus IPS, 13.6% on average, via variance reduction (**A**, Dudík, Langford & Li, ICML 2011).
- Importance-sampling variance grows exponentially in horizon; this is an inherent hardness result matching a Cramér–Rao bound, not an estimator artefact (**A**, Jiang & Li, ICML 2016).
- IS-based policy *selection* can be unfair, returning the worse of two policies more than half the time, and systematically favours shorter trajectories (**A**, Doroudi, Thomas & Brunskill, UAI 2017).
- Expected cumulative reward under a fitted student model is a biased and inconsistent estimator of policy value across representations (**A**, Mandel et al., AAMAS 2014, appendix counterexample).
- With uniform logging over K arms, the replay estimator retains 1/K of events; O(KT) logged events yield T usable ones (**A**, Li et al., WSDM 2011, Thm. 1).
- High-confidence off-policy evaluation required 2M trajectories for a marginal 95% lower bound and 6M to support continued deployment on a site with hundreds of thousands of daily visitors (**A**, Thomas et al., AAAI 2015).
- OLS is not asymptotically normal on standard bandit-collected data absent a unique optimal arm; Type I error inflates and coverage falls below nominal. Corrections: W-decorrelation, Batched OLS, adaptively weighted M-estimators (**A**).
- Bandit assignment in simulated educational experiments cut power from 0.80 to 0.57 (binary) and 0.49 (normal); ≥2× participants needed; effect sizes overestimated (0.2 → 0.30); false positives inflate under temporal drift (**A**, Rafferty, Williams & Ying, *JEDM* 2019).
- Live randomisation of Bing mainline reserves (log-normal, σ = 0.3, 95% of multipliers in [0.52, 1.74]) over 22M page views produced a small significant rise in ads/page and no significant change in click yield or revenue versus an unrandomised control (**A**, Bottou et al., *JMLR* 2013).
- In an educational game, uniform-random concept sequencing was statistically indistinguishable from an expert-designed policy (p = 0.389); the offline-selected adaptive policy beat both by 32% at n = 2,000 (**B**, Mandel et al., AAMAS 2014).
- Over half of empirical studies comparing RL-induced instructional policies to baselines found significant gains, often with d ≥ 0.8 among the significant ones, but success concentrated in constrained paired-associate and concept-learning settings (**A**, Doroudi, Aleven & Brunskill, *IJAIED* 2019).
- Robust-evaluation-matrix predictions of +0.31 to +2.23 points on a 16-point posttest were contradicted by the experiment (n = 220); the diagnosed cause was mis-modelled time per problem, with the baseline completing ~48 problems versus ≤28 for the alternatives (**A**, ibid., Appendix B).
- In two large factorial experiments (10K and 70K subjects) the most engaging game conditions produced the slowest learning rates (**A**, Lomas et al., CHI 2013).
- Performance during instruction is an unreliable index of learning; some manipulations have opposite effects on the two (**A**, Soderstrom & Bjork, 2015 — abstract read).
- Under Prentice surrogacy, the ATE on a surrogate index equals the ATE on the long-term outcome; six quarters of surrogates recovered nine-year impacts with 35% smaller standard errors (**A**, Athey, Chetty, Imbens & Kang, *ReStud*).
- Sample ratio mismatch usually invalidates results entirely; a 50.2/49.8 split on ~1.64M users has probability under 1 in 500,000 by chance; documented causes include a bucketing bug visible in an A/A test and bot-filtering that removed the treatment's most engaged users (**A**, Fabijan et al., KDD 2019).
- Always-valid p-values from an mSPRT permit continuous monitoring with valid inference at any stopping time (**A**, Johari, Pekelis & Walsh, KDD 2017 / *Operations Research*).
- O'Brien–Fleming group-sequential maximum sample size inflation is ≈1.008/1.017/1.024/1.028 for 2/3/4/5 looks (**B** — Jennison & Turnbull tables via secondary sources; Tsiatis's ≈1.03 corroborates).
- RD design effects relative to an RCT: 2.75 (normal forcing variable), 4 (uniform), up to ~5 (bimodal); 9–17× once optimal bandwidth selection and misspecification adjustment are included in education applications (**A**, Schochet 2008; Deke & Dragoset).
- Team-draft interleaving is one to two orders of magnitude more sensitive than A/B tests, but its agreement with satisfaction A/B metrics ranged from chance to 76% (**A**, Schuth et al., 38 experiments, >3B clicks).
- Optimal switchback design depends on the order of the carryover effect; the optimal randomisation probability is 1/2 (**A**, Bojinov, Simchi-Levi & Zhao, *Management Science* 2023).
- MCAT total scores: mean 500.6, SD 11.2 across all test takers; SD 9.9 for applicants, 6.7 for matriculants (**A**, AAMC).
- Production contextual-bandit deployments with correct explore/log discipline achieved 14–25% (reported elsewhere as 25–30%) lifts against strong baselines; the paper's enumerated failure modes are partial feedback, incorrect data collection, non-stationarity, and weak reproducibility (**A**, Agarwal et al., Decision Service).
- An estimator using approximate propensity scores is consistent for counterfactual policy values whether the logging policy is stochastic or deterministic, by exploiting local variation at decision boundaries (**B/C**, Narita et al., KDD 2021 workshop).

## DOK 2 — insights and implications

1. **The determinism problem is an identification problem, not a data-volume problem, and that changes the engineering priority.** Teams instinctively treat evaluability as something more logging or more users will eventually deliver. Sachdeva et al.'s Theorem 1 says the suboptimality bound survives infinite data. A deterministic launch cohort is not a small sample of the counterfactual; it is a zero sample. This is why item 2 on the checklist sits above item 16.
2. **Tolerance-band sampling is the rare intervention that improves three things at once.** Restricting exploration to near-ties simultaneously (a) bounds learner cost at δ by construction, (b) *lowers* estimator variance relative to ε-greedy because p_min rises from ε/K to 1/|band|, and (c) implements the policy-space restriction that Sachdeva et al. independently identify as the best of three remedies. The cost is a restricted evaluable policy class — which for a team that intends to tune scoring weights rather than replace the scheduler is exactly the class they care about.
3. **The exploration objection dissolves once you notice that band members are candidates you cannot rank.** If δ is set below the score resolution implied by BKT posterior uncertainty, randomising within the band is not delivering worse activities; it is declining to fabricate a ranking. That reframing is what will get the change approved.
4. **The Fractions Tutor throughput failure is this system's most likely future post-mortem.** A time-budgeted planner evaluated by a simulator that mis-predicts per-activity duration will confidently mis-rank policies, and the published instance of that error had a smaller action space and a shorter horizon than Today's Path. Time-on-task is not telemetry hygiene here; it is a load-bearing evaluation input.
5. **The proxy hazard runs in the harmful direction in this specific product.** Adaptive practice already has explicit target success rates, so "next-item correctness" can be raised by any policy willing to serve easier items — precisely the move the desirable-difficulties literature says damages retention, and precisely the pattern Lomas et al. measured at 70,000-subject scale. Optimising the obvious proxy would push the scheduler against its own pedagogy.
6. **You cannot validate a proxy without ever observing the outcome, so consent and score collection are logging requirements, not marketing requirements.** The surrogate-index result is an *identification* result conditional on a cohort with both. Skipping score collection at launch does not delay validation; it forecloses it for that cohort permanently.
7. **A/B infrastructure is the least urgent item and will be treated as the most urgent one.** It is fully retrofittable; the decision log is not. If the team builds only one thing before launch, the ranking above says it must be the decision log, and the reasoning is asymmetric reversibility rather than expected value.
8. **The horizon mathematics forces the problem framing.** Exponential IS variance in horizon rules out trajectory-level evaluation at ~330 decisions, so the entire evaluation programme must be a decision-level contextual-bandit problem with short-horizon rewards, plus a slow, separate, cohort-level validation of those rewards against exam outcomes. Any plan that treats scheduling as an RL problem to be evaluated offline is arithmetically doomed regardless of implementation quality.
9. **Small cohort size changes which safeguards work, not merely their power.** The χ² SRM test is Microsoft's canonical guardrail and it is useless at n = 200. The substitute is structural — invariant assertions, A/A tests, separate assignment and exposure events — which is cheaper but must be designed in rather than bought.
10. **The 140-item diagnostic is the ideal pilot for the whole pipeline.** Short horizon, small per-stage action space, a comparatively well-defined reward (measurement accuracy against a later full-length practice exam), and randomisation already partially present. Prove propensity logging, replay, Σw/n, ESS, and clipped-IPS-versus-direct-method sandwiching there before extending to Today's Path.
11. **The item bank is the binding physical constraint on evaluability, not the user base.** 2,108 items over 422 KCs leaves ≈5 items/KC. Reserved probes cost 20% per item held back, exposure contamination is permanent, and every retention-valid reward signal depends on holding items back. This deserves as much pre-launch attention as the logging schema and will get far less.
12. **Bandits and A/B tests are not the two options.** The third — fixed, known, logged randomisation probabilities with no adaptivity — dominates both for a pre-launch team: it yields clean frequentist inference (unlike bandits), clean off-policy evaluation of arbitrary future policies at ~log K cost in data (unlike A/B tests, which evaluate only the arms you shipped), and requires no adaptive-inference corrections. It is also the least glamorous option, which is why it needs advocating.

## What I could not verify

- **Any exploration-fraction number specific to education.** No source I found reports a recommended ε or randomisation fraction for instructional sequencing. The 0.05–0.20 range I quote comes from bandit tooling documentation (**C**), and Bottou et al.'s σ = 0.3 is an ad-auction reserve-price perturbation with no clean translation to activity selection. The δ and band-size recommendations are engineering judgement, not findings.
- **Cost of exploration in a high-stakes, deadline-driven learning product.** The two empirical cost measurements I found (Bing ads; a fractions game for 4th–5th graders) differ from MCAT prep in exactly the dimension that matters — a fixed exam date and a paying adult learner. I found no study measuring the learner cost of deliberate randomisation in high-stakes adult test preparation.
- **Whether the existing seeded diagnostic randomisation persists propensities.** I did not read application code (out of scope by instruction). If only the seed is stored, the system is replayable but not evaluable; this needs a one-line check.
- **Staggered difference-in-differences econometrics.** I did not search this literature. Goodman-Bacon (2021) and Callaway & Sant'Anna (2021) are the standard cautions about two-way fixed-effects estimators under staggered adoption and should be read before relying on a staged-rollout DiD. Treat my §7 DiD paragraph as **C**.
- **The exact Jennison & Turnbull inflation-factor table.** The values 1.008/1.017/1.024/1.028 came from calculator documentation citing that book; Tsiatis's ≈1.03 corroborates the magnitude but I did not see the primary table.
- **Full text of several papers.** Read as abstract-only or abstract-plus-quoted-passages: Su et al. (DR with shrinkage); Deshpande et al. (W-decorrelation); Soderstrom & Bjork; Athey et al. (abstract plus the working paper's assumption statement); Schuth et al.; Bojinov et al. (abstract plus quoted passages, not the derivations); Dudík et al. (abstract plus the RMSE results passage, not the full experiments); Jiang & Li (abstract plus quoted passages, not the proofs).
- **The peer-review status of arXiv 2603.21485** ("OPE for ranking policies under deterministic logging"). Slides appear on the ICLR 2026 site, which suggests acceptance, but I did not confirm. Treated as **C** throughout and its ranking-specific click-propensity remedy is not applicable to single-slot activity selection without adaptation.
- **Any published ed-tech company guidance on propensity logging.** Duolingo's public material covers proxy metric construction and experiment process, not off-policy evaluation or propensity logging. I found no ed-tech company write-up on OPE instrumentation. The instrumentation precedent I lean on (the Decision Service) is from content recommendation and advertising.
- **A documented case of an education system optimising a proxy in production and demonstrably worsening real outcomes.** Lomas et al. is the closest and is strong — it shows the proxy and the outcome moving in opposite directions in a real system at 70,000-subject scale — but the *proxy was not actually being optimised by a deployed policy*; the divergence was measured experimentally. I found no case study of a shipped learning system whose proxy-optimising policy was later shown to have reduced durable learning. If such a case exists I did not locate it, and I have not invented one.

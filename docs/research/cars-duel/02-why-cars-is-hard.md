# Why CARS is hard — and which causes students cannot self-diagnose

**Scope.** Evidence review on the cognitive sources of MCAT CARS difficulty for already-proficient adult readers, what students misdiagnose, whether CARS is trainable, and what an intervention must do. No application code was read; this document is literature only.

**Evidence tiers used inline:** **A** = strong / meta-analytic / consensus. **B** = reasonable but with population, criterion, or design limits. **C** = weak, single-source, indirect, or commercial/forum.

**Reading depth.** Noted per source in `## Sources`. I read substantial extracted full text for Yang et al. (2022), Rayner et al. (2016), Briggs (2001), Powers & Rock (1998/1999), and Kuhn (2009). Most others are abstract plus quoted result passages. Nothing below is a paraphrase of a number I did not see.

---

## Recommendation

CARS difficulty is not a speed problem and not a vocabulary problem. It is a **standards-of-coherence problem compounded by an invisible monitoring failure**. Test-takers read for gist or "search for the answer," build a text-level representation adequate for recall but not for argument structure, and then cannot detect the shortfall: readers' predicted-versus-actual comprehension correlates about **.18–.27** (**A**), i.e. barely above coin-flip discrimination. That is the one cause students structurally *cannot* self-diagnose, and it is why more volume feels productive while scores stay flat.

An intervention must therefore (1) force a **generated** commitment about the author's argument before answers are visible, (2) confront it with the **author's actual reasoning**, not a right/wrong mark, and (3) expose the **predicted-versus-actual gap externally** so mis-calibration becomes a visible datum. Full multi-question passage sets are required for calibration reasons. Producing free-form arguments on topics with no passage is the weakest-evidenced activity in this space.

## Findings

### 1. Component skills of adult reading comprehension

#### 1.1 Inference-making

**Does inference skill predict comprehension independent of vocabulary? Yes — but the load-bearing evidence is developmental, not adult.** Cain and Oakhill's longitudinal work found that inference-making, comprehension monitoring, and story-structure knowledge each **predicted unique variance in reading comprehension after controlling for word reading, vocabulary, and verbal ability**, at ages 8, 9, and 11; further, the inference–comprehension and monitoring–comprehension relations were **not wholly mediated by working memory** (Cain, Oakhill & Bryant, 2004, *JEP* 96(1):31–42). The companion dissociation analysis showed that phoneme deletion predicted word reading while text integration, metacognitive monitoring, and working memory predicted comprehension — different skills for different outcomes (Oakhill, Cain & Bryant, 2003, *LCP* 18(4):443–468). **Tier A for the claim within child samples; tier C when extrapolated to already-proficient adults.** I found no equivalent component-skills regression in a college-educated adult sample. This is a real gap for a CARS team.

**Text-connecting vs gap-filling.** Cain and Oakhill distinguish inferences that connect propositions *within* the text from inferences that fill gaps using world knowledge. Critically, Cain, Oakhill, Barnes and Bryant (2001, *Memory & Cognition* 29(6):850–859) controlled knowledge availability and concluded that **knowledge deficits are not the primary source** of poor comprehenders' inference failures — the deficit is in the inferential process itself (**B**, child sample). This maps cleanly onto CARS: the AAMC's "no outside knowledge required" design deliberately strips gap-filling-by-content and loads text-connecting inference plus principled extension. So the construct CARS targets is precisely the one that survives knowledge controls.

**Trainable, over what timescale?** Not directly established for proficient adults. The nearest evidence is self-explanation training (§3.3): reliable over hours-to-weeks, but concentrated in *lower*-knowledge readers.

#### 1.2 Comprehension monitoring / metacomprehension — **the load-bearing number**

The user's prior (~0.2–0.3) is correct, and the modern estimate is at the **bottom** of that range.

| Source | Estimate | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Maki (1998c) | **G = .27** | mean intra-individual correlation across 25 studies from her lab |
| Dunlosky & Lipko (2007) | **G = .27** | 36 published experiments from Dunlosky's lab (independently identical estimate) |
| Lin & Zabrucky (1998) | **≈ .2** | narrative review |
| Prinz et al. (2020a) | **r = .24** | meta-analysis |
| **Yang, Zhao, Yuan, Luo & Shanks (2022)** | **MC = .178, 95% CI [.155, .200]** | 502 effects, 15,889 participants, 115 studies; **non-intervention effects only** |

Yang et al. is the best current estimate and the one to use. Their overall pooled figure across all effects was **MC = .242**, but they showed that intervention conditions (**MC = .332 [.309, .356]**) and non-intervention conditions (**MC = .178**) are statistically heterogeneous, *Q*(1) = 259, *p* < .001, and argued that the classic .27 and Prinz's .24 **overestimate normal accuracy** because both pooled intervention effects in. **Tier A.**

The practical translation matters more than the coefficient. Yang et al. ran a correlation-to-accuracy simulation: at **G = .27**, the probability that a reader correctly assigns high judgments to well-comprehended texts and low judgments to poorly-comprehended ones is **56.9%** against a 50% chance baseline; at **MC = .178** it is **54.5%**. Their own illustration: a student wanting to restudy the 20 worst-understood of 40 sections would pick about **11 of the 20 correct ones and 9 wrong ones**.

Two further details are directly relevant to CARS:

- **The format does not rescue it.** Maki and colleagues obtained correlations "no greater than +.32" across fill-in-the-blank, free recall, and **four-alternative multiple-choice** criterion tests (Maki & Berry, 1984; Maki, Jonas & Kallod, 1994; Maki & Serra, 1992, as reported verbatim in Thiede & Anderson, 2003, *Contemporary Educational Psychology*). So the poor calibration is not an artifact of recall testing; it holds for the CARS-like format (**A**).
- **Skilled readers are included.** The Lancaster review of this literature explicitly frames the finding as "poor metacomprehension accuracy amongst **skilled** readers." This is not a struggling-reader phenomenon (**A**).

**Trainable?** Yes, and reasonably fast — but only by specific manipulations. From Yang et al.'s intervention table (difference in MC vs non-intervention effects):

| Intervention | Δ MC | Detectable? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Intervention **combinations** | **+0.299 to +0.316** | yes, *Z* = 12.9–13.3 |
| **Delayed** summary writing | **+0.210** | yes, *Z* = 9.00 |
| Test expectancy **consistent vs inconsistent** | **+0.207** | yes, *Z* = 4.38 |
| Concept mapping / diagramming / drawing | **+0.196** | yes, *Z* = 5.89 |
| **Delayed** keyword generation | **+0.184** | yes, *Z* = 9.87 |
| **Self-explaining** | **+0.179** | yes, *Z* = 5.08 |
| Test expectancy consistent (vs no manipulation) | +0.090 | yes, *Z* = 2.67 |
| Rereading after short delay | +0.063 | yes, *Z* = 2.56 |
| Question **generation** | +0.042 | **no**, *p* = .406 |
| **Immediate** summary writing | +0.030 | **no**, *p* = .225 |
| **Immediate** keyword generation | +0.009 | **no**, *p* = .710 |
| Keyword *reading* | **−0.096** | borderline harmful, *p* = .050 |
| Test expectancy **inconsistent** | **−0.117** | yes, *Z* = −2.34 |
| Reading questions + answers (no generation) | **−0.095** | **no**, *p* = .065 |
| Actively answering practice questions **vs** reading Q&A | **+0.264** | yes, *Z* = 5.15 |
| Single interventions (pooled) | +0.101 | yes, *Z* = 9.56 |

Three design-critical patterns fall straight out (**A** for the meta-analytic estimates; the design inferences are **B**):

1. **Delay is the active ingredient.** Immediate summarising and immediate keyword generation do nothing (+0.030, +0.009, both ns); delaying the same activity produces the two largest single effects (+0.210, +0.184). Mechanism per Yang et al.: immediately after reading, surface and textbase cues are still available and readers use them; after a delay, only situation-model cues survive, and those are the diagnostic ones.
2. **Generation is required; consumption is not enough.** Actively answering practice questions beat *reading* the questions and answers by **+0.264**, and reading questions and answers alone was not distinguishable from doing nothing (−0.095, *p* = .065). A learner who reads a rationale without first committing to an answer gains approximately nothing in calibration.
3. **Format mismatch actively harms.** Inconsistent test expectancy was **worse than no manipulation at all** (−0.117). Practising in a format that mis-signals the criterion task is not neutral.

Yang et al. also note Glenberg, Sanocki, Epstein and Morris (1987): monitoring accuracy was superior when a practice test was **identical** rather than dissimilar to the criterion test — the transfer-appropriate-monitoring result (**B**, single study).

#### 1.3 Working memory in integrating across a dense passage

Daneman and Merikle's meta-analysis (1996, *Memory & Cognition*; 77 studies, 6,179 participants) found reading-span and listening-span measures correlate **r = .41 with global comprehension** and **r = .52 with specific tests of integration**, versus **.28 and .40** for simple storage span. Their interpretation: working memory matters most for "the processes that integrate successively encountered ideas" (**A**). That is the CARS load profile — nine dense passages, integration across paragraphs, 90 minutes.

**But it is not a training target.** Melby-Lervåg, Redick and Hulme (2016; 87 publications, 145 experimental comparisons) found reliable near transfer to other working-memory tasks and **no convincing far transfer to reading comprehension** against treated controls; gains on working-memory measures were **not related** to far-transfer magnitude; and publication-bias analysis showed "no evidential value" from treated-control studies. Sala and Gobet's second-order meta-analysis (2019, *Collabra*; Model 3, k = 233) concluded that once placebo effects and publication bias are controlled, far-transfer effect size and true variance **equal zero** (**A**). Working-memory capacity is a real source of CARS difficulty and a dead end as an intervention.

The productive reading is that WM constrains *how much coherence-building a reader can afford*, which is why the goal manipulation in §1.6 interacts with WM capacity.

#### 1.4 Vocabulary — still limiting at this level?

**Probably not the binding constraint, but I cannot prove it, and this is one of my weaker sections.**

For: Rayner et al. (2016) are explicit that "language skill is at the heart of reading speed" and name **increased vocabulary** as one of only two routes (with practice) to reading faster without losing comprehension (**A** as a review conclusion).

Against, for this population: the quantitative literature on vocabulary as a comprehension bottleneck is **L2 lexical-coverage research**. Hu and Nation (2000) reported that learners needed to know 98–99% of a text's words for adequate unassisted comprehension. Schmitt, Jiang and Grabe (2011, N = 661) found **no threshold** — comprehension rose steadily with coverage, no dramatic inflection — and recommended 98% as a reasonable target for academic texts. Follow-up work suggests ~95% for minimal and ~98% for optimal comprehension (**A** within L2; **C** when transported to L1 college-educated readers).

L1 college-educated adults reading CARS passages are almost certainly above 98% coverage, so the coverage model predicts vocabulary is not lexically limiting. **I could not find a study measuring vocabulary as a limiting factor for L1 college-educated adults on admissions-test reading passages.** Treat "vocabulary is my problem" as unsupported-but-unfalsified rather than refuted.

One caveat that is not about word *knowledge*: CARS passages use familiar words in unfamiliar rhetorical positions ("qualifies," "concedes," "the author's stance"). Failing that is an argument-structure failure wearing a vocabulary costume.

#### 1.5 Situation-model construction: remembering propositions vs modelling what the author is doing

Kintsch's construction–integration model (1988, *Psych Review* 95(2):163–182) established the two-level architecture: propositions are constructed bottom-up without regard to discourse context, then integrated into a coherent structure by spreading activation. Zwaan and Radvansky (1998, *Psych Bulletin* 123(2):162–185) reviewed the evidence that comprehension produces **situation models** — integrated mental representations of the described state of affairs — and argued for treating their multidimensionality seriously. **Tier A as theory; both are frameworks, not effect sizes.**

The empirical payoff for CARS runs through metacomprehension. Yang et al.'s preferred explanation for why calibration is so poor is the **situation-model approach to metacomprehension**: readers base judgments on surface and textbase cues (fluency, familiarity, "I followed that"), which are *not diagnostic* of situation-model quality, and every intervention that forces situation-model access — delayed summarising, self-explaining, concept mapping — improves calibration, while every intervention that leaves surface cues available does not (**A** for the pattern; **B** for the causal interpretation).

This is the cleanest mechanistic statement of why CARS is hard for a strong reader: **the reader has a textbase good enough to feel comprehended and a situation model too thin to answer the question, and the felt sense of comprehension is generated by the wrong one of those two.**

#### 1.6 Standards of coherence — the mechanism most under-appreciated for CARS

Van den Broek's framework holds that readers implicitly set a **target level of comprehension** (standards of coherence), which then dictates which inferential activities they perform, which in turn determines the comprehension actually attained (Oudega & van den Broek, 2018, in *Handbook of Reading Research*-adjacent volume, doi:10.4324/9781315109503-5).

The empirical base is solid and directly on point:

- Think-aloud studies comparing study goals vs entertainment goals: a **study goal elicited more coherence-building processes** (connecting inferences, explanatory inferences, predictive inferences, rephrasing), while an **entertainment goal elicited text-external processes** (associative elaborations, evaluations, comments on the text) — Narvaez, van den Broek & Ruiz (1999); van den Broek, Lorch, Linderholm & Gustafson (2001) (**A** as a replicated pattern).
- **These effects were strongest for readers with large working-memory capacity** (Linderholm & van den Broek, 2002) — goal-setting is a lever that the strongest readers can actually pull (**B**).
- Eye-tracking (Yeari et al., 2015): first-pass reading was driven by structural centrality with **no effect of reading goal**; **second-pass reading (reinspection) was strongly affected by reading goal**. So the goal operates on *rereading*, not on initial uptake (**B**, single study).
- More demanding reading purposes produce a **more coherent representation and better memory** for the text (multiple studies, summarised in Bohn-Gettler et al., doi:10.1037/edu0000769) (**A**).

**And there is a study of exactly the CARS situation.** In a repeated-measures eye-tracking experiment with 60 undergraduates answering multiple-choice reading comprehension questions (doi:10.1080/10627197.2018.1513787), the same students were tested with no overarching reading goal and with a summarising goal. With **no goal**, they spent **much less time reading the passage before answering, more time re-reading while answering, and more time on parts of the text not needed for the questions**. The authors' conclusion: "providing examinees without an explicit goal for reading may inadvertently encourage a **'search for the answer' reading process, rather than building a coherent mental model** of text content" (**B** — 60 participants, single study, but the population and task are the right ones).

That is the CARS failure mode stated in the literature's own words. A test-taker who opens a passage with the goal "answer five questions" adopts a lower standard of coherence than the questions require, encodes accordingly, and then compensates by hunting — which costs the time they believe they lack.

### 2. What students misdiagnose

#### 2.1 Reading speed — the dominant misdiagnosis. **Folklore.**

Rayner, Schotter, Masson, Potter and Treiman (2016, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 17(1), doi:10.1177/1529100615623267) is the definitive review, and its conclusions are unambiguous (**A**):

- Skilled college-educated adults read at about **200–400 wpm**. In Rayner's classic eye-movement table, ten skilled readers averaged **216 ms fixations, 8.1-character saccades, 11% regressions, 308 wpm**.
- There is a **trade-off between speed and accuracy**. "It is unlikely that readers will be able to double or triple their reading speeds (e.g., from around 250 to 500–750 wpm) while still being able to understand the text as well as if they read at normal speed."
- **Skimming** runs two to four times faster with **lower comprehension** — it is a different task, not a faster version of the same one.
- There is "**little evidence for a unique behavior, such as speed reading, in which speed and comprehension are both high**."
- The only route to more text per unit time at high comprehension is **practice and greater language skill (e.g. vocabulary)** — not eye-movement training, not RSVP, not peripheral-vision drills.
- Crucially for CARS: **readers repair comprehension failures by rereading**, demonstrated with a trailing-mask manipulation that made rereading impossible (Schotter, Tran & Rayner, 2014). Speed-reading training discourages rereading; RSVP devices prevent it. The repair mechanism is the thing being removed.

**Is time pressure or comprehension failure the binding constraint on CARS?** The honest answer is **comprehension failure is primary, with time pressure as a downstream symptom** (**B**, because I have no CARS-specific speededness analysis). The argument:

- The eye-tracking result above shows that a low standard of coherence *causes* more re-reading and more time on irrelevant text. Slowness is generated by shallow initial encoding, not the reverse.
- The child eye-tracking data point the same way: **correct** responders were better at recognising when rereading was needed and **more precise at locating** the relevant region; incorrect responders re-read less and searched imprecisely. Higher-achieving readers needed **less** time for both passages and questions and were **less** likely to re-read — but when they did re-read, they were more likely to be right (doi:10.1111/1467-9817.12369; doi:10.1111/1467-9817.70018). Speed is an *output* of comprehension quality (**B**, third/fifth/eighth graders — do not over-extend to adults).
- Counterweight I must state: **most timed tests are unintentionally speeded** (Sireci et al., 2005, as cited in Lovett's 2010 review, doi:10.3102/0034654310364063), and extended time raises scores for examinees **without** disabilities too. Huesman and Frisbie (2000) found non-LD sixth-graders **did** gain significantly under extended time *when told to work slowly and carefully*, but **not** when told to work at a normal rate. A reanalysis of 11 comparative experiments found the proportion of non-disabled examinees who benefit from extra time roughly **equals** the proportion among LD students (doi:10.1177/0734282915616537). So time limits do suppress some real comprehension capability. **What this does not license** is the inference that the fix is reading faster; the fix in those studies was reading *more carefully with more time*, i.e. raising the standard of coherence.

**Verdict.** "I am too slow" is the most common self-diagnosis and among the least actionable. Speed-reading training is refuted (**A**). Pacing *strategy* — when to abandon a question, how to distribute 90 minutes over nine passages — is a legitimate separate skill, but it is a scheduling skill, not a reading-rate skill.

#### 2.2 Vocabulary — **unsupported at this level, not refuted.** See §1.4. Commonly blamed; no evidence base for L1 college-educated adults on this kind of passage; the L2 coverage model predicts it should not bind. Tier **C** either way. When a student says "I didn't know that word," check whether the missed word was a *stance* word.

#### 2.3 Topic interest and boredom — **partially supported, but weaker than students think, and mostly not the humanities/science story they tell.**

Schiefele's programme found substantial interest–text-learning relations that are **stronger for deep-level than surface-level learning** and **largely independent of prior knowledge and cognitive ability** (doi:10.1207/s1532799xssr0303_4) (**B**). But the magnitudes are modest. In the K–12 motivation meta-analysis (185 samples, 1,154 effect sizes; doi:10.3102/0034654320919352), overall motivation–reading **r = .22**, and the **interest** subconstruct specifically was **r = .13, 95% CI [.08, .18]** — the *smallest* of the six subconstructs, well below beliefs about self (r = .28). Longitudinal analysis found **earlier reading predicted later motivation more strongly than motivation predicted later reading** (**A** for the meta-analytic figures; **B** for extension to adults, since the sample is K–12).

Reading-motivation interventions do move comprehension a little (d = 0.27) and the effect was **larger for typical readers than for struggling readers** (doi via VU Amsterdam record) (**B**).

**Verdict.** Interest is real, small (r ≈ .13), and partly an *effect* of reading skill rather than a cause. "I bomb philosophy passages because they bore me" is more likely a standards-of-coherence report than a motivation report: unfamiliar rhetorical structure lowers the standard the reader sets. Building an app around topic-interest matching would be optimising a r ≈ .13 lever while ignoring a calibration lever worth Δ ≈ 0.2.

#### 2.4 Overconfidence and premature termination of study — **evidence-supported, and invisible to the student by construction.**

Dunlosky and Rawson (2012, *Learning and Instruction* 22(4):271–280) ran two studies — one manipulating judgment accuracy experimentally, one exploiting individual differences — and found in both that **greater absolute judgment accuracy was associated with higher long-term retention**, a link not explained by differential feedback, effort during study, or trials to criterion. The mechanism they demonstrated: **overconfidence → premature termination of study → less learning during practice → lower retention two days later** (**A**).

Their accuracy manipulation is directly transplantable: participants who scored their own recall attempts **against explicit idea-unit standards** (shown the idea units contained in the correct answer) judged more accurately and retained more than participants who saw only their own responses. That is externally-supplied scoring criteria improving self-assessment — the concrete form of §4(c).

Note the compounding with §1.2: metacomprehension *relative* accuracy is ≈ .18, so a student's ranking of which passages they understood is near-random; and *absolute* accuracy is biased optimistic, so they stop early. The student experiences neither of these as an error. They experience "I get it" and "I'm done." **This is the single strongest candidate for "cause students cannot self-diagnose."**

#### 2.5 Answer-choice evaluation as a distinct skill — **supported, and contested, and the most interesting result in this review.**

There is a 30-year line of work showing that examinees answer reading-comprehension multiple-choice items **well above chance with the passages deleted**:

- Katz, Lautenschlager, Blackburn and Harris (1990, *Psychological Science* 1(2):122–127): college undergraduates performed well above chance on SAT Reading Comprehension **without the passages**; passage-less performance **correlated with their verbal SAT scores**; and item difficulty without passages correlated with normative item difficulty. Their conclusion: the task "**substantially measures factors unrelated to reading comprehension**" (**B** — small, contested).
- Katz and Lautenschlager (1999, *Perceptual and Motor Skills* 85:1157) repeated this on the post-1994 SAT-I with two parallel forms and reported that **more than 60% of all items were flawed** by their passage-dependence criterion, with passage-less scores again strongly correlated with SAT-V.
- Katz and Lautenschlager (1994, *Educational Assessment* 2(4):295–308) extended the finding to the **SAT-I, ACT, and GRE**.
- **Powers and Leung (1995, *Journal of Educational Measurement* 32(2):105–129)** is the process study and the most useful one here. They administered question sets without passages to verbally able students in eight secondary schools and surveyed strategies. The two most-cited strategies were **choosing answers on the basis of consistency with other questions in the set**, and **reconstructing the missing passage's main theme from all the questions and answers in the set**. These strategies were **more likely to yield correct answers than any of the many other, less construct-relevant strategies** available.

**Contested:** Freedle and Kostin disputed Katz et al., and Katz and Lautenschlager replied (1995, *Psych Science* 6(2):126–127). I did not read the exchange in full. Rupp, Ferne and Choi (2006, *Language Testing* 23(4):441–474) argue more broadly that the multiple-choice format itself reshapes the construct being measured. Call the whole line **B**, not A.

**What it means for CARS, carefully.** The correct inference is *not* "the passage doesn't matter." It is that a **substantial and separable portion of item performance comes from reasoning over the option set and over the set's internal consistency** — a skill that is (a) genuinely part of what strong test-takers do, (b) largely absent from any practice format that does not present a full question set, and (c) partly construct-relevant, since reconstructing a theme from questions is itself argument reconstruction. Powers and Leung's finding that the *successful* strategies were the more construct-relevant ones is the important nuance: option evaluation done well is argument evaluation, not gaming.

So the answer to "is the failure in understanding the passage or in evaluating the options?" is **both, and they are separable, and the option-evaluation component is trainable in a way the comprehension component may not be**. I could not find think-aloud or eye-tracking data on *adult* option evaluation in an admissions-test context; the eye-tracking work I found on question-and-option reading is with children (§2.1). **Gap.**

#### 2.6 Summary: which misdiagnoses are folklore, in both directions

| Student belief | Verdict | Tier |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "I'm too slow; I need to read faster" | **Folklore.** Speed-reading refuted; slowness is largely an output of shallow encoding | **A** |
| "I don't know enough words" | **Unsupported at this level** (not refuted; no L1-adult study exists) | **C** |
| "Boring/unfamiliar topics kill me" | **Weakly supported** (interest r ≈ .13, partly an effect of skill, not a cause) | **B** |
| "I understood that passage" | **Systematically unreliable** — relative accuracy ≈ .18, ≈54.5% discrimination | **A** |
| "I've reviewed enough; I've got this" | **Systematically optimistic** — overconfidence → premature termination → lower retention | **A** |
| "My problem is the passage, not the answers" | **Half wrong.** Option evaluation is separable and substantial | **B** |
| "Time pressure is the binding constraint" | **Mostly folklore as stated** — but tests *are* often unintentionally speeded, and extra time helps everyone who then reads carefully | **B** |
| "More practice passages will fix it" | **Folklore without calibration feedback** — reading Q&A without generating was indistinguishable from nothing (Δ = −0.095, ns) | **A** |

**Folklore in the other direction** (things students dismiss that matter): delaying summarisation rather than doing it immediately; committing to an answer *before* reading rationales; practising in the exact criterion format (mismatch is actively harmful, Δ = −0.117); and treating "I feel like I understood it" as evidence of nothing.

Caveats on my arithmetic: I did not verify the SAT or GRE section SDs from primary sources — 110 and 100–110 are conventional values I am assuming, and the conversion also assumes comparable score-scale properties across tests, which is a stretch. Treat the CARS column as an order-of-magnitude translation, not an estimate. **I could not find the AAMC raw-to-scaled conversion table for a 53-question CARS section**, so I cannot express these as "questions correct." The one AAMC-published illustration (a section where raw 35–37 → 123 and raw 46–48 → 128) implies roughly **2–3 raw items per scaled point** near the middle of the scale *[own calculation, and that example may not be CARS]*.

#### 3.3 Is verbal genuinely more prep-resistant than content sections?

**Partly — but the honest answer is that the received wisdom is stronger than the evidence, and the real moderator is item type, not "verbal vs content."**

Evidence *for* verbal being more resistant (**B**):
- Powers and Rock's verbal effect was consistently about **half** the math effect, across five different estimators.
- Briggs replicated the same ratio and found the ACT **Reading** coaching effect was **negative**.
- GRE verbal showed **no dose-response** relationship with coaching hours, while GRE analytical showed a strong one.

Evidence *against*, which must be stated (**B**):
- Montgomery and Lilly's experimental synthesis found SAT verbal **+23.5** vs math **+32.7** — a ratio of 0.72, not 0.44 — and characterised the combined effect as "nearly triple that which was previously assumed."
- Their studies are from **1970–2000**, i.e. mostly the pre-1994 SAT-V, which contained analogies and antonyms — discrete vocabulary items far more coachable than passage-based reading. The comparison is therefore not clean, and if anything the discrepancy *supports* the item-type story: verbal sections were coachable **when they contained learnable discrete item types**.
- Powers and Rock's design does not meet WWC group design standards, because baseline equivalence of the analytic groups was not demonstrated (IES/WWC intervention report on ACT/SAT test preparation). The most-cited "verbal is barely coachable" figures come from a quasi-experimental survey design.

**The GRE result is the one to build on.** Powers's design used the analytical section as a *baseline of known coachability* against which to judge the others. Analytical items — analysis of explanations, logical diagrams — moved **+66 points** and showed strong dose-response, and were deleted from the test for precisely that reason. Those are formal-reasoning items with **explicit, teachable structure**. Verbal and quantitative, without such structure, did not respond to duration.

The inference for CARS (**B**, and this is my own synthesis rather than a published claim): coachability tracks whether the item type has **an enumerable, formally describable solution structure**, not whether the section is "verbal" or "content." CARS items *do* have such structure — the AAMC's own three skills (Foundations of Comprehension, Reasoning Within the Text, Reasoning Beyond the Text) and the recurring operations under them (identify thesis, identify author stance, identify paragraph function, strengthen/weaken, apply a principle to a new case). To the extent CARS items are typed, they are more like GRE analytical than like GRE verbal. To the extent an intervention teaches "read more closely," they are more like GRE verbal.

**Say plainly:** the evidence that CARS specifically is prep-resistant is **thin to nonexistent**. What exists is (a) small coaching effects on other tests' reading sections, from mostly quasi-experimental designs, one of them negative; and (b) no CARS study at all. "CARS is barely trainable" and "CARS is highly trainable with the right method" are both currently under-determined by evidence. That is an uncomfortable answer but it is the correct one.

#### 3.4 Does reading-strategy instruction help already-proficient adults?

This is the sharpest limiting result in the review, and it cuts against most prep-industry practice.

Self-Explanation Reading Training (SERT) and its automated form iSTART are the best-studied reading-strategy interventions for the college-age range (McNamara, 2004, *Discourse Processes* 38(1), doi:10.1207/s15326950dp3801_1):

- In the original SERT experiment (n = 42 trained vs 42 control, four science texts), SERT improved comprehension and self-explanation quality **only for participants with low domain knowledge** — and for those participants, the gain appeared on **text-based questions and not on bridging-inference questions**. Protocol analysis showed SERT helped them substitute domain-general logic for absent domain knowledge (**B**).
- In a classroom generalisation study (265 introductory biology students, 59 trained; ERIC ED577143), SERT **benefited students who began with less science knowledge and did not benefit students with greater prior knowledge**. Low-knowledge trained students performed **as well as** high-knowledge students across three exams (**B**).
- A partial reversal worth noting: in one middle-school study and one 44-student college study, iSTART improved **text-based** performance for students with *less* prior strategy knowledge but improved **bridging-inference** performance for students with *greater* prior strategy knowledge (O'Reilly et al., 2004; Magliano et al., 2005, as reported in McNamara's review) (**C** — single studies, small, reported secondhand).

**Verdict.** Reading-strategy instruction is well-evidenced for readers with a **deficit** — low domain knowledge, low strategy knowledge, or poor comprehension. Its benefit for already-proficient adults is **not established**, and in the two largest samples it was **zero** for high-knowledge students. A CARS population of college-educated pre-meds sits mostly on the wrong side of that moderator. The one signal that a proficient reader might gain on **bridging-inference** measures is exactly the CARS-relevant one, but rests on two small studies I read only secondhand.

This is a strong argument for targeting **calibration** (where even skilled readers are demonstrably near-random, §1.2) rather than **strategy** (where skilled readers are already near their own ceiling).

### 4. Design implications

**Priority order.** Ranked by (evidence quality × expected effect × how badly the failure is self-invisible):

1. **Force a generated commitment about the author's argument before answers are visible, then confront it with the author's actual reasoning.** Best-evidenced single move. Combines the two largest reliable calibration effects (delayed summary Δ +0.210; self-explaining Δ +0.179) with the generation requirement (active answering beat reading Q&A by Δ +0.264, while reading Q&A alone was indistinguishable from nothing). **A.**
2. **Make the predicted-versus-actual gap externally visible and per-passage.** Attacks the failure students cannot see (relative accuracy ≈ .18; overconfidence → premature termination). Combining interventions was better than any single one (Δ +0.299 to +0.316 vs +0.101). **A for the components; B for this specific packaging.**
3. **Practise on full multi-question passage sets in the criterion format.** Format mismatch is not neutral but actively harmful (inconsistent test expectancy Δ **−0.117**); set-level reasoning is a real, separable, partly construct-relevant skill (Powers & Leung). **B.**
4. **Type the items and teach the types.** The GRE analytical precedent is the only large coaching effect in this whole literature (+66 pts, d ≈ 0.6), and it came from items with explicit formal structure. **B, inferential.**
5. **Introduce timing late, as pacing calibration.** **B.**
6. **Do not build:** working-memory training (far transfer = 0, **A**); speed-reading or eye-movement training (**A**, refuted); topic-interest matching as a primary mechanism (r ≈ .13, **B**); generic "read dense material daily" without a generated product and a comparison (no calibration benefit is expected from consumption alone, **A** by extension from the reading-Q&A null).

#### (a) Must practice use full multi-question passage sets to be transfer-appropriate?

**Yes — but for calibration and set-strategy reasons, not because comprehension training requires it.**

For: transfer-appropriate monitoring is one of the three mechanisms Yang et al.'s meta-analysis supports. Consistent test expectancy improved calibration (Δ +0.090 vs no manipulation; Δ +0.207 vs inconsistent), and **inconsistent expectancy was worse than doing nothing (Δ −0.117)**. Glenberg et al. (1987) found monitoring accuracy superior when the practice test was *identical* to the criterion test. Separately, Powers and Leung showed that **cross-question consistency and set-level theme reconstruction are the strategies that actually work** on real reading items — and these do not exist in a single-item or no-item format. Nine passages in 90 minutes also makes stamina and inter-passage pacing part of the criterion task.

Against, honestly: Kang, McDermott and Roediger (2007) showed format matching is **not** required for learning benefits — short-answer practice with feedback beat multiple-choice practice **even on a multiple-choice final test**. So "transfer-appropriate processing" as a strict format-matching doctrine is wrong.

**Reconciliation:** format matching is required for **calibration** (knowing how well you understood, which is criterion-format-sensitive) and not required for **learning** (which benefits from harder generative practice). An intervention should therefore do both: generative production for learning, full sets in criterion format for calibration. Do not choose one.

#### (b) Must feedback explain the author's reasoning rather than mark answers?

**Yes, and the evidence is unusually direct.**

- Marking answers is the null condition. **Reading questions and answers without generating was not distinguishable from no intervention at all** (Δ = −0.095, *p* = .065), while actively answering beat reading Q&A by **Δ +0.264**. A right/wrong mark plus a rationale the learner reads passively is, on this evidence, close to worthless for calibration (**A**).
- **Feedback is what flips the format effect.** In Kang et al. (2007) *without* feedback, multiple-choice practice beat short-answer practice; *with* feedback, short-answer practice produced the best final performance, and the feedback benefit for the short-answer condition was **d = 0.40** (*t*(190) = 2.75) while feedback made **no difference** to the multiple-choice condition (*p* = .29). Generative practice is only superior *when the generation is corrected* (**A**).
- **Explicit standards improve self-assessment.** Dunlosky and Rawson's accuracy manipulation gave learners the **idea units contained in the correct answer** to score themselves against; this improved judgment accuracy and downstream retention (**A**).
- **Self-explanation of causal/logical relations is a validated calibration lever** (Δ +0.179), and its mechanism is forcing situation-model access rather than surface-cue access (**A** for the effect, **B** for the mechanism).

**Design translation.** Feedback should state the author's argumentative move, why the keyed option matches it, and — this is the part usually skipped — **why each distractor is a specific kind of misreading** (too strong, too narrow, true-but-not-the-author's-claim, right relation with wrong direction). That last piece is what converts a rationale into an idea-unit standard the learner can score their own reasoning against, and it is what makes the option-evaluation skill (§2.5) explicitly trainable.

#### (c) Would externally-measured calibration feedback address the monitoring failure?

**It is the best-motivated intervention in this review, and the direct trial does not exist.**

The case is strong and indirect:
- The failure is real, large, and structural: relative accuracy ≈ .178, i.e. **54.5% discrimination against a 50% baseline** (**A**).
- It has demonstrated downstream cost: overconfidence causes premature termination of study, which causes lower retention (**A**).
- It is responsive to intervention, with the largest gains from **combinations** (Δ +0.299 to +0.316) (**A**).
- The mechanism by which practice tests help includes exactly this: Yang et al. state that practice test performance "provides informative feedback and induces awareness of the gap between perceived and actual learning, which then calibrate subsequent comprehension judgments" (**B** — their interpretation).
- Dunlosky and Rawson's idea-unit standards are external scoring criteria improving self-assessment (**A**).

**What I could not find:** any study that gives learners **explicit numeric calibration feedback** (your predicted score vs your actual score, tracked over sessions) on **reading comprehension** and measures downstream comprehension gains. The metacomprehension literature manipulates *when and how judgments are made* (delay, summarising, self-explaining), not *whether learners are shown their calibration history*. So the specific product feature is **B at best, extrapolated**.

**Practical consequence.** Prioritise the **structural** version, which is directly evidenced: predict per passage → answer the full set → see predicted vs actual immediately, with the delay placed between reading and judging (the delay is the active ingredient, §1.2). Treat a longitudinal calibration dashboard as a plausible add-on, not as the evidenced mechanism. And note the trap: a dashboard is *consumed*, and consumption without generation is the condition that produced Δ = −0.095.

#### (d) Does timed practice matter early, or is it counterproductive?

**Counterproductive early; necessary late. B — this is mechanism-based inference, not a direct trial.**

Against early timing:
- Time pressure lowers the standard of coherence a reader adopts. The undergraduate eye-tracking study showed that **without an overarching comprehension goal**, readers spent less time on the passage, more time re-reading during questions, and more time on irrelevant text — the "search for the answer" mode. A clock is the strongest possible push toward that mode (**B**).
- **Rereading is the repair mechanism** for comprehension failure (Rayner et al.; Schotter et al., 2014). Timing suppresses it. Early practice should be building the repair habit, not suppressing it (**A** for the mechanism).
- The extended-time literature shows non-disabled examinees gain from extra time **when instructed to work slowly and carefully**, but **not** when told to work at a normal rate (Huesman & Frisbie, 2000). Extra time only helps if the reader raises their standard to use it. Early untimed practice without an explicit comprehension goal wastes the time (**B**).
- Delay-based calibration interventions require a gap between reading and judging. A timer compresses that gap to zero, converting delayed summarising (Δ +0.210) into immediate summarising (Δ +0.030, ns) (**A** for the effect sizes; **B** for the inference).

For late timing:
- Nine passages in 90 minutes makes pacing and stamina genuinely part of the criterion task, and unintentional speededness is common in timed tests (Sireci et al., 2005). A learner who has never practised the pacing decision will make it badly.
- Transfer-appropriate monitoring implies the final calibration rehearsals should match the criterion conditions, including timing.

**Design translation.** Untimed passages with an explicit comprehension goal and a delayed generated summary first; then timed *sets* for pacing calibration; then full-section simulations. The variable to instrument early is **accuracy at a chosen standard of coherence**, not seconds per passage. Showing a learner a stopwatch in week one teaches them that CARS is a speed problem, which §2.1 says it is not.

**Gap:** I found no study comparing timed-early vs timed-late schedules for reading-test preparation. This recommendation is a mechanism argument.

#### (e) Is there value in *producing* arguments rather than selecting among options?

This is the question that matters most to this team, so here is the sharpest answer the evidence supports, including the part that cuts against production.

**The most directly relevant evidence says production practice did *not* measurably improve evaluation.**

Kuhn (2009, *Informal Logic* 29(4), doi:10.22329/il.v29i4.2905) tested precisely this. Students in a twice-weekly, multi-year dialogic argumentation curriculum — dense, authentic practice at *producing* arguments and counterarguments against opposing-side peers — were assessed on a forced-choice instrument measuring their ability to *evaluate* argumentative moves (distinguishing a Counter-Critique, which weakens the opponent's actual argument, from a Counter-Alternative, which changes the subject).

| Group | Mean (of 9) |
| --- | --- |
| 6th graders, before any of the curriculum | **5.66** |
| 7th graders, after **one full year** of the curriculum | **6.17** |
| Expert comparison (37 doctoral students in developmental/educational psychology) | **7.46** |

The 6th–7th grade difference was **not statistically significant**: *t*(150.7) = −1.731, *p* = .10. Kuhn's own summary: young adolescents "lack proficiency in this area, with only at best modest gains and much room for improvement after a full year of dense practice in argumentation." Meanwhile the **production** gains from the same intervention were large and significant: at posttest, **35% of intervention students used Counter-Critiques more than a third of the time versus 13% of controls**. Her proposed explanation: "**Meta-level demands involved in evaluation but not present in production may make evaluation more difficult**."

Note also that chance on this 2-option, 9-item instrument is 4–5, so the 6th-grade baseline of 5.66 was barely above chance and the trained 6.17 was still low — and **even the experts scored 7.46/9, not at ceiling**. Evaluation is hard for everyone.

**Limits I must state.** Single study; middle-school students, not adults; small N per cell; a 9-item 2-option instrument that Kuhn herself notes was later expanded to avoid ceiling effects; the difference was in the *right direction* and *p* = .10, so this is "not detected," not "shown absent." **Tier B, and B partly because it is the only study I found that asks the question at all.** Kuhn and Crowell (2011, *Psychological Science* 22(4):545–552) separately showed that dialogic argumentation **beat extensive essay-writing practice** on individual essay quality and produced "greater awareness of the relevance of evidence to argument" — so dialogue is an excellent way to build production, and shows *some* meta-level movement. But the criterion in that study was production too.

**Now the evidence that production *does* transfer — and the condition that separates the two cases.**

- **Graham and Hebert (2011, *Harvard Educational Review* 81(4):710–744; also the Carnegie "Writing to Read" report).** Writing **about text that was read** improved reading comprehension with a weighted **ES = 0.40** (0.35 on published standardized norm-referenced tests across 9 studies; 0.49 on researcher-designed measures across 44 studies). **57 of 61 outcomes (93%) were positive.** Writing about text beat reading it, rereading it, studying it, *and discussing it*. For lower-achieving students explicitly taught how to use the writing activities, **ES = 0.63**. Grades 2–12, majority middle/high school (**A** within that range; **B** extrapolated to adults).
- **Kang, McDermott and Roediger (2007).** With corrective feedback, **short-answer (generative) practice produced better final performance than multiple-choice practice, on both short-answer and multiple-choice final tests** (feedback benefit for the SA condition **d = 0.40**). This directly contradicts a strict format-matching account: generating beat selecting *on a selection criterion*. Caveat: the criterion was **retention of facts**, not reasoning or comprehension (**A** for the result, **B** for relevance here).
- **Self-explaining** improves metacomprehension accuracy (Δ **+0.179**) and **delayed summary writing** is the largest single calibration intervention (Δ **+0.210**) — both are production, and both are production *about a text* (**A**).
- Counter-data within the same family: **question generation** did *not* improve calibration (Δ +0.042, *p* = .406). Not all production works (**A**).

**The discriminating variable is the object of production, not the act of production.**

| Production activity | Object | Transfers to CARS-like criterion? | Best evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Summarise a passage after a delay | **the passage** | Yes, calibration Δ +0.210 | **A** |
| Self-explain the author's causal/logical structure | **the passage** | Yes, calibration Δ +0.179; comprehension via SERT for lower-knowledge readers | **A / B** |
| Write an analysis/interpretation/summary of a text read | **the passage** | Yes, comprehension ES ≈ 0.40 | **A** (K-12) |
| Recall/short-answer with corrective feedback | the studied material | Yes, beats MC practice even on MC tests, d = 0.40 | **A** (facts) |
| Generate questions about a text | the passage | **No detectable calibration benefit** (+0.042, ns) | **A** |
| Debate a topic from one's own opinion, no passage | **the learner's own position** | **Not shown.** Production gains were large; evaluation gains were not detected (*p* = .10) | **B** |

**Verdict on (e).** Production has real value for a CARS intervention, but only when what is produced is **a reconstruction of the source text's argument, immediately comparable against a standard**. Two things must be true simultaneously: the learner generates (because consumption is worth ≈ 0, Δ −0.095) and the generation is corrected against the author's actual reasoning (because without feedback, generative practice loses to selection). That is a strong recommendation for "read the passage, then write in one sentence what the author is *doing* and what would weaken it, then see the author's actual move" — a production activity whose object is the passage.

It is a **weak** recommendation for free-form debate on a topic with no passage. Such an activity trains the skill Kuhn showed *does* improve with practice (argument production) and not the skill CARS scores (argument evaluation on someone else's text under time pressure). It also has no passage, so it cannot be scored against a text-derived standard, which removes the feedback ingredient that Kang et al. showed is what makes generative practice superior. And Graham and Hebert's result was specifically that writing about text beat **discussing** the text.

**What would change my mind:** a study showing that adults who practise producing rebuttals to *presented arguments in prose* improve on multiple-choice argument-evaluation items. Kuhn's is the closest attempt and it did not detect the effect. If the team wants to keep a debate mechanic, the highest-value modification is to **give it a passage** — have the learner rebut the author of a real CARS-style passage rather than a topic string — which converts the object of production from the learner's opinion to the source text and makes the reflection scoreable against the text.

## DOK 1 — established facts

1. Readers' relative metacomprehension accuracy is **MC = .178, 95% CI [.155, .200]** under non-intervention conditions (Yang et al., 2022; 502 effects, 15,889 participants, 115 studies). Earlier canonical estimates: **G = .27** (Maki, 1998c, 25 studies), **G = .27** (Dunlosky & Lipko, 2007, 36 experiments), **≈ .2** (Lin & Zabrucky, 1998), **r = .24** (Prinz et al., 2020a). Yang et al. argue the higher figures overestimate normal accuracy because they pooled intervention conditions. **A**
2. In judgment-accuracy terms this equals **54.5%** correct discrimination at MC = .178 and **56.9%** at G = .27, against a 50% chance baseline (Yang et al. simulation). **A**
3. Metacomprehension accuracy is not rescued by criterion format: correlations "no greater than +.32" across fill-in-the-blank, free recall, and four-alternative multiple choice (Maki and colleagues). **A**
4. Skilled college-educated adults read at about **200–400 wpm** (mean 308 wpm in Rayner's eye-movement table; 216 ms fixations, 8.1-character saccades, 11% regressions). **A**
5. There is a speed–accuracy trade-off in reading; doubling or tripling rate while preserving comprehension is not achievable; skimming is 2–4× faster with lower comprehension; there is "little evidence" for a distinct high-speed high-comprehension mode (Rayner et al., 2016). **A**
6. Readers repair comprehension failures by **rereading**; preventing rereading (trailing-mask, RSVP) degrades comprehension (Schotter, Tran & Rayner, 2014). **A**
7. SAT verbal coaching effects are **+6 to +8 scaled points**, roughly half the math effect (**+13 to +18**), across two independent studies with different designs (Powers & Rock, 1999; Briggs, 2001). Both authors describe the effects as small. **B** (quasi-experimental; Powers & Rock does not meet WWC group design standards)
8. Briggs found the **ACT Reading** coaching effect **negative** (−0.6 to −0.7 points) and no significant coaching effect on any section for students without prior PSAT exposure. **B**
9. Montgomery and Lilly's synthesis of 10 experimental studies (1970–2000) reported much larger effects: SAT verbal **+23.5**, math **+32.7**. Those studies predate the 1994 SAT-V revision. **B**
10. GRE **verbal** score effects were not significantly related to amount of coaching, while GRE **analytical** effects were (dose-response *r* = .45 and .35 for two item types; **+66 points** from a targeted program). Those two item types were subsequently **removed from the test** for being too coachable (Powers, 1985; Swinton & Powers, 1982). **B**
11. Inference-making, comprehension monitoring, and story-structure knowledge each predict **unique variance in reading comprehension after controlling word reading, vocabulary, and verbal ability**, and are not wholly mediated by working memory (Cain, Oakhill & Bryant, 2004). **A within child samples**
12. Knowledge deficits are **not** the primary source of poor comprehenders' inference failures when knowledge availability is controlled (Cain, Oakhill, Barnes & Bryant, 2001). **B**
13. Working-memory span correlates **r = .41** with global comprehension and **r = .52** with tests of integration; simple storage span correlates **.28 / .40** (Daneman & Merikle, 1996; 77 studies, 6,179 participants). **A**
14. Working-memory training produces **no reliable far transfer to reading comprehension** against treated controls, with no evidential value after publication-bias correction; second-order meta-analysis puts far-transfer effect size and true variance at **zero** (Melby-Lervåg, Redick & Hulme, 2016; Sala & Gobet, 2019). **A**
15. Reading goals systematically change processing: study goals elicit more coherence-building inferences, entertainment goals more text-external elaboration; effects are strongest for high-working-memory readers; reading goal affects **second-pass** but not first-pass reading (van den Broek and colleagues; Linderholm & van den Broek, 2002; Yeari et al., 2015). **A / B**
16. Undergraduates given **no overarching reading goal** on a multiple-choice reading test spent less time reading the passage before answering, more time re-reading while answering, and more time on text irrelevant to the questions — a "search for the answer" rather than mental-model-building process (doi:10.1080/10627197.2018.1513787, N = 60). **B**
17. Examinees answer SAT/ACT/GRE reading-comprehension items **well above chance with the passages deleted**; the successful strategies are cross-question consistency and reconstructing the missing passage's theme from the question set; passage-less performance correlates with verbal test scores (Katz et al., 1990; Katz & Lautenschlager, 1994, 1999; Powers & Leung, 1995). Contested by Freedle and Kostin. **B**
18. Overconfidence causes **premature termination of study**, which lowers learning and two-day retention; supplying **explicit idea-unit standards** for self-scoring improves both judgment accuracy and retention (Dunlosky & Rawson, 2012). **A**
19. Writing **about text read** improves reading comprehension, weighted **ES = 0.40** (0.35 standardized / 0.49 researcher-designed; 57/61 outcomes positive; 0.63 for lower-achieving students explicitly taught), and beats reading, rereading, studying, and **discussing** the text (Graham & Hebert, 2011). **A** (grades 2–12)
20. With corrective feedback, short-answer practice beats multiple-choice practice on a later test **regardless of the final test's format** (feedback benefit for short-answer, **d = 0.40**); without feedback the ordering reverses (Kang, McDermott & Roediger, 2007). **A** (fact retention)
21. Reading-strategy instruction (SERT/iSTART) benefits **low-knowledge and low-strategy readers** and produced **no benefit for high-prior-knowledge students** in a 265-student classroom study; in the original lab study the low-knowledge gain appeared on text-based and not bridging-inference questions (McNamara, 2004; ERIC ED577143). **B**
22. Topic interest relates to reading achievement at **r = .13, 95% CI [.08, .18]** — the smallest of six motivation subconstructs; overall motivation–reading **r = .22**; earlier reading predicts later motivation more strongly than the reverse (185 samples, 1,154 effect sizes). **A** (K-12)
23. MCAT CARS is scored 118–132 with mean **≈ 125** and SD **≈ 3.0–3.2** (AAMC percentile tables). **A**
24. **No peer-reviewed study estimating a coaching, training, or intervention effect on MCAT CARS was found.** **A** (as a negative finding about the literature)

## DOK 2 — insights and implications

1. **The binding constraint on CARS is the standard of coherence the reader adopts, and it is set before the first question is read.** Fact 16 is the linchpin: students without a comprehension goal read the passage less, re-read more during questions, and dwell on irrelevant text. Fact 15 says a study goal produces more connecting and explanatory inferences. Fact 5 says the compensating strategy — going faster — is unavailable. The intervention lever is therefore the **goal the learner holds while reading**, which is set by what the app asks them to produce afterward. This is why an app's *task design* is more consequential than its content volume.

2. **Perceived slowness is a downstream symptom of shallow encoding, and treating it as the cause makes the problem worse.** Search-mode reading generates the re-reading and irrelevant dwelling that consume the clock (fact 16); the fix is a higher standard of coherence, and the extended-time data confirm extra time only helps readers who then read carefully rather than at "normal" pace (§2.1). A product that leads with timers, pacing drills, or wpm metrics is training the reader into the mode that causes the problem. **This is the highest-leverage misdiagnosis to correct and the most commercially tempting to indulge.**

3. **The failure students cannot self-diagnose is calibration, and it has a number: they discriminate their own well-understood from poorly-understood passages at ~54.5% versus 50% chance.** Facts 1–3 mean self-selected review is close to random, and fact 18 means the resulting overconfidence causes them to stop early. **Every student-directed feature that depends on the student knowing what they got wrong and why is built on a coin flip.** Externalising that judgment is not a nice-to-have; it is the only way the learner's own control decisions become better than chance.

4. **"More passages" is not merely inefficient, it is the specific null condition in the literature.** Reading questions and answers without generating produced Δ = −0.095 (ns) — indistinguishable from doing nothing — while active answering beat it by Δ +0.264 (fact 1 table). A volume-based practice product that shows rationales after each item is, on this evidence, delivering close to zero calibration benefit no matter how many passages it serves. Volume only converts to learning through generation plus correction.

5. **Delay is a free, load-bearing, and almost universally ignored design parameter.** Immediate summarising Δ +0.030 (ns) versus delayed Δ +0.210; immediate keyword generation Δ +0.009 (ns) versus delayed Δ +0.184. The *same activity* is worthless or best-in-class depending on whether it happens before or after the surface trace has decayed. Placing the reflection prompt after the full question set (or after several passages) rather than immediately after reading is a scheduling change with a larger evidenced effect than most content investments.

6. **Format mismatch is not neutral — it is negative.** Inconsistent test expectancy scored Δ **−0.117** relative to no manipulation (fact 1 table). A practice activity that signals the wrong criterion task actively degrades calibration. This upgrades "our practice format doesn't quite match the real thing" from a cosmetic complaint to a mechanism for harm, and it is the strongest argument for full nine-passage-style multi-question sets.

7. **Coachability tracks item-type formalisability, not "verbal versus content."** The single largest coaching effect in this entire literature is GRE **analytical** (+66 points, d ≈ 0.6, strong dose-response) — a reasoning section, not a content section — and it was coachable because analysis-of-explanations and logical-diagram items have enumerable solution structures (fact 10). GRE verbal, with no such structure taught, showed no dose-response. CARS items *do* have enumerable operations. **The plausible path to a CARS effect is typing the reasoning operations and teaching them explicitly, not teaching "close reading."** This reframes "is CARS coachable?" as "have we described its item types precisely enough to teach them?"

8. **The prep-resistance of CARS is an empirical vacuum, not an established fact, and the team should stop treating it as either settled truth or settled falsehood.** Fact 24: no peer-reviewed CARS intervention study exists. The transferable evidence is small verbal coaching effects from quasi-experimental designs on other tests, one of them negative (facts 7–9). My own conversion (§3.2) puts an SAT-verbal-sized effect at roughly **0.2 CARS scaled points** and a GRE-analytical-sized effect at roughly **2 points** — an order of magnitude apart, and the difference is method. This is an opportunity as much as a caveat: a team that instruments its own cohort properly would be generating the first real evidence in this space.

9. **Strategy instruction is aimed at the wrong moderator for this population.** SERT/iSTART works for low-knowledge and low-strategy readers and did **nothing** for high-prior-knowledge students in the largest classroom sample (fact 21). Pre-meds are high-knowledge, high-strategy readers. Meanwhile calibration is poor *even in skilled readers* (fact 3 and the "skilled readers" framing in §1.2). **Calibration is where proficient adults have real headroom; strategy is where they do not.** Any roadmap choice between "teach reading strategies" and "measure and correct calibration" should go to the second.

10. **Answer-choice evaluation is a separable, substantial, and probably more tractable target than passage comprehension — and it is trainable through distractor anatomy.** Examinees score well above chance with no passage, using cross-question consistency and theme reconstruction (fact 17), and Powers and Leung found the *successful* strategies were the more construct-relevant ones. So option evaluation done well *is* argument evaluation. Feedback that names each distractor's failure mode (too strong, too narrow, true-but-not-asserted, right relation wrong direction) supplies exactly the idea-unit standard that improved self-assessment in fact 18. **This is the most concrete, most immediately buildable recommendation in this document.**

11. **On production versus selection: the object of production decides everything, and this is the finding most likely to overturn a team's assumptions.** Producing arguments **about a passage** is well-evidenced (writing about text ES 0.40, fact 19; delayed summarising Δ +0.210; self-explaining Δ +0.179) and generative practice with feedback even beats selection practice on selection criteria (fact 20). Producing arguments **about one's own opinion with no passage** is the one case where the transfer was directly tested and **not detected**: a year of dense dialogic argumentation moved production a lot and evaluation from 5.66 to 6.17 of 9, *p* = .10 (§4e). Kuhn's proposed reason — evaluation carries meta-level demands that production does not — is exactly the CARS construct. **A debate mechanic without a passage trains the half of the skill that transfers to producing arguments and not the half that CARS scores.** Adding a passage to the debate is a small change that recruits the entire writing-to-read and self-explanation literature in its favour.

12. **Two things must co-occur or neither works: generation and correction.** Without feedback, generative practice *loses* to multiple-choice practice (fact 20). Without generation, feedback is worth Δ ≈ −0.095 (fact 1 table). Most prep products fail one side: question banks give correction without generation (answer-marking plus rationale-reading), and free-form writing or debate gives generation without correction (no keyed standard to score against). **The design target is the intersection, and it is unoccupied.**

13. **Working-memory capacity is a genuine source of CARS difficulty and a confirmed dead end as a feature.** r = .41–.52 with comprehension and integration (fact 13); far transfer = 0 (fact 14). The productive use of the finding is that high-WM readers benefit *most* from goal manipulations (fact 15) — so the same construct that cannot be trained directly predicts who will respond to the goal-setting intervention that can be.

14. **Interest-matching and vocabulary drilling are the two most requestable features with the weakest support.** Interest r = .13 and partly an effect rather than a cause (fact 22); vocabulary has no L1-adult evidence base at this level (§1.4). Both will be asked for. Both are order-of-magnitude worse bets than calibration. If shipped, ship them as engagement features and do not attribute score movement to them.

## What I could not verify

1. **Any peer-reviewed effect size for coaching, tutoring, or any intervention on MCAT CARS.** I searched for scholarly work and found only: a scientific-thinking classroom module evaluated on MCAT2015 *preview-guide* passage items in introductory psychology (not CARS; Stevens & Witkow, 2014); a full-length-practice-exam predictive study with **N = 19** (correlational, doi:10.1177/2382120520981979); a test-anxiety near-peer-coaching pilot; and a doctoral dissertation on LLM-generated CARS explanations for non-U.S. students (qualitative, no score outcome). Every quantitative CARS improvement claim I encountered — "10-point section jump in 8 weeks," "Reasoning Beyond the Text is 40% of your score," "strategy-focused prep produces the largest gains" — came from **commercial prep sites with no data or methods** (**C**, do not cite).
2. **The AAMC raw-to-scaled conversion table for the 53-question CARS section.** Without it I cannot express effect sizes as "questions correct." The one published AAMC illustration implies roughly 2–3 raw items per scaled point mid-scale, but I am not certain it refers to CARS.
3. **The SAT and GRE section standard deviations from primary sources.** My *d* values in §3.2 assume SD ≈ 110 (SAT section) and ≈ 100–110 (GRE section). These are conventional figures I did not verify. All conversions in that table are **[own calculation]** and should be treated as order-of-magnitude.
4. **The exact Δ MC for practice-testing interventions versus non-intervention** in Yang et al. — the figure was truncated in my extracted text. I have the two adjacent values (practice tests vs reading questions-and-answers, **Δ +0.264**; reading questions-and-answers vs non-intervention, **−0.095, ns**) and the authors' statement that practice tests did enhance accuracy, but not the headline coefficient.
5. **Any study giving learners explicit numeric calibration feedback on reading comprehension and measuring downstream comprehension gains.** The metacomprehension literature manipulates *how and when judgments are made*, not *whether calibration history is displayed*. §4(c) is therefore extrapolation.
6. **Any study of vocabulary as a limiting factor for L1 college-educated adults on admissions-test reading passages.** The lexical-coverage literature (Hu & Nation; Schmitt et al.; Laufer) is entirely L2.
7. **Component-skills regressions (inference, monitoring, integration, WM, vocabulary) in a college-educated adult sample.** Cain and Oakhill's work is with 7–11-year-olds. The extension to proficient adults is theoretically reasonable and empirically unsupported by anything I found.
8. **Metacomprehension accuracy on argument-structure items specifically.** The literature uses factual, inferential, recall, and general multiple-choice criterion tests. Whether calibration is even worse for "what is the author doing" items than for "what did the text say" items is unknown and would be a valuable thing for this team to measure.
9. **Think-aloud or eye-tracking data on *adult* answer-option evaluation in an admissions-test context.** The option-reading eye-tracking work I found is with third, fifth, and eighth graders, plus one ESL study at three proficiency levels. I extrapolated cautiously and flagged it.
10. **Whether MCAT CARS is speeded in the psychometric sense.** No AAMC speededness analysis or completion-rate data found. My claim that time pressure is a symptom rather than the binding constraint is a mechanism argument, not a measurement.
11. **Any comparison of timed-early versus timed-late practice schedules** for reading-test preparation. §4(d) is entirely mechanism-based inference.
12. **Whether Kuhn's evaluation finding replicates, or holds in adults.** It is one study, middle-schoolers, small N, a 9-item 2-option instrument, *p* = .10 in the positive direction. It is the closest test of the production→evaluation transfer question I could find, and it is thin. My verdict on (e) leans on it more heavily than I would like, which is why I paired it with the object-of-production distinction that is independently supported by facts 19–21.
13. **The Freedle/Kostin vs Katz & Lautenschlager exchange.** I know it exists and that the passage-independence finding is contested; I did not read the rebuttals and cannot adjudicate.
14. **Full text of several sources.** Notably Cain, Oakhill & Bryant (2004), Powers & Leung (1995), Katz et al. (1990), Montgomery & Lilly (2012), Kang et al. (2007), Dunlosky & Rawson (2012), Melby-Lervåg et al. (2016), the 2018 reading-purpose eye-tracking study, Kintsch (1988), and Zwaan & Radvansky (1998) — abstract plus quoted result passages only. Every number I report from these I saw quoted verbatim; none is reconstructed from memory.

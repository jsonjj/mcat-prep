# Post-error feedback and retry: what should happen when a learner gets a practice item wrong?

Literature review with design recommendation. Scope: standalone 4-option MCQs, pre-written per-choice rationales, 10–30 item sessions, MCAT prep.

---

## Recommendation

**Verdict on the shipped design: keep it. Make one evidence-backed fix, plan one evidence-backed change the team has already identified, and treat the core deferral as an open question to measure rather than a defect to remove.**

**I overstated the case against Design B in the first draft of this document, and the two objections I called strongest do not survive contact with the shipped code.** The first draft rested on (i) Finn & Metcalfe's (2010) minimal-feedback condition, in which the correct answer was **never supplied at all**, and (ii) an argument that a lucky retry among the three remaining options would let roughly a third of errors escape without an explanation. The shipped flow supplies the key and the correct-answer rationale within about three items, and `subAfterSecondAttempt = () => 'reveal'` fires unconditionally, right or wrong. Neither objection applies. Both are retracted below and requalified where they appear in `## Findings`. The correction is material, not cosmetic: it moves the shipped design from "contradicted by the literature" to "inside the range the literature cannot adjudicate."

What actually ships is a **single ~3-item deferral of the key and the correct-answer rationale, with the chosen-distractor refutation delivered immediately and full disclosure guaranteed on the second attempt.** That is not answer-withholding. It is a spaced two-attempt retrieval sequence with terminal elaborated feedback, and against the alternative I originally recommended — immediate elaborated feedback plus a later re-test — the expected difference is small and its sign is not established.

Ranked by evidential strength:

| # | Change | Basis | Strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Stop the end-of-session clamp from producing a massed retry.** When `min(index + 1 + gap, len)` cannot honour the gap, reveal fully on the first miss instead of retrying immediately. | Lag-0 retry is the single condition in this literature with direct evidence of harm: **.19** at two days vs. **.60** at lag 5, and worse than one delayed test (**.36**) — Karpicke & Roediger (2007) | **The evidence says change this.** Tier B |
| 2 | **Serve a fresh sibling item rather than the same item on the retry** (already recorded internally as a known stand-in). | Not retention — Butler (2010) is a null. Measurement validity (Butler et al. 2013: .62 repeated vs. .30 new) plus a defect specific to this flow: refuting one option turns the retry into a 3-option item solvable by elimination | **The evidence says change this**, on validity grounds rather than learning grounds. Tier B/C |
| 3 | **Ensure the concept returns in a later session,** whatever happens within one. | Cepeda et al. (2008): optimal gap is 20–40% of a one-week retention interval, 5–10% at a year. Every within-session gap is far below optimum for a months-out exam | **The evidence says change this**, if it is not already handled outside this file. Tier A |
| 4 | `RETRY_GAP = 3` versus 5 | Karpicke & Roediger tested 0, 1, 5, 9 — never 2, 3, or 4 — on vocabulary pairs taking seconds each | **Product preference. The evidence cannot distinguish these.** Leave it |
| 5 | Deferring the correct-answer rationale by ~3 items | Genuinely open; see `## The shipped design` | **Product preference. A/B it, do not argue it** |

**Confidence: high** that the shipped design is not harmful and should not be reverted to immediate full reveal on the strength of current evidence. **Medium** on changes 1–3. **Low** on any claim about the sign of the deferral effect itself.

**Single strongest piece of evidence behind the verdict:** Butler, Karpicke & Roediger (2007), the only study that cleanly separates feedback timing from multiple-try structure in a multiple-choice content-learning paradigm. Deferring full feedback to the end of a block cost nothing and numerically helped (**d = 0.28**, ns, at a 10-minute delay; **d = 0.47**, significant, at a one-day delay), and the multiple-try manipulation was a flat null (.71 vs. .71; .64 vs. .65). A ~3-item deferral sits below the shorter of their two delays. The honest reading is that this design change is expected to be worth approximately zero in either direction — which is a reason not to spend engineering effort reversing it, not a reason to celebrate it.

**The one thing that has not changed:** for a **transfer** test — which is what the MCAT is, since every real exam item is novel — bare correct-answer feedback is worth approximately nothing. Butler, Godbole & Marsh (2013) found correct-answer feedback produced .30 on new inference questions versus .28 for *no feedback at all* (t < 1, null), while explanation feedback produced .45 (d = 0.90 vs. correct-answer feedback). The shipped design delivers the explanation. That it delivers it on the second attempt rather than the first is a timing question; that it delivers it at all is the thing that matters, and this is where the per-choice rationale bank earns its cost.

## Why not the alternatives

This section evaluates the four designs **as specified in the original brief**. The brief's Design B is a harsher variant than what ships; see `## The shipped design` for the re-judgment that governs.

**(A) Immediate full reveal — viable, roughly equal to what ships, but insufficient as literally specified.** Design A as written ("show the correct answer, why it is correct, why the chosen distractor is wrong, move on") is nearly right, and it is what every meta-analysis in this space endorses: elaborated feedback outranks correct-answer-only feedback which outranks right/wrong-only feedback, in that order, in Van der Kleij et al. (2015: 0.49 / 0.32 / 0.05), Mertens et al. (2022: g = 0.71 / 0.49 / 0.24 for lower-order outcomes; 0.46 / 0.22 ns / 0.14 ns for higher-order), and Wisniewski et al. (2020: high-information d = 0.99, corrective d = 0.46, no-information d = 0.24). The defect is the last two words. "Move on" wastes the item. A single correct-answer exposure produces poor durability: in Butler & Roediger (2008), immediate feedback converted 31% of multiple-choice errors into correct answers a week later — real, but that leaves 69% still wrong. Karpicke & Roediger (2007) show that adding a spaced re-retrieval of the same material at a lag of five items lifted two-day retention from .24 to .36 (d = 0.48) over an immediate-only retrieval. A is right about *what* to show and wrong to stop there.

**(B) Withheld reveal with delayed retry — rejected as specified in the brief; the shipped variant is not this design and is not rejected.** The distinction is load-bearing, so state it first. The brief's B **"reveals fully only after a second failure"** — a *conditional* reveal, which means a learner who gets the retry right never receives the correct-answer explanation. The shipped flow instead reveals **unconditionally** on the second attempt, right or wrong, and does so about three items after the miss. Two of the three objections below therefore apply only to the brief's variant.

*Objection 1 — applies to the brief's variant, and only in its extreme form.* Finn & Metcalfe's (2010) minimal-feedback condition produced .08–.11 final retention with a 4–9% self-correction rate, and Pashler et al. (2005, N = 258) found right/wrong-only feedback statistically indistinguishable from no feedback and, on the immediate retest, slightly *worse* than the pre-feedback baseline (t(44) = 2.6, p < .05), while correct-answer feedback raised one-week retention by 494%. **These conditions never supplied the correct answer at any point.** They are evidence about permanent silence, not about a three-item deferral, and I was wrong to deploy them against a design that guarantees disclosure. What Finn & Metcalfe's 4–9% figure still legitimately supplies is a bound on how much an *unaided* retry corrects by itself, which bears on §5 below.

*Objection 2 — retracted for the shipped flow.* The structural arithmetic argument was this: on a 4-option item, refuting the chosen distractor leaves three candidates, so a blind retry succeeds by chance roughly a third of the time, and under a conditional reveal that ~33% of errors is resolved by a lucky guess with the correct-answer explanation never shown. Butler, Karpicke & Roediger's (2007) answer-until-correct data give the cost of late or lucky arrival: items where the correct option was selected first retained at .80, items where it was reached last retained at .37. This is a real defect **of the brief's conditional-reveal rule** and the shipped `subAfterSecondAttempt = () => 'reveal'` closes it completely. Against the shipped design the objection is void.

*Objection 3 — survives in principle, but does not reach a three-item gap.* An endorsed distractor that is never corrected becomes a durable false belief. Butler & Roediger (2008) found unfeedback multiple-choice errors produced .24 lure intrusions a week later against a .16 no-test baseline (d = 0.65), and learners rated those intrusions at the same confidence whether or not feedback had been given — feedback works all-or-none, either it corrects the error or the misinformation retains full potency. Fazio et al. (2010) showed the negative effect shrinks but is not eliminated after a week. But every one of these measurements is of an error left uncorrected *through to the criterion test*, days or a week later. None of them measures an error held for three items and then corrected. The persistence literature indicts permanent non-correction; it says nothing about a few minutes of suspense.

What remains true of the brief's B, and worth keeping: its partial feedback is genuinely more informative than bare right/wrong, I could find no experiment manipulating *exactly* distractor-refutation-without-answer, and the burden of proof sits with any design that defers. The shipped design discharges much more of that burden than the brief's variant does.

**(C) Answer-until-correct — viable but worse, and it carries a specific hazard.** The best-controlled tests find it no better than simply showing the answer. Butler, Karpicke & Roediger (2007) disentangled AUC from feedback timing (previous studies had confounded them) across two experiments (N = 48, N = 40) and found AUC and standard feedback identical: .71 vs. .71, then .64 vs. .65 overall, and .52 vs. .53 and .41 vs. .43 conditional on an initial error. Clariana's review of 30 studies (reported in Narciss, 2008) found all feedback types beat no feedback but no single-try versus multiple-try difference. Mertens et al. (2022) put AUC at g = 0.55 (lower-order) and 0.41 (higher-order), above KCR but below elaborated feedback, with wide overlapping intervals. Finn & Metcalfe (2010) found AUC *below* standard feedback on an immediate test (.61 vs. .73). The hazard: AUC forces additional distractor endorsements. Subjects in Finn & Metcalfe selected 2.7–2.9 wrong options before finding the right one, and Butler et al. explicitly flag that each additional lure selection is an additional dose of the misinformation that drives the negative suggestion effect. For a four-option item the exposure is smaller than in these six-option studies, but the direction is unfavourable and the upside is a measured null.

**(D) Minimal feedback — rejected outright, worst option available.** Right/wrong-only is the weakest feedback type in every meta-analysis: Van der Kleij et al. (2015) 0.05; Mertens et al. (2022) 0.24 (lower-order) and 0.14, non-significant (higher-order); Wisniewski et al. (2020) 0.24; Swart et al. (2019) found knowledge-of-response inferior to both elaborated and correct-answer feedback. Pashler et al. (2005) found it equivalent to nothing. Ryan et al. (2020), in the study closest to this product's task, measured median feedback reading time of 3 seconds for right/wrong versus 25–52 seconds for elaborated forms — learners do not engage with it because there is nothing to engage with. Worst of all, D leaves the endorsed distractor uncorrected, which is the condition under which Butler & Roediger (2008) observed testing to be *net harmful*: in their no-study/no-feedback cell, lure intrusions (.32) exceeded correct answers (.18), and the tested condition was worse than not having been tested at all.

## The shipped design

Verified in `apps/learner-web/src/screens/practice/session-flow.ts`: `RETRY_GAP = 3`; a miss is re-inserted via `resurfaceAt(index, len) = min(index + 1 + gap, len)`; the first miss shows the refutation of the chosen option with the key and correct-answer rationale redacted to `null` at the render boundary; `subAfterSecondAttempt = () => 'reveal'` discloses the key, the correct-answer rationale and the refutation of whatever was picked, unconditionally; the retry re-presents the same item; a correct first attempt is confirmed immediately.

### The contrast that actually needs judging

Not "reveal versus silence." The real comparison is:

- **Shipped:** retrieve → refutation of chosen option → ~3 items → retrieve again, unaided → full elaborated reveal. Two retrieval attempts, one elaborated feedback delivery, the second attempt unaided.
- **A-upgraded (my original recommendation):** retrieve → full elaborated reveal → later → retrieve a sibling → feedback. Two retrieval attempts, two elaborated feedback deliveries, the second attempt informed.

Both make the learner err, both correct the error, both re-test. The differences are the *ordering* of the explanation relative to the second attempt, the *number* of elaborated exposures, and *which item* the second attempt uses. That is a much narrower gap than the first draft of this document implied.

### Does Butler, Godbole & Marsh (2013) indict the deferral?

**No, and this is the most important correction in the pass.** BGM is a **content** manipulation, not a timing one. Their three conditions were no feedback, correct-answer feedback, and explanation feedback, all delivered at the same moment; the d = 0.90 transfer advantage is what you get from *having* an explanation versus not having one. The shipped design has one. It arrives on attempt two.

The residual worry is that the shipped flow delivers the transfer-carrying content **once**, whereas A-upgraded delivers it twice (once at the miss, once after the sibling). Karpicke & Roediger (2007) gave feedback after every test in the schedules that performed best, so more feedback deliveries are weakly favoured — but that is confounded with time on task and was never manipulated. **Tier C.** Call the cost of the deferral itself small and of unknown sign; call the cost of the single exposure small, plausibly real, and cheap to fix independently of the deferral (nothing stops the flow from surfacing the same rationale again in a later review).

### Does the interposed unaided retry earn its place?

Probably not much, but it does not cost anything either.

The errorful-generation literature (§5) supports making a retrieval attempt before being told, but with two conditions that bite here. First, the benefit requires the error to be semantically related to the target — Huelser & Metcalfe (2012), Grimaldi & Karpicke (2012), Knight et al. (2012) — and **Kang et al. (2011) found no benefit when learners were forced to guess with no knowledge**. The shipped retry is a second attempt at an item the learner just failed, having received exactly one piece of eliminative information. Whether that is a productive generation event or a forced guess depends on the item and the learner, and the flow cannot tell which.

Second, the base rates say most retry successes will not be understanding. Finn & Metcalfe (2010) measured unaided self-correction at **4–9%** in cued recall. On a 4-option item with one option refuted, chance alone yields roughly 33%. The gap between those numbers is guessing and elimination, not insight. Metcalfe & Finn (2011) do show second guesses are more often correct after high-confidence errors, so the retry is not worthless — but the yield is small and the flow currently cannot distinguish a reasoned recovery from a coin flip.

Against that, the retry is nearly free, and the meta-analytic estimate for multiple-try structures is positive: Mertens et al. (2022) put AUC at g = 0.554 (lower-order) and 0.412 (higher-order), above KCR though below EF. Butler, Karpicke & Roediger (2007) found multiple-try a flat null in the closest paradigm. **Expected value: between zero and slightly positive. Tier B.** Keep it; do not defend it strongly.

### Is a refutation without the key usable corrective feedback across a 3-item interval?

For the purpose it is actually serving, yes — and the question matters far less than the first draft claimed, because the refutation is a way-station rather than a terminus.

The reason "is partial feedback enough?" is a serious question in the literature is that partial feedback there is the *only* feedback (Pashler's right/wrong-only; Finn & Metcalfe's minimal condition; the KR row in every meta-analysis at 0.05 / 0.24 / 0.24). The harm those studies measure is a false belief surviving to a criterion test days later. The shipped design corrects the error within about three items, which on the assumption that an MCAT item runs 60–120 seconds is roughly three to six minutes. Nothing in the persistence literature (§8) speaks to a window that short.

There is also a point in the shipped design's favour that the first draft missed. The refutation is delivered **at the moment of surprise**, which is where the hypercorrection literature locates the high-attention window: Butterfield & Metcalfe (2006) demonstrated attentional capture during feedback to high-confidence errors via tone-detection interference, and Van Loon et al. (2015, via Metcalfe 2017) found misconception correction occurred with refutation texts that explicitly named and rebutted the misconception but not with texts merely stating the correct information. The shipped flow spends the surprise window on exactly the refutation content that literature says is potent.

The mirror-image concern is the best remaining argument for immediate full reveal: if the surprise window is where attention peaks, deferring the *constructive* half of the correction moves it outside that window and into a moment when the learner has just guessed again and the surprise has decayed. This is a mechanistic inference from a paradigm that never tested the split. **Tier C.** It is a reason to run the experiment, not a reason to change the code.

### Audit: does Butler, Karpicke & Roediger (2007) support the withhold?

**Partially, and more weakly than the internal citation implies. Half of what it is being asked to support, it does not address.**

What BKR actually establishes, both **Tier A** within its paradigm:

- **Deferring full feedback is not harmful and may help.** d = 0.28 (ns) with a 10-minute delay and one-day retention; d = 0.47 (significant) with a one-day delay and one-week retention. The advantage grew with retention interval.
- **Multiple-try structure is a null.** AUC versus standard feedback: .71 vs. .71 and .64 vs. .65 overall, .52 vs. .53 and .41 vs. .43 conditional on an initial error.

Three mismatches with the shipped design:

1. **Magnitude does not transfer at this gap.** BKR's shorter delay — the whole test block, about 10 minutes — produced d = 0.28, non-significant. The significant d = 0.47 came from the *one-day* delay. A three-item gap is shorter than the shorter of the two. The defensible reading is that the deferral is worth something at or below a non-significant d = 0.28, which is to say approximately nothing.
2. **BKR's delayed conditions gave the learner nothing at the moment of error.** They did not deliver a partial refutation and then defer the rest. The shipped design's specific manipulation is untested.
3. **BKR does not support the retry component at all.** Its multiple-try result is a null, and the paper's contribution was precisely to *disentangle* multiple-try from timing because prior work had confounded them. Citing it for a design that bundles a deferral with a retry re-bundles what BKR separated.

Verdict on the citation: **defensible for "deferring the reveal by a few items should not hurt," which is a null-ish claim. Not support for "deferring is better," and not support for the retry.** The internal citation should be rewritten to claim the weaker thing.

**The Pashler et al. (2005) citation on `subAfterSecondAttempt` is correct and well-chosen.** Pashler is exactly about the necessity of supplying the correct answer, and an unconditional reveal is the right way to honour it. That comment is doing real work: it is the line of code that makes the two objections in the first draft of this document inapplicable.

### The three surviving deltas

**Delta 1 — deferring the correct-answer rationale on the first miss. Worth approximately zero, sign unknown. Confidence: low. Do not change on evidence; measure it.**
For it: BKR's d = 0.28/0.47 for deferral; Mertens' AUC estimates; the surprise-window argument for spending the moment of error on refutation. Against it: Metcalfe, Kornell & Finn (2009) showed the adult delayed-feedback advantage vanishes once lag-to-test is controlled, so much of BKR's effect is a recency artifact that a 3-item gap will not reproduce; Van der Kleij et al. (2015) found delayed timing *negatively* predicted effect sizes in computer-based environments (abstract only); Kulik & Kulik (1988) found applied settings favour immediate feedback, which Metcalfe (2017) attributes to whether feedback processing is actually enforced — and Ryan et al.'s (2020) 3-second median reading time for uninformative feedback shows how fast unenforced processing collapses. The unmeasured risk is not retention, it is engagement and abandonment: a learner who is told they are wrong and denied the answer may skip the session, and no study in this review measures that.

**Delta 2 — `RETRY_GAP = 3` versus the lag-5 evidence. Noise. Confidence: high that this does not matter. Leave it.**
Karpicke & Roediger (2007) tested lags of 0, 1, 5, and 9. They never tested 2, 3, or 4, so the literature cannot distinguish 3 from 5, and constructing a preference between them would be manufactured precision. What their data do establish is the shape: lag 0 is catastrophic (.19 at two days), lag 5 beats lag 1 (36% vs. 24%, d = 0.48), and uniform beats expanding (.60 vs. .49). Three is comfortably on the right side of the only boundary the data locate, which is the one between zero and non-zero. There is also a unit problem that cuts in the shipped design's favour: their items were vocabulary pairs taking seconds each, so five of their intervening items is likely *less* wall-clock time and less interference than three MCAT items. **Tier B.**

**Delta 3 — same item versus a fresh sibling. The best-supported change on the list, and the team already knows it. Confidence: medium.**
There is no retention argument: Butler (2010) found same-question and rephrased-question repeated testing statistically indistinguishable on retention and on near and far transfer (all t < 1), though at N = 24 per experiment that null cannot exclude a modest effect. **Tier C** on retention. The case rests on three other things, and the third is specific to this flow and is the strongest:

1. *Measurement validity.* Butler et al. (2013) got .62 on repeated questions and .30 on new questions from the identical intervention. A same-item retry after a partial reveal will produce an impressive second-attempt success rate that says little about the concept. Any mastery or scheduling signal computed from it will be inflated.
2. *Distractor hygiene.* Roediger & Marsh (2005) found lure production scales with exposure to lures. Re-presenting the identical item re-exposes the exact distractor the learner just endorsed. A sibling swaps the distractor set.
3. *The elimination artifact.* This is the one the general literature does not cover. Because the first miss refutes the chosen option, the retry is effectively a **3-option item**, and the learner can reach the key by elimination without engaging the concept. That both inflates the success rate and hollows out the retrieval attempt that is supposed to justify the deferral in the first place. A fresh sibling restores a full 4-option decision that cannot be solved by remembering "not C."

One design constraint the team should price before making this change: if the second attempt is a sibling, the original item's key still has to be disclosed somewhere, or the flow reintroduces exactly the permanent-silence failure mode that Pashler et al. rules out. Disclosing the original alongside the sibling's feedback, or at end of session, both work; neither is free.

### Verdict

**The shipped design should not be reverted.** It is not the design the first draft of this document rejected, and against the design it actually is, the literature does not establish a difference. Fix the end-of-session clamp, because a lag-0 retry is the one thing here with direct evidence of harm (.19 versus .60 at two days) and the clamp produces gaps of 0, 1, and 2 for misses in the final three positions. Move to fresh siblings, on validity and elimination-artifact grounds rather than learning grounds. Confirm the concept recurs across sessions, because Cepeda et al. (2008) imply every within-session gap is far below optimum for an exam months away. Leave `RETRY_GAP = 3` alone. Treat the deferral itself as the thing to A/B, and rewrite the internal BKR citation to claim only what BKR supports.

## Findings

### 1. Immediate versus delayed feedback

**The delayed-feedback effect is real but small, paradigm-specific, largely reducible to spacing, and it reverses in applied settings. For a self-paced app, feed back immediately.**

Kulik & Kulik (1988, 53 studies) is the canonical summary and is routinely misquoted. Their actual finding is a three-way split: applied studies using real classroom quizzes and real materials usually favoured **immediate** feedback; experimental studies of acquisition of test content usually favoured **delayed**; laboratory list-learning was mixed. (Abstract only; the Visible Learning database attributes a weighted mean of 0.49 across 53 effects to this meta-analysis, but that figure appears to represent feedback effects generally rather than the immediate-versus-delayed contrast, and I could not verify which.) **Tier A** for the pattern, **Tier C** for the 0.49.

In the paradigm that most resembles this product — multiple-choice tests over prose content — delayed feedback wins modestly. Butler, Karpicke & Roediger (2007) found .73 vs. .68 (d = 0.28, non-significant) with a 10-minute delay and one-day retention, and .70 vs. .60 (d = 0.47, significant) with a one-day delay and one-week retention; the effect grew with retention interval. Butler & Roediger (2008, N = 72) found .56 vs. .45 (d = 0.56) at one week. **Tier B** (two studies, one lab, same group).

Three things defuse this for the present design. First, "delayed" in these studies means *end of the test block* — minutes. Butler & Roediger say so explicitly: "what we are calling delayed feedback is what many instructors who cannot use computerized testing would see as immediate feedback." So the finding does not license withholding for hours or days. Second, Metcalfe, Kornell & Finn (2009) showed that when lag-to-test is controlled, the delayed advantage **disappears entirely for college students** (it persisted for Grade 6 children). Much of the effect is a recency artifact of the delayed group having seen the correct answer more recently. **Tier B**. Third, Van der Kleij et al. (2015) found delayed timing *negatively* predicted effect sizes in computer-based learning environments across 70 effect sizes, and Metcalfe's (2017) reading of the laboratory/applied split is that the operative variable is whether the learner actually attends to the feedback — lab studies force it, classrooms and apps cannot. Ryan et al.'s (2020) 3-second median reading time for uninformative feedback is a concrete demonstration that unenforced feedback processing collapses.

Transfer of list-learning findings to this context: **poor**. Butler et al. (2007) and Butler & Roediger (2008) used 280–400-word prose passages with 4–6 alternative factual questions and cued-recall criterion tests. MCAT items are multi-step reasoning over novel stimuli. The delayed-feedback mechanism (letting the incorrect response dissipate to reduce response competition, per Kulhavy's interference-perseveration account) is specific to a competition between two discrete verbal responses. It is not obvious there is an analogous competition when the error is a reasoning-chain failure rather than a wrong lexical item. Treat the delayed advantage as unproven here and take the engagement argument instead.

### 2. Is withholding the correct answer after an error harmful?

**Yes, where "withholding" means never supplying it. This is the strongest, most consistent finding in the review.** Tier A.

**Scope qualification, added on revision.** Every study below withholds the correct answer *permanently* — through to a criterion test days or a week later. None of them defers it and then supplies it. These results establish that an error must be corrected; they do not establish *when*, and they cannot be used against a design that corrects within a few minutes. §1 is the section that governs timing, and it is much weaker. I originally applied this section's numbers to a three-item deferral, which was an error of scope.

Direct evidence, ordered by how closely it manipulates the variable of interest:

| Study | Manipulation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Finn & Metcalfe (2010), Exps 1–3, N = 24/25/18 | "Minimal feedback": told wrong, one unaided retry, correct answer never supplied, vs. standard/scaffolded/AUC | Minimal: **.08 / .09 / .11** final retention. All answer-supplying conditions: **.51–.77**. Self-correction at feedback: **.09 / .04 / .09** |
| Pashler et al. (2005), N = 258, Luganda–English pairs, 1-week final | Correct-answer vs. right-wrong-only vs. 0-s blank vs. 5-s blank | Correct answer **+494%** retention over no feedback after errors. Right/wrong-only indistinguishable from blank screen; showed a small *decline* from Test 1 to Test 2 |
| Butler & Roediger (2008), N = 72, 1-week final | No feedback / immediate / delayed, conditional on initial MC error | Errors later answered correctly: **.08** (no feedback) vs. **.31** (immediate) vs. **.46** (delayed) |
| Butler, Karpicke & Roediger (2007) | No-feedback control | Only **.11** of errors spontaneously corrected without feedback |

Metcalfe's (2017) *Annual Review of Psychology* synthesis states the position flatly: "People get virtually no benefit unless the feedback they receive provides the correct answer." Meta-analytically, the feedback type that omits the correct answer (KR) is the floor in every synthesis: 0.05 (Van der Kleij), 0.24 lower-order / 0.14 ns higher-order (Mertens), 0.24 (Wisniewski).

**Does partial feedback — refuting the chosen distractor without supplying the answer — count as corrective feedback?** This is the crux and the honest answer is that **no study manipulates exactly this**, so the conclusion is inferential. The relevant considerations:

- It is *more* than KR. It carries error-specific diagnostic information, which is the defining property of Narciss's "bug-related" / informative tutoring feedback.
- The one study that deliberately withholds the correct answer while supplying error-specific correction — **Narciss & Huth (2006)**, N = 50 — found bug-related feedback *superior* to KR-KCR for both achievement and motivation. This is the only real counter-evidence I found and I take it seriously. But: fourth-graders with learning difficulties, written subtraction. In a *procedural* domain, telling the learner which rule they misapplied is functionally equivalent to giving them the answer, because they can then execute the rule and derive it. Refuting one distractor on a conceptual MCAT item leaves three candidates and determines nothing. **Tier C** for generalisation to this context, and **abstract only** — I could not obtain effect sizes.
- Metcalfe & Finn (2011, via Metcalfe 2017) found that learners told simply that they were wrong produce a *correct* second guess more often after high-confidence errors than low-confidence errors. So refutation does sometimes unlock the answer. But Finn & Metcalfe's measured base rate for that unlocking, unaided, was 4–9%.
- Van Loon et al. (2015, via Metcalfe 2017) found hypercorrection of scientific misconceptions occurred with **refutation texts** — which name and rebut the misconception — but not with plain texts stating the correct information. This argues strongly that *naming and refuting the specific distractor is valuable*, but the refutation texts also supplied the correct information. It supports A-upgraded, not B.
- Grimaldi & Karpicke (2012, via Metcalfe 2017) found that an *experimenter-presented* wrong answer harmed later memory for the correct answer, whereas a self-generated error helped. A multiple-choice distractor is experimenter-presented, then endorsed. That is the less favourable case.

Verdict: distractor refutation is a genuine and valuable component of corrective feedback, but it is not a substitute for the correct answer. Ship both. Whether "both" must arrive in the same moment, or may be separated by a few items, is not settled by anything in this section — see `## The shipped design`.

### 3. Multiple-try feedback versus single-try with the answer given

**Roughly a wash, with a small higher-order edge for MTF that does not survive in the best-controlled multiple-choice studies.** Tier B.

- **Mertens et al. (2022)**, network meta-analysis, 77 studies / 163 effect sizes, all effectively immediate feedback. Versus no-feedback: lower-order EF g = 0.714, AUC 0.554, KCR 0.489, KR 0.237; higher-order EF 0.462, AUC 0.412 (p = .039), KCR 0.224 (ns), KR 0.136 (ns). 95% prediction intervals all span zero, so ranking is more reliable than magnitude.
- **Clariana (1993)**, review of 30 studies (read via Narciss, 2008): all feedback beat no feedback; **no difference between single-try and multiple-try**.
- **Clariana & Koul (2005)**: AUC less effective for verbatim outcomes, more effective for higher-order outcomes. A secondary source reports ES = 0.11 for MTF under higher-order outcomes; I could not verify this in the original (obscure journal, no full text found). **Tier C**.
- **Butler, Karpicke & Roediger (2007)**, the only study that cleanly disentangles multiple-try from feedback timing in a multiple-choice content-learning paradigm: **null in both experiments**, including in the conditional analysis restricted to initially-incorrect items (.52 vs. .53; .41 vs. .43).
- **Finn & Metcalfe (2010)**: AUC below standard immediately (.61 vs. .73), equal at delay (.56 vs. .53; .52 vs. .51), below scaffolded throughout.

**Moderators worth noting.** Mertens et al. found prior knowledge flips the ranking: for **low** prior knowledge learners (m = 43), AUC was best (g = 0.77), ahead of EF (0.72), KCR (0.64), KR (0.40); for **high** prior knowledge learners (m = 71), EF dominated (0.74) and AUC fell to 0.48. Item format also matters: for multiple-choice items specifically, EF 0.55, AUC 0.50, KCR 0.42, KR 0.15 (ns) — feedback effects were uniformly *smaller* with multiple-choice than with constructed-response items (EF 1.11 with constructed response). Undergraduates specifically: EF 0.76, AUC 0.62, KCR 0.60.

Implication for MCAT prep: the population is high-prior-knowledge undergraduates on multiple-choice items, which is the cell where elaborated feedback wins and multiple-try adds least.

### 4. Elaborated feedback versus just the correct answer

**Elaborated feedback adds a small-to-moderate increment on repeated items and a large increment on transfer. For this product, transfer is the only outcome that matters, so the gain is decisive.** Tier A.

Meta-analytic estimates for EF over KCR:

| Source | EF | KCR | KR |
|---|---|---|---|
| Van der Kleij et al. (2015), 40 studies / 70 ES | 0.49 | 0.32 | 0.05 |
| Mertens et al. (2022), lower-order | 0.714 | 0.489 | 0.237 |
| Mertens et al. (2022), higher-order | 0.462 | 0.224 (ns) | 0.136 (ns) |
| Wisniewski et al. (2020), 435 studies, N > 61,000 | 0.99 (high-info, k = 42) | 0.46 (corrective, k = 238) | 0.24 (reinforcement, k = 39) |

Note the pattern: the EF-over-KCR gap roughly **doubles** for higher-order outcomes (0.46 vs. 0.22 in Mertens; Van der Kleij likewise reports EF was "particularly more effective than KR and KCR for higher-order learning outcomes").

The two primary studies that matter most here:

**Butler, Godbole & Marsh (2013,** *Journal of Educational Psychology***).** N = 60 (Exp 1, between-subjects), 2-day delay. On **repeated** questions: no feedback .43, correct answer .62 (d = 0.85), explanation .66 (d = 1.06) — correct-answer and explanation feedback did not differ. On **new inference** questions: no feedback .28, correct answer **.30**, explanation **.45**; explanation beat correct answer by d = 0.90 (d = 0.61 by item analysis) and correct answer did not beat no feedback (t < 1). Experiment 2 replicated within-subjects: .37 vs. .29. This is the single most important number in the review for a product whose criterion test is entirely novel items: **bare correct-answer feedback bought zero transfer.**

**Ryan et al. (2020,** *Perspectives on Medical Education***).** N = 64 second-year medical students, single-best-answer clinical MCQs requiring application of basic science, tested immediately and at one week. Three feedback types: right/wrong *including identification of the correct option* (so effectively KCR), response-oriented (brief explanation of why each option is correct or incorrect — i.e., precisely the per-choice rationales this product already has), and conceptually focused. Means across both test occasions: **51.3% / 56.3% / 59.4%**, with d = 0.2 and d = 0.4 versus right/wrong. Response-oriented and conceptually focused did not differ. Near transfer 59.1%, far transfer 52.2%. Exploratory and non-significant but suggestive: when the parent item was answered **incorrectly**, far-transfer performance was 46% (right/wrong), 52% (response-oriented), 59% (conceptual), d = 0.6 for conceptual versus right/wrong. This is the closest study in the literature to the actual product and it validates the per-choice rationale design directly. **Tier B** (single study, N = 64, one-week horizon).

**Does the gain justify the reading time?** Ryan et al. measured it. Median feedback reading time: **3 s** for right/wrong, **25 s** for response-oriented, **52 s** for conceptually focused. After an error specifically: 5 s, 31 s, 55 s. So elaboration costs roughly 20–50 additional seconds per missed item. In a 25-item session with a 30% error rate that is 7–8 items × ~40 s ≈ 5 minutes, against d = 0.2–0.4 on transfer plus the difference between some transfer benefit and none (Butler et al. 2013). Yes, it justifies the time. Note also that the time is spent *reading an explanation*, which is itself study, not overhead.

### 5. Errorful generation

**The error-generation literature supports letting learners get things wrong. It does not support withholding the answer, because corrective feedback is supplied in every one of those studies.** Tier A.

Kornell, Hays & Bjork (2009, six experiments): unsuccessful retrieval attempts followed by feedback produced better retention than reading the question and answer together. Their own framing in the General Discussion is explicit: "Unsuccessful attempts to retrieve information from memory **that were accompanied by feedback** enhanced learning in the present experiments." Metcalfe's (2017) review says the same in its thesis sentence: "errorful learning **followed by corrective feedback** is beneficial to learning… Corrective feedback, including analysis of the reasoning leading up to the mistake, **is crucial**." Metcalfe & Kornell (2007) put it bluntly: "errors, when corrected, do not result in interference… Most often, when no feedback was given, responses that were initially wrong simply stayed wrong."

So the answer to the question posed is: **yes, feedback is always supplied**, and the distinction matters enormously. This literature is an argument for tolerating errors — for serving hard items, not filtering them down to a comfortable difficulty — and simultaneously an argument *against* Design B.

Boundary conditions worth carrying forward:
- The benefit requires the error to be **semantically related** to the target. Huelser & Metcalfe (2012), Grimaldi & Karpicke (2012), and Knight et al. (2012) found no benefit for unrelated pairs; Kang et al. (2011) found none when learners were forced to guess with no knowledge. Implication: plausible, misconception-anchored distractors are load-bearing, and random guesses on items far above the learner's level buy nothing. **Tier A**.
- The error must be **self-generated**. Grimaldi & Karpicke found that an experimenter-presented incorrect word *harmed* memory. Multiple-choice distractors are experimenter-presented; the learner's endorsement is what makes it self-generated-ish. This is a reason to expect somewhat weaker error-generation benefits in MC than in the cued-recall paradigms. **Tier B**.
- **Hypercorrection**: high-confidence errors are corrected *more* readily than low-confidence errors, replicated many times (Butterfield & Metcalfe 2001, 2006; Butler et al. 2008, 2011; Fazio & Marsh 2009, 2010; Metcalfe & Finn 2011), with converging ERP (P3a) and fMRI (anterior cingulate) evidence for a surprise/attention mechanism. Van Loon et al. (2015) found this extends to scientific misconceptions **only when refutation texts explicitly named and rebutted the misconception**. Direct product implication: a confidently-held wrong answer is the highest-yield feedback moment you get, and naming the specific distractor is what unlocks it. **Tier A** for the effect, **Tier B** for the refutation-text extension.
- **Exception**: amnesic patients benefit from errorless learning (Glisky et al. 1986; Baddeley & Wilson 1994). Not relevant to this population.

### 6. The retry interval

**Insert a lag before the retry. Any non-zero lag of a few items is supported; the literature cannot resolve finer than that. Use uniform, not expanding, intervals. Never allow a lag of zero. The bigger win is across sessions, not within them.** Tier B.

Karpicke & Roediger (2007, *JEP:LMC*) is the study with actual numbers in intervening-item units. Subjects learned GRE vocabulary pairs; conditions were massed (0–0–0), expanding (1–5–9), and equally spaced (5–5–5), plus single-test controls at lag 1 and lag 5.

Experiment 2 (feedback provided after each test, N = 48), proportion correct:

| Schedule | 10-min retention | 2-day retention |
|---|---|---|
| Massed (0–0–0) | .49 | .19 |
| Expanding (1–5–9) | .90 | .49 |
| Equal (5–5–5) | .87 | **.60** |
| Single test, lag 1 | .60 | .24 |
| Single test, lag 5 | .71 | **.36** |

Three findings transfer directly:

1. **Massed retry is a disaster.** Retrying an item with zero intervening items yielded .19 at two days, worse than a single delayed test. This rules out the "try again right now" pattern as a *retention* strategy (which is separate from the answer-until-correct question in §3).
2. **A lag of five intervening items beats a lag of one**, with feedback, at both horizons: 71% vs. 60% (d = 0.44) at 10 minutes and 36% vs. 24% (d = 0.48) at two days.
3. **Expanding intervals are not better than uniform ones, and were worse at two days** (.49 vs. .60). Experiment 3 isolated the cause: what matters is **delaying the first retrieval attempt**, not the shape of the subsequent schedule. Storm, Bjork & Storm (2010) push back with interference-heavy materials, but the Karpicke & Roediger result held across three experiments and is the more directly applicable one. **Tier B** — one lab, vocabulary pairs.

Is five the *optimum*? No. It is the largest single-test lag they tested and the best-performing one; the true optimum is unbounded above in these data. They tested lags of 0, 1, 5, and 9 and nothing in between, so the literature cannot distinguish a gap of 3 from a gap of 5. There is no study I found that identifies an optimal number of intervening items for multiple-choice reasoning items. Five is a defensible floor, not a tuned value, and treating it as a target precise enough to reject a gap of 3 would be manufactured precision.

**The one place this bites the shipped code.** `resurfaceAt(index, len) = min(index + 1 + gap, len)` clamps the re-insertion point to the end of the queue, so a miss on the final item retries with **zero** intervening items, and misses in the last three positions retry at gaps of 0, 1, and 2. Lag 0 is the single condition in this literature with direct evidence of harm: **.19** at two days, against .60 for lag 5 and .49 for the expanding schedule, and *worse than a single delayed test* (.36). A massed retry is not a weak version of a spaced retry; it is worse than not retrying at all. The clamp is well-motivated — without it a late miss would lose its second pass entirely, and with it the guaranteed disclosure that makes this design defensible — but the right resolution is to drop the deferral rather than the gap: when the gap cannot be honoured, reveal fully on the first miss. That converts the affected items to Design A, which the same literature is comfortable with. Carrying the retry into the next session is the better answer if the scheduler can support it, since §6's own spacing logic favours it. **Tier B**, from vocabulary pairs, but the lag-0 result is large and consistent across their three experiments.

**Within-session versus across-session.** Cepeda et al. (2008, *Psychological Science*, N > 1,350) mapped the gap-by-retention-interval surface: the optimal inter-study gap is about **20–40% of the retention interval** for a one-week horizon, falling to **5–10%** for a one-year horizon; in absolute terms the optimal gap for a 35-day retention interval was 11 days and for a 70-day interval, 21 days. The MCAT retention interval is months. Every within-session gap is therefore far below optimum, and the ratio literature says short gaps hurt long-term retention more than long gaps do. Conclusion: within-session retry is worth doing because it is cheap and beats massing, but the substantive spacing has to happen **across sessions and days**. Do not treat a same-session retry as having consolidated anything. **Tier A** for the spacing function, **Tier B** for the translation to this product.

### 7. Same item versus a new item on the same concept

**No reliable retention or transfer difference has been demonstrated. Prefer a different item, on grounds of measurement validity and distractor hygiene rather than on retention grounds.** Tier C for the retention claim, Tier B for the reasons to prefer a new item.

The one study that tests this head-on is **Butler (2010,** *JEP:LMC***)**. Subjects repeatedly took either the *same* test question or *rephrased* versions of it (the "variable test" condition, motivated by encoding-variability theory), then were tested a week later on repeated questions (Exp 1a), new inferential questions in the same domain (Exp 1b), or new inferential questions in a different domain (Exp 3). Both testing conditions massively beat restudying (e.g., .60 vs. .30, d = 1.03; .57 vs. .30, d = 0.93). **Same-test and variable-test did not differ on any measure** (all t < 1), including on transfer, which is where encoding variability should have shown up. N = 24 per experiment, so this is a null from an underpowered design and cannot exclude a small effect.

Two considerations nonetheless favour a new item:

- **Measurement validity.** Re-presenting the identical item after showing the answer measures recognition of that item, not understanding of the concept. Butler et al. (2013) is the demonstration: correct-answer feedback lifted *repeated* questions from .43 to .62 while lifting *new inference* questions from .28 to .30. If you re-present the same item, you will observe a large improvement that means almost nothing about MCAT performance. This is a serious risk for any adaptive/mastery signal computed from retry data.
- **Distractor hygiene.** The negative suggestion effect is driven by exposure to lures (§8). Re-presenting the identical item re-exposes the identical distractor the learner just endorsed. Roediger & Marsh (2005) found lure production scales with the number of lures read. A different item on the same concept swaps the distractor set.

A third consideration applies only to a flow that refutes the chosen option before the retry, as the shipped one does: **the retry is then effectively a 3-option item**, and the learner can reach the key by elimination without engaging the concept. This inflates the second-attempt success rate and weakens the retrieval attempt that is supposed to justify deferring the explanation. Nothing in the literature measures this directly — it follows from the structure — but it converges with the measurement-validity argument and makes the fresh-sibling change more valuable here than Butler's (2010) null would suggest on its own.

Countervailing: Ryan et al. (2020) found near transfer (59.1%) reliably exceeded far transfer (52.2%), so a *very* different item will look harder and may be discouraging. Their "near transfer" items were the parent item with superficial features changed (age, gender). That is a sensible target: same concept, same reasoning structure, different surface.

### 8. Persistence of the wrong answer

**Endorsing a distractor makes you more likely to produce it later. Feedback that supplies the correct answer eliminates this essentially completely; feedback that does not, does not.** Tier A.

- **Roediger & Marsh (2005)**: prior reading of more multiple-choice lures both decreased the positive testing effect and increased production of those lures as answers on a later cued-recall test — even though subjects were explicitly warned not to guess. "Multiple-choice testing may inadvertently lead to the creation of false knowledge."
- **Butler & Roediger (2008)**, N = 72, one-week cued recall. Lure intrusions: no feedback **.24**, immediate feedback **.15**, delayed feedback **.14**, no-test baseline **.16**. No-feedback versus no-test: d = 0.65. Feedback drove intrusions back to the level of never having seen the lures at all. Immediate and delayed feedback were equally effective at this (unlike for correct responses, where delayed won). The authors' conclusion: "taking a multiple-choice test with feedback is no more harmful than not taking a multiple-choice test at all."
- **Confidence in false answers.** Butler & Roediger measured confidence in intrusions: 39 (all tested conditions, regardless of feedback) versus 26 (no-test), d = 0.56. Feedback did not reduce confidence in the intrusions it failed to correct. Their interpretation — **"feedback works in an all-or-none manner: if students do not successfully correct the error, then feedback does not diminish the potency of the misinformation."** Under free-report instructions, subjects in the no-study/no-feedback cell still chose to keep .19 of their intrusions. They believed them.
- **Net harm is possible.** In Butler & Roediger's no-study/no-feedback cell, intrusions (.32) exceeded correct answers (.18), and the no-test control produced both fewer correct (.10) and fewer intrusions (.17). Unfeedback multiple-choice practice on material the learner has not yet studied is worse than no practice. Directly relevant: MCAT learners routinely practise content they have not yet reviewed.
- **Durability.** Fazio, Agarwal, Marsh & Roediger (2010) tracked the negative testing effect over a one-week delay: it was reduced but **never eliminated**, and it was strongest when the multiple-choice test and the criterion test fell in the same session.
- **Faulty reasoning persists more than guessing.** Huelser & Marsh (2006, cited in Butler & Roediger 2008) found errors from faulty reasoning are more likely to persist than guesses. MCAT distractors are engineered to be reachable by a specific faulty reasoning path. This is the persistence-prone category.

## Where the evidence runs out

1. **Nobody has run Design B.** No study manipulates distractor-refutation-without-the-correct-answer against distractor-refutation-with-it. Finn & Metcalfe's minimal-feedback condition is the closest and it lacked any refutation content. Narciss & Huth (2006) is the closest in the other direction and used fourth-graders doing arithmetic. The rejection of B rests on convergent inference, not a head-to-head trial.
2. **No item counts for reasoning items.** Karpicke & Roediger's "five intervening items" comes from GRE vocabulary pairs at ~5–10 seconds per trial. An MCAT item takes 60–120 seconds. Five intervening MCAT items is 5–10 minutes of elapsed time and a great deal more interference. Whether the optimum in *items* or in *minutes* is what transfers is unknown.
3. **Almost nothing on multi-step reasoning items.** Ryan et al. (2020) is the only study here using items that require applying basic science to a clinical scenario, and it is N = 64 with a one-week horizon and no retry manipulation at all. Every other primary source uses word pairs, trivia facts, or 280–400-word passages with factual questions, and retention intervals of 10 minutes to one week. The MCAT horizon is 3–12 months.
4. **Per-choice rationales specifically are barely studied.** Ryan et al.'s "response-oriented" condition is the only direct test, it did not separate the chosen-distractor rationale from the whole-option-set rationale, and it did not beat generic conceptual feedback.
5. **Motivation and abandonment.** Every effect size here is a retention outcome from a paid or credit-bearing lab session. None of them measure whether a learner quits. Design B's plausible upside is engagement (curiosity gap), and Narciss's line of work reports motivational benefits from withholding the solution. That upside is entirely unmeasured in retention-outcome studies and is a real reason someone might still want to test B.
6. **Same-item versus different-item is a null from N = 24.** Butler (2010) cannot exclude a meaningful effect in either direction.
7. **Confidence capture.** The hypercorrection literature says a confidence rating at answer time would identify the highest-yield feedback moments, but I found no study testing whether *adapting feedback verbosity to confidence* improves outcomes.

**What I would have to test myself**, in rough priority order:

- **A/B the reveal policy** at the session level: immediate full elaborated reveal (recommended design) versus B (refutation-only, retry at lag ≥5, full reveal after second failure). Primary outcome: accuracy on *held-out items testing the same concepts* 7 and 28 days later. Secondary: session completion rate and items-per-session. This is the one experiment that closes the actual gap, and the effect being tested is large enough in the analogue literature (.08 vs. .73 in Finn & Metcalfe) that even a modest sample should resolve it.
- **Retry lag sweep** (0, 5, 10 intervening items, plus next-session-only), outcome measured on new items on the same concept.
- **Same item versus new item at retry**, with the criterion test always being a third, unseen item — this is the only design that avoids the measurement-validity problem in §7.
- **Distractor-endorsement tracking in production.** Log which distractor was chosen and whether that same distractor (or the same reasoning error, if items are tagged by misconception) is chosen again on later items. This gives a direct, cheap measure of the negative suggestion effect in your own item bank, which no published study can give you.
- **Explanation-length trial.** Ryan et al. found conceptual feedback (52 s) no better than response-oriented (25 s). Worth testing whether your long rationales can be cut in half without loss.

## DOK 1 — established facts

1. Feedback improves learning. Overall meta-analytic estimates: d = 0.48 (Wisniewski et al. 2020, 435 studies, k = 994, N > 61,000); d = 0.38 with ~⅓ of effects negative (Kluger & DeNisi 1996, 131 studies, N > 12,000); d = 0.26 in test-like events (Bangert-Drowns et al. 1991, 58 effect sizes / 40 reports, N = 4,666); g = 0.35 for learning from text (Swart et al. 2019, 104 contrasts, N = 6,124).
2. Feedback type ranks consistently: elaborated > correct-answer-only > right/wrong-only. Van der Kleij et al. (2015): 0.49 / 0.32 / 0.05. Mertens et al. (2022) lower-order: 0.714 / 0.489 / 0.237; higher-order: 0.462 / 0.224 ns / 0.136 ns. Wisniewski et al. (2020): 0.99 / 0.46 / 0.24.
3. The elaborated-over-correct-answer advantage is roughly twice as large for higher-order outcomes as for lower-order ones.
4. Right/wrong-only feedback is statistically indistinguishable from no feedback for correcting errors (Pashler et al. 2005, N = 258).
5. Supplying the correct answer after an error increased one-week retention by 494% relative to no feedback (Pashler et al. 2005).
6. Without feedback, learners spontaneously correct 8–11% of their errors (Butler & Roediger 2008: .08; Butler et al. 2007: .11; Finn & Metcalfe 2010 minimal condition: .04–.09).
7. Withholding the correct answer while permitting a retry produced .08–.11 final retention versus .51–.77 when the answer was supplied (Finn & Metcalfe 2010, three experiments).
8. Scaffolded feedback — incremental hints until the learner self-generates the answer, with the answer guaranteed by the end — matched standard feedback on immediate tests and beat it at delay (.66 vs. .53 at 30 min; .67 vs. .51 at ~1 day) (Finn & Metcalfe 2010).
9. Answer-until-correct is not better than simply showing the answer when timing is not confounded: .71 vs. .71 and .64 vs. .65 (Butler, Karpicke & Roediger 2007, N = 48 and N = 40); no single-try/multiple-try difference across 30 studies (Clariana 1993).
10. Delayed feedback beats immediate feedback in laboratory multiple-choice studies by d = 0.28–0.56, with the advantage growing with retention interval (Butler et al. 2007; Butler & Roediger 2008). "Delayed" in these studies means end-of-test-block, i.e. minutes.
11. That advantage disappears for college students once lag-to-test is controlled (Metcalfe, Kornell & Finn 2009).
12. Applied classroom studies favour immediate feedback; laboratory acquisition-of-test-content studies favour delayed (Kulik & Kulik 1988, 53 studies). Van der Kleij et al. (2015) found delayed timing negatively predicted effect sizes in computer-based environments.
13. Correct-answer-only feedback produced no transfer benefit at all: .30 on new inference questions versus .28 for no feedback (t < 1), while explanation feedback produced .45 (Butler, Godbole & Marsh 2013, N = 60).
14. On clinical application MCQs, per-option explanations scored 56.3% and conceptual feedback 59.4% versus 51.3% for right/wrong-plus-correct-option (d = 0.2 and 0.4); median feedback reading times were 25 s, 52 s, and 3 s (Ryan et al. 2020, N = 64).
15. Error generation followed by corrective feedback beats error-free study (Kornell, Hays & Bjork 2009, six experiments; Metcalfe 2017 review). Feedback supplying the correct answer was present in all of these studies.
16. The error-generation benefit requires the error to be semantically related to the target; unrelated errors and blind guesses produce no benefit (Huelser & Metcalfe 2012; Grimaldi & Karpicke 2012; Kang et al. 2011).
17. Hypercorrection: errors held with high confidence are corrected more readily than low-confidence errors, replicated across many studies with ERP (P3a) and fMRI (anterior cingulate) correlates (Butterfield & Metcalfe 2001, 2006; Butterfield & Mangels 2003; Metcalfe et al. 2012, 2015).
18. Spacing: retrying at a lag of 5 intervening items beat a lag of 1 (36% vs. 24% at two days, d = 0.48, with feedback); massed retry (lag 0) yielded .19; equal intervals beat expanding intervals at two days (.60 vs. .49); the causal factor is delaying the *first* retrieval, not the schedule shape (Karpicke & Roediger 2007, three experiments).
19. The optimal inter-study gap is ~20–40% of the retention interval at one week and ~5–10% at one year; in absolute terms, 11 days for a 35-day interval and 21 days for a 70-day interval (Cepeda et al. 2008, N > 1,350).
20. Repeated testing with the *same* question and with *rephrased* questions produced statistically indistinguishable retention and transfer; both hugely outperformed restudying (d ≈ 0.93–1.03) (Butler 2010, N = 24 per experiment).
21. Negative suggestion effect: reading and endorsing multiple-choice lures increases later production of those lures as answers, and reduces the positive testing effect (Roediger & Marsh 2005).
22. Feedback returns lure intrusions to the never-tested baseline: .24 (no feedback) vs. .15 / .14 (immediate / delayed feedback) vs. .16 (no test) (Butler & Roediger 2008).
23. Feedback does not reduce confidence in the errors it fails to correct — confidence in intrusions was ~39 in all tested conditions regardless of feedback, versus 26 in the no-test condition (Butler & Roediger 2008).
24. Unfeedback multiple-choice testing on unstudied material was net harmful: .32 intrusions versus .18 correct, worse than not testing (Butler & Roediger 2008).
25. The negative testing effect is reduced but not eliminated after one week (Fazio, Agarwal, Marsh & Roediger 2010).
26. Feedback effects are smaller for multiple-choice items than for constructed-response items across every feedback type (Mertens et al. 2022: EF 0.55 vs. 1.11; KCR 0.42 vs. 0.64).
27. Prior knowledge moderates feedback type: low-prior-knowledge learners benefit most from AUC (g = 0.77); high-prior-knowledge learners benefit most from elaborated feedback (g = 0.74, with AUC falling to 0.48) (Mertens et al. 2022).

## DOK 2 — insights and implications

1. **The literature that celebrates errors and the literature that demands feedback are the same literature.** Every errorful-generation study supplies the correct answer. Reading Kornell/Bjork/Metcalfe as license to withhold answers inverts their finding. The correct reading is: serve hard items, expect errors, and then always correct them.
2. **The "delayed feedback effect" is mostly two other effects wearing a trenchcoat** — a spacing effect on the correct answer (which disappears when lag-to-test is controlled, per Metcalfe et al. 2009) and an attention effect (lab studies force feedback processing; classrooms and apps do not). You can capture the spacing benefit through the retry schedule without paying the attention cost. Design B pays the attention cost to buy a benefit you can get for free.
3. **Design B is two decisions bundled together, and they have opposite evidence.** "Delay the retry" is supported (Karpicke & Roediger: lag 5 > lag 1 > lag 0). "Withhold the answer" is contradicted (Finn & Metcalfe; Pashler; Butler & Roediger). Unbundle them: reveal immediately, retry later.
4. **The conditional-reveal rule in B has a specific arithmetic flaw.** With four options and one refuted, a blind retry succeeds ~1/3 of the time. Those learners never see the explanation, because B reveals fully only after a second failure. So B systematically denies elaborated feedback to a large slice of errors, and does so preferentially for the learners who guessed rather than reasoned. Butler et al.'s AUC ordinal data (.80 when the correct option is found first, .37 when found last) suggest late or lucky arrival at the answer retains poorly.
5. **Because the MCAT is entirely novel items, transfer is the only outcome that counts, and on transfer, correct-answer-only feedback is worth zero** (Butler et al. 2013: .30 vs. .28, t < 1). This turns "should we show explanations?" from an optimisation question into an existential one. A product that shows only the correct answer is, on the best available evidence, providing no benefit toward the actual criterion.
6. **Per-choice rationales are the highest-leverage asset already in the item bank, and the reason is hypercorrection.** Van Loon et al. found misconception correction occurred with refutation texts that explicitly named and rebutted the misconception, and not with texts that merely stated the correct information. A rationale written against the specific distractor the learner chose is a refutation text delivered at the exact moment of maximum surprise. Ryan et al.'s response-oriented condition — which is structurally the same thing — beat correct-option-only by d = 0.2 and, after errors on far-transfer items, by more.
7. **Show the distractor rationale first, then the correct answer, in one uninterrupted reveal.** This preserves the refutation-then-correction sequence that hypercorrection and refutation-text findings favour, while never leaving the learner without the answer. It gets B's supposed mechanism at none of B's cost.
8. **Confidence is the cheapest high-value signal you are not collecting.** Hypercorrection means high-confidence errors are the most correctable moments in the whole session; Butler & Roediger showed feedback dramatically improves calibration (delayed-feedback learners were near-perfectly calibrated; no-feedback learners were badly overconfident). A one-tap confidence rating before submission costs almost nothing and identifies which errors deserve the longest explanation and the earliest re-test.
9. **The strongest argument against answer-until-correct is not that it fails to help but that it manufactures the exact hazard §8 documents.** Each additional distractor selection is an additional endorsement of misinformation, and endorsement is what drives intrusions. With a measured null benefit (Butler et al. 2007, twice) and a mechanistic cost, there is no case for it.
10. **Re-presenting the same item will produce impressive-looking numbers that mean nothing.** Butler et al. (2013) got .62 on repeated questions and .30 on new questions from the identical intervention. Any mastery signal computed from same-item retries will be badly inflated. If you retry the same item for scheduling reasons, do not let its result feed a mastery estimate.
11. **Within-session retry is a fluency check, not consolidation.** Cepeda's ratio work implies that for a months-long retention interval the optimal gap is days to weeks. A same-session retry at lag 5 beats lag 0 and is worth doing, but the durable learning has to come from the cross-session schedule. Design the within-session retry as a cheap error-detection loop and put the real spacing in the review queue.
12. **Practising unstudied content without feedback is actively harmful, not merely useless** (Butler & Roediger's .32 intrusions vs. .18 correct). Since MCAT learners commonly practise ahead of their content review, the feedback requirement is not just about maximising gain — it is what prevents the product from installing misconceptions.
13. **Feedback effects are systematically smaller with multiple-choice than with constructed-response items** (Mertens et al. 2022, every cell). Any feature that pushes learners to commit to an answer before seeing the options — a "predict the answer" step, or free-recall before reveal — should be expected to increase the value of the feedback that follows. This is the one place where Finn & Metcalfe's scaffolding result might genuinely transfer, and it is worth testing.
14. **The population is the unfavourable cell for multiple-try designs.** Mertens et al. found AUC wins for low-prior-knowledge learners and elaborated feedback wins for high-prior-knowledge undergraduates on multiple-choice items — which is exactly the MCAT population. If multiple-try is ever adopted, adopt it adaptively for weak-topic-weak-learner cells, not globally.
15. **Explanation length has a knee.** Ryan et al. found 52-second conceptual feedback no better than 25-second response-oriented feedback. Longer is not monotonically better, and the marginal second of reading is a second not spent on another item.

## What I could not verify

- **Van der Kleij, Feskens & Eggen (2015)** — abstract only; the article is paywalled. I have the headline effect sizes (EF 0.49, KCR 0.32, KR 0.05) and the direction of the timing moderator from the abstract, but not the confidence intervals, the number of studies contributing to each feedback-type estimate, or the exact delayed-timing coefficient.
- **Kulik & Kulik (1988)** — abstract only. I could not verify effect sizes for the immediate-versus-delayed contrast within each of their three study categories. The 0.49 figure circulating in the Visible Learning database is attributed to this meta-analysis but appears to be a general feedback effect, not the timing contrast; I could not confirm what it measures and have not relied on it.
- **Bangert-Drowns et al. (1991)** — abstract plus secondary summaries only. The d = 0.26 mean and the presearch-availability finding (feedback groups underperforming no-feedback controls when learners could see answers before responding) are well-attested in secondary sources but I did not read the original tables.
- **Narciss & Huth (2006)** — abstract only. This is the single most important gap, because it is the only study I found that withholds the correct answer and still wins. I have the direction ("significantly more beneficial for achievement and motivation than KR-KCR feedback") and the sample (N = 50 fourth-graders with subtraction difficulties) but **no effect sizes**. My discounting of it rests on the domain and population mismatch, not on having examined its statistics.
- **Clariana & Koul (2005)** — not obtained; *International Journal of Instructional Media* has no accessible full text. The "ES = 0.11 for MTF under higher-order outcomes" figure comes from a third-party citation (Educ. Sci. 2023) and I could not confirm it. The qualitative claim (AUC worse for verbatim, better for higher-order) is corroborated by Narciss's 2008 chapter summary.
- **Clariana (1993)** review of 30 studies — read only via Narciss's (2008) description, not the original.
- **Roediger & Marsh (2005)** and **Fazio et al. (2010)** — abstracts plus partial text. I have the qualitative findings and the design but not full effect-size tables. The specific intrusion proportions I quote come from Butler & Roediger (2008), which I did read in full.
- **Kornell, Hays & Bjork (2009)** — abstract and General Discussion only, not the full results sections. The claim I rely on (feedback always supplied) is a direct quotation from their General Discussion.
- **Van Loon et al. (2015)**, **Grimaldi & Karpicke (2012)**, **Metcalfe & Finn (2011)**, **Huelser & Metcalfe (2012)**, **Kang et al. (2011)**, **Butterfield & Mangels (2003)** — all read only through Metcalfe's (2017) *Annual Review* summaries. Effect sizes not obtained. Tiered accordingly.
- **Whether any study manipulates distractor-refutation-without-correct-answer.** I searched for this specifically and found nothing. I cannot prove it does not exist, only that a targeted search did not surface it.
- **Anything on abandonment, frustration, or motivation as an outcome of answer-withholding in a self-directed exam-prep product.** Narciss's line of work reports motivational benefits of informative tutoring feedback, but in supervised settings with children. There is no evidence I found on adult self-paced learners' tolerance for withheld answers.

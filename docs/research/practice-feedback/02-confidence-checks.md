# Confidence ratings on practice questions: evidence review and design recommendation

Scope: should the practice engine ask learners to rate confidence on each 4-option MCQ, and use it to (a) target high-confidence errors for stronger correction and (b) reinforce low-confidence correct answers as fragile knowledge?

Method note: web literature search, July 2026. Every load-bearing claim is tiered **A** (strong direct evidence / meta-analytic consensus), **B** (reasonable with limits), **C** (weak, indirect, or single-study). Where I read only an abstract, publisher landing page, or a results excerpt rather than the full paper, the Sources section says so explicitly. No numbers or citations are invented; a handful of arithmetic derivations from published rates are labelled as mine.

---

## Recommendation

**Don't ship a per-item confidence rating. Confidence: medium-high.**

The two hypothesised payoffs are real phenomena but neither requires eliciting a rating, and the one experiment that tested the exact proposed mechanism — vary feedback richness by confidence × correctness — found no learning benefit and worse overall time-efficiency than uniform feedback (Mory, 1994, replicated in a web version by Whitehurst; Tier B).

The decisive argument is not that the effects are weak. It is that **the causal ingredient in both payoffs is the feedback, not the measurement.** In Butler, Karpicke & Roediger (2008) the intervention that doubled retention of low-confidence correct answers (.40 → .85) was "give feedback on every item including the correct ones." Confidence was a *measured moderator*, not a treated variable; every item in the feedback condition got identical feedback regardless of its rating. This application already has pre-written explanation prose for all four choices on every item, so **the low-confidence-correct payoff is already captured** — measurement would only let you re-describe it in analytics. Similarly, hypercorrection occurs whenever corrective feedback is delivered; Sitzman, Rhodes & Tauber (2013) showed prior domain knowledge predicts error correction and that "confidence alone played little unique role after controlling for prior knowledge" (Tier B). This system already maintains per-concept mastery estimates — a prior-knowledge signal that the literature says is the *better* predictor, available at zero learner cost.

Against a benefit of approximately zero, the costs are: ~2–3 s × every item (≈3–8% of session time, my arithmetic from a 3,000 ms control-screen anchor in Double & Birney, 2018); a measured 3–5× drop in voluntary uptake when medical students were offered the same practice questions with certainty-based marking instead of plain MCQ (Schoendorfer & Emmett, 2012, N=400; Tier C but the only behavioural friction estimate in the population of interest); and a reactivity distribution centred near zero for educationally realistic material with a **negative tail specifically for low-self-confidence learners** (Double & Birney, 2017a/2018; interaction β ≈ .30–.36), which is a meaningful subgroup in a high-anxiety admissions-test population.

**Conditional exception, stated crisply:** ship confidence ratings only if the goal is *measurement or metacognitive feedback rather than retention* — i.e. you decide to give learners explicit over/under-confidence feedback, or you need the psychometric reliability gain for a diagnostic instrument. Certainty-based marking raised exam reliability by an amount "equivalent to using over 50% more questions" (Gardner-Medwin, 2019; Tier B, single institution, T/F items), and feedback on self-assessment accuracy improves that accuracy (g = 0.34, 47 studies, N=5,487; Tier B). In that case confine it to a bounded diagnostic (one session, opt-in), not the everyday practice loop. Do **not** ship it as a retention intervention or as an input to scheduling.

## The two hypothesised payoffs, judged

### Payoff 1 — High-confidence errors as high-priority correction events: **fails on the "requires measurement" test.**

The phenomenon survives scrutiny. Hypercorrection replicates across labs, ages, materials, and a one-week delay (Tier A). Within-subject gamma correlations between confidence-in-error and later correction run **+.13 to +.28** (Butterfield & Metcalfe, 2006: γ = .13 and .16; Butler, Fazio & Marsh, 2011: γ = .28 at 6 min, d = 0.86; γ = .19 at 1 week, d = 0.79; Metcalfe & Finn, 2011 in children: γ = .22, d = 0.46). Butler et al. (2008, Exp 1) found .93 of high-confidence errors corrected vs .80 for all other confidence levels, d = 0.59.

But four things break the case for eliciting it:

1. **The effect is not caused by the rating.** No study manipulates confidence; all correlate a measured rating with later correction. Removing the rating does not remove the underlying process — surprise at a contradicted expectation, and the semantic proximity of a confident error to the truth. Butterfield & Metcalfe (2006) demonstrated the mechanism as *attentional capture by the feedback* (impaired concurrent tone detection during high-confidence-error feedback), which happens whether or not the learner typed a number first. **Measuring confidence buys targeting, not the effect.** (Tier A for the distinction; it follows from the designs themselves.)
2. **Confidence is a proxy for something you already have.** Sitzman, Rhodes & Tauber (2013, *Memory & Cognition*) found prior knowledge more predictive of error correction than subjective confidence, with confidence contributing little unique variance once prior knowledge was controlled (Tier B, abstract + discussion excerpt). Per-concept mastery is a prior-knowledge estimate this system already computes.
3. **The multiple-choice evidence is materially weaker than the short-answer evidence.** Stock, Kulhavy, Pridemore & Krug (1992) found **no relation** between confidence and probability of correcting an error when feedback was right/wrong only; the positive relation appeared only when feedback marked the correct alternative (Tier B, abstract). Butler et al. (2008, Exp 2), using 4-option MCQs and a 2-day delay, found **no hypercorrection at all** and a non-significant numerical trend toward *hypo*correction (.62 of high-confidence errors corrected vs .73 for other levels, p = .10). Nearly all clean hypercorrection demonstrations use forced-report short-answer generation, not 4-option selection.
4. **It decays, and the confident error comes back.** Over a week, overall error correction fell from .86 to .56, and the higher the original confidence, the *more* likely the original error was reproduced (γ = .15, d = 0.51; Butler, Fazio & Marsh, 2011; Tier B, single study N=50 but 6,000 observations). Metcalfe (2017) is explicit that one feedback exposure is not enough.

**Verdict: do not build targeting on elicited confidence.** The actionable content of this literature is about *feedback design and follow-up*, both of which are confidence-agnostic: use refutation-style prose that names and rejects the misconception rather than only asserting the truth (Van Loon et al., 2015: hypercorrection appeared only with refutation texts, not standard factual texts; N=114; Tier B, abstract), and re-test missed items shortly after feedback (Fazio, 2011, reported in Butler et al., 2011: an immediate retest after feedback left "hardly any forgetting" at one week; Tier C, secondary report).

### Payoff 2 — Low-confidence correct answers as fragile knowledge: **the phenomenon is real and large, and you already get it for free.**

This is the better-evidenced of the two payoffs, with the cleanest numbers in the whole review — and the least need for measurement.

Butler, Karpicke & Roediger (2008), *JEP:LMC*, two experiments, N=30 each, 4-option MCQ, 4-point confidence scale (1 = guess … 4 = high), final cued-recall test:

| Initial MC outcome | No feedback | With feedback |
|---|---|---|
| Incorrect (all confidence) | .03 corrected | .82 corrected (d = 6.08) |
| Correct, "guess" (lowest confidence) | **.40 retained** | **.85 retained** |
| Correct, high confidence | .79 → near ceiling | .93 (ceiling) |
| Correct, overall | .79 | .93 (d = 0.76) |

Experiment 2 (2-day delay, forced-report final test) replicated: correct responses .78 → .93 with feedback, d = 0.99; significant learning-condition × confidence interaction, η²p = .18. Low-confidence correct answers were 38–40% of all correct responses under forced responding. **Tier A** (full text read; two experiments, replicated pattern, large effects).

So a correct-but-unsure answer really is fragile: without feedback, 60% of "guess"-level correct answers were lost within minutes. But note what the manipulation was — **feedback vs no feedback, applied to all items alike.** The confidence rating identified *which* items benefited; it did not produce the benefit. The paper's own conclusion is "educators should try to give comprehensive feedback (i.e. on both correct and incorrect answers) whenever possible." A system that already renders explanation prose for all four choices after every item has implemented the intervention.

Are they "retrieval successes that confidence underestimates" rather than luck? Largely yes, and this matters because it undercuts the "fragile knowledge" framing's implication of near-emptiness. In 9,106 paired MC-plus-confidence responses from 8 undergraduate science midterms (5-option items), the **least**-confident responses were correct 34–52% of the time against a 20% chance baseline, and mid-confidence responses were correct 52–70% against a 32% "randomly eliminate some options" baseline (Abu-Ghazalah, Dubins & Poon, 2023, *Applied Measurement in Education*; Tier B, substantial full text). The authors' words: "a self-assessed lack of confidence reflected a more considered approach than random guessing." Partial knowledge, not blindness.

The **implicit/unconscious knowledge framing does not hold up well** and shouldn't be leaned on. Dienes' guessing criterion (above-baseline accuracy on trials the participant calls a guess) is the standard test, but accurate guessing is strategically fragile: Voss & Paller (2010) showed that encouraging guessing produced highly accurate guesses while encouraging confident responding drove guess accuracy to chance, in otherwise near-identical procedures — and Jeneson et al. failed to replicate accurate guessing entirely (Tier B, full text of the reply article). Ziori & Dienes (2012) even report below-baseline guessing accuracy in some blocks. Above-chance low-confidence accuracy in a 4-option MCQ is much more parsimoniously explained by distractor elimination and partial knowledge than by unconscious knowledge, and nothing in the design decision depends on which it is.

**Verdict: the payoff is real and already banked by universal explanation feedback.** Measuring confidence would let you *escalate* low-confidence correct items in scheduling — but see Finding 6: there is no evidence that graded self-report beats binary correctness as a scheduling input, and Butler et al. (2008) found feedback *reduced* the correlation between initial confidence and final performance (γ .70 → .40 in Exp 1, d = 0.75; .57 → .38 in Exp 2), i.e. once you give good feedback, the confidence rating loses much of its predictive value for what you'd schedule.

## Findings

### 1. The hypercorrection effect: size, robustness, mechanism, and whether it needs measurement

**Size.** Reported as within-subject Goodman–Kruskal gamma between confidence-in-error and later correction: **γ = .13–.28**, with between-subject Cohen's d against zero of **0.46–0.86**. (Butterfield & Metcalfe, 2006: γ = .13, t(35) = 2.5 and γ = .16, t(38) = 4.7. Butler, Fazio & Marsh, 2011: γ = .28, d = 0.86 at 6 min; γ = .19, d = 0.79 at 1 week; N = 50, 6,000 responses. Metcalfe & Finn, 2011, children: γ = .22, d = 0.46, N = 40.) In proportion terms, Butler et al. (2008, Exp 1) got .93 vs .80 correction, d = 0.59. **Tier A** for existence and approximate magnitude; these are small-to-moderate ordinal associations, not large effects.

**Robustness.** Replicated by at least five independent groups (Butterfield & Metcalfe; Butler/Fazio/Marsh; Kulhavy, Yekovich & Dyer 1976; Metcalfe & Finn; Eich et al.), in children, young adults, and (attenuated) older adults, and extended to kanji pronunciation, false episodic memories, and science misconceptions (Metcalfe, 2017 review; Tier A). Boundary conditions that matter here: attenuated or absent in **older adults** (Eich et al., 2013: high-confidence errors corrected only slightly more than low; Tier B); absent with **verification-only feedback** in MCQ (Stock et al., 1992; Tier B); absent at **2 days in a 4-option MCQ paradigm** (Butler et al., 2008 Exp 2; Tier B); absent for **science misconceptions read as standard expository text**, present only with refutation text (Van Loon et al., 2015; Tier B).

**Mechanism — three accounts, two of which are supported:**
- *Surprise / attentional capture.* Butterfield & Metcalfe (2006): concurrent tone detection was selectively impaired during feedback to high-confidence errors, and tone-detection failure predicted better final performance. Fazio & Marsh (2009): source memory for the *appearance* of feedback (font colour, presenter voice) was better when feedback was expectation-incongruent — for both high-confidence errors and correct guesses. Butterfield & Mangels (2003): larger frontal P3 to high-confidence-error feedback. **Tier A.**
- *Prior knowledge / "knew it all along."* Metcalfe & Finn (2011): after high-confidence errors participants were more likely to produce the correct answer on a second guess, to pick it from an alternatives set excluding their original answer, and to need fewer letter cues — i.e. they partly *did* know it. Latent semantic analysis shows high-confidence errors are semantically closer to the target. Sitzman, Rhodes & Tauber (2013): prior knowledge dominates, confidence adds little unique variance. **Tier A** for the phenomenon; **Tier B** for the strong claim that prior knowledge fully subsumes confidence (one paper's regression, plus a follow-up in older adults).
- *Mediation / interference* (the original prediction that confident errors should be *harder* to fix): rejected at short delays, partially vindicated at long ones (confident errors return; Butler et al., 2011).

**Does it require eliciting a rating? No — and this is the pivot of the whole decision.** Every hypercorrection study uses confidence as a measured covariate. The surprise mechanism is triggered by the *mismatch between an internally held expectation and the feedback*, which exists whether or not the learner externalises the expectation on a keypad. There is no experiment showing that hypercorrection is larger when confidence is elicited than when it is not; there is no experiment showing that a system which *knows* the confidence and acts on it beats a system that gives everyone equally good feedback — indeed the one experiment that tried it found no difference (Finding 2). Precisely stated: **eliciting confidence converts a naturally occurring, feedback-driven memory-updating advantage into an observable label. The advantage is not contingent on the label.**

The corollary is the constructive one: if you want the label, cheaper proxies exist. Prior-mastery-high-but-wrong is the better-evidenced predictor (Sitzman et al., 2013). And Weidemann & Kahana (2016, *R. Soc. Open Sci.*) report that in recognition memory "much of the information from confidence ratings can be obtained from response times" (Tier B, abstract only) — a fast wrong answer on a high-mastery concept is a decent free proxy for a confident error.

### 2. Response certitude × feedback processing (Kulhavy & Stock and successors)

The older instructional-feedback tradition modelled feedback processing as *discrepancy reduction*: learners invest effort in feedback in proportion to the gap between their expectation and the outcome.

- **Feedback study time rises with the confidence–correctness discrepancy.** Kulhavy, Yekovich & Dyer (1979): after a *correct* response, learners studied feedback significantly longer if that response was low-confidence, and low-confidence-correct study times were roughly equal to low-confidence-incorrect study times (reported in Butler et al., 2008; Tier B, secondary). Hancock, Stock & Kulhavy (1992): "the amount of effort a subject expends on a feedback message is a direct function of the discrepancy between external and internal knowledge states," and with *elaborative* feedback errors were replaced by corrects at a high rate "especially for high-certitude errors" (Tier B, abstract/results excerpt).
- **But the certitude→correction link in MCQ depends on feedback type.** Stock et al. (1992): with right/wrong messages, feedback study intervals were *longer after correct than incorrect* choices, and there was **no** relation between correcting errors and confidence; with feedback marking the correct alternative, the positive relation appeared (Tier B, abstract).
- **The decisive applied test: confidence-adaptive feedback does not beat uniform feedback.** Mory (1994, *Journal of Educational Computing Research*) randomly assigned undergraduates to feedback whose informativeness varied by correctness × certitude, or to invariant feedback. "Effects of adaptive feedback were not significantly different from non-adaptive feedback on student performance." Adaptive feedback was more efficient *per unit of feedback time*, but non-adaptive was significantly more efficient for the **lesson overall**. A later web-based replication (UNCG dissertation, Whitehurst) reached the same conclusion for concept learning, again finding higher feedback study time for high-certitude wrongs and low-certitude corrects. **Tier B** (two studies, 1990s–2000s, moderate N, abstracts only). This is the closest existing test of the proposal on the table, and it is null on learning.

**What this implies for feedback design:** the learner already allocates feedback attention adaptively without being asked — that is exactly what the study-time data show. So the system's job is to make sure the feedback is worth that attention, not to ration it. Meta-analytically, elaborated feedback (explanation) outperforms knowledge-of-correct-response, which outperforms knowledge-of-result: **g = 0.49 vs 0.32 vs 0.05** (Van der Kleij, Feskens & Eggen, 2015, *Review of Educational Research*, 40 studies, 70 effect sizes; **Tier A**), replicated by a network meta-analysis ranking EF first for both recall and transfer (Wisniewski-style network MA in *J. Educ. Psych.* 2022, 163 effect sizes, 77 studies; Tier A, abstract). **Uniform elaborated feedback for every item and every distractor is the highest-value, best-evidenced move, and it costs no learner time to decide.**

### 3. Low-confidence correct answers: retention data by cell

Full numbers are in "Payoff 2" above. The cell-level forgetting rates from Butler et al. (2008, Exp 1), N=30, final cued recall after 5 min, **Tier A**:

| Initial confidence (correct responses) | Retained, no feedback | Retained, with feedback | Forgetting w/o feedback |
|---|---|---|---|
| Guess | .40 | .85 | **60%** |
| Low → Medium | rising monotonically | ~.85–.90 | intermediate |
| High | .79 | .93 (ceiling) | 21% |

Interaction: F(3,87) = 17.9, η²p = .38 (Exp 1); F(3,87) = 6.3, η²p = .18 (Exp 2, 2-day delay). Item distribution (Exp 1): 38% of correct responses were rated guess/low; 41–44% of *errors* were rated "guess" and only 6–10% "high."

Interpretation: **genuinely fragile, and genuinely knowledge-bearing.** Both. Above-chance accuracy at the lowest confidence level (34–52% vs 20% chance in the Abu-Ghazalah et al. data; Tier B) says partial knowledge is present; 60% loss within minutes absent feedback says it is not consolidated. The "unconscious knowledge" gloss is not needed and is empirically shaky (see Payoff 2). Also note the metacognitive benefit: in Butler et al. Exp 2, feedback moved final-test resolution from γ = .67 (no feedback) to γ = .94 and calibration from over-confident (conf .56 vs acc .47) to essentially perfect (.83 vs .82), d ≈ 0.92 — **feedback fixes calibration without any confidence-based targeting** (Tier B, single experiment).

### 4. Reactivity — does asking change learning? This is the central cost question, and the answer is "≈0 on average, negative for a subgroup you care about."

**Judgments of learning, meta-analytically.** Ingendahl et al. (2025), *Psychological Bulletin*: 344 effect sizes, 175 experiments, 49 records, **N = 15,079**. Overall **g = 0.22 [0.17, 0.27]**. Strongly moderated by material: **related word pairs g = 0.44; unrelated pairs g = −0.09**. The authors report "indications of publication bias favoring positive reactivity." **Tier A** (abstract only, but it is the definitive synthesis). The earlier meta-analysis found no overall effect: g = 0.054 [−0.027, 0.135], with related pairs g = 0.323 and word lists g = 0.384 (Double, Birney & Walker, 2018, *Memory*, 19 experiments/8 studies; Tier A, abstract). Myers, Rhodes & Hausman (2020) meta-analysed by test type: related items d = 0.25 [.14, .35], driven by cued recall (d = 0.518, k = 3, N = 148) and recognition (d = 0.228); free recall d = −0.036; unrelated items overall d = 0.10 [−.002, .21] with free recall d = −0.232 (Tier B, table excerpts).

**Negative reactivity is as real as positive.** Ingendahl & Undorf (*Collabra*, 17 experiments): "negative JOL reactivity for unrelated pairs is similarly large and robust as positive reactivity for related pairs" (Tier B, abstract). Mechanisms proposed: cue-strengthening plus dual-task costs; the changed-goal account (Mitchum et al., 2016 — making JOLs led learners to spend *more* time on easy items and less on hard ones, impairing hard-item recall); item-order/relational-memory dissociations. Study-list structure flips the sign: positive reactivity for mixed lists (+0.08), negative for pure lists (−0.08), interaction z = 5.83, p < .001 (Kelly-style single-paper meta-analysis, *Memory & Cognition* 2024; Tier B).

**The educationally relevant subset — which is what matters here — is null or negative.** Ingendahl & Undorf (2024, *JARMAC*): three experiments with **general knowledge facts** tested by **multiple-choice and short-answer**, plus a cumulative meta-analysis: "Neither experiment revealed reliable effects of making JOLs on performance… soliciting metamemory predictions does not affect test performance for general knowledge facts." **Tier B, abstract only, but this is the closest published analogue to our item type.** Their 2026 follow-up in *Metacognition and Learning* (key-term definitions, country outlines, animal species): "making immediate JOLs did not improve test performance. On the contrary… making JOLs reduced test performance when difficulty was high" (Tier B, abstract).

**Post-retrieval judgments specifically — our case — have no effect beyond retrieval practice.** Our proposed prompt comes *after* the learner has answered, so it is a retrospective confidence judgment on a just-attempted item, not a study-phase JOL. Four replication attempts of Dougherty, Scheck, Nelson & Narens (2005) concluded: "making a metacognitive judgment, regardless of the type, has no impact on later memory performance above and beyond retrieval practice" (*Memory & Cognition*, 2018; **Tier B**, abstract + excerpt, but a multi-experiment replication package and directly on point).

**Confidence ratings specifically are reactive in a person-dependent way, and the direction is bad for anxious learners.** Double & Birney (2017a, *Thinking & Reasoning*): confidence ratings on Raven's matrices improved performance overall in Exp 1, but Exp 2 showed the benefit held only for participants high in reasoning self-confidence and was **detrimental for those low in self-confidence** (Tier B, abstract). Birney, Beckmann, Beckmann & Double (2017), business managers: confidence ratings **impaired** Raven's performance, worst on difficult items (Tier C, secondary report). Double & Birney (2019, *Frontiers in Psychology*, priming study): no main effect of confidence ratings; significant self-confidence × condition interactions, **β = .35, t = 2.00, p = .048** (Exp 1) and **β = .30, t = 2.22, p = .029** (Exp 2), with Exp 2's effect "largely driven by impaired performance of low PCA participants in the CR group." They attribute it to *priming the word "confident"* rather than to metacognitive reflection — a task-irrelevant rating containing the word produced the same pattern (Tier B, substantial full text). Double & Birney (2018, *Metacognition and Learning*, N = 89, Latin Square Task): the confidence-rating group was **significantly more miscalibrated** than controls, and members of that group anchored their retrospective self-appraisals on their *pre-task* self-confidence rather than on their actual performance (group × prospective-confidence β = 61.02, t = 3.27, p = .002); high-confidence participants slowed down per item and improved item-level accuracy but not overall accuracy — a short-term-performance orientation at the expense of coverage (**Tier B**, substantial full text; older community sample, so generalisation to 22-year-olds is a real limit).

**Net:** expected direct retention effect of adding a confidence prompt to MCQ practice on factual/conceptual science content ≈ **0**, with a plausible small negative for low-self-confidence learners and a documented risk of *worse* self-monitoring and a shift toward per-item performance orientation. That is the opposite of what the feature is supposed to deliver.

### 5. Is self-reported confidence reliable enough to act on?

Aggregate resolution is respectable; individual-level noise and bias are the problem.

- **Resolution.** In 4-option MCQ general-knowledge testing, within-subject gamma between confidence and correctness was **.55–.59** (Butler et al., 2008, both experiments; Tier A for these samples). Three ordinal levels cleanly separate performance tiers: most-confident **79–88%** correct, partially confident **52–70%**, least confident **34–52%**, non-overlapping 95% credible intervals (Abu-Ghazalah et al., 9,106 responses; Tier B). A large real-world CBM corpus (UCL/Imperial self-tests, >200,000 submitted sessions, ~51 questions each) shows 78% correct overall, **61% at low confidence and 88% at high confidence** (Gardner-Medwin, 2019; Tier B, author's own aggregate).
- **Overconfidence magnitude.** Butler et al. (2008, Exp 2): mean confidence .60 vs accuracy .52 on the initial MCQ, F(1,29) = 28.6 — **~8 percentage points**, modest. The "blunder" rate (confident and wrong) is the operationally important bias: **8.6% of all responses**, i.e. 1 – p₁ = **11–21% of confident responses across eight tests** (Abu-Ghazalah et al.; Tier B). Independently, first-semester medical students predicting an oral anatomy exam: 35.5% overestimated, 46% underestimated, 18.5% accurate, ρ = −0.59 between actual and self-assessed score (BMC Med Educ 2024, N = 426; Tier C — a global self-assessment, not item-level, and the negative correlation is partly regression artifact).
- **Between-person variance is large.** Kruger & Dunning (1999): bottom-quartile participants were "nearly 4 times more miscalibrated" than top-quartile, and were worse at discriminating their own correct from incorrect answers (Study 4). Training improved self-assessment via improved metacognitive skill (Tier A for the pattern, Tier B for the metacognitive interpretation, which is contested on statistical grounds — hard-easy effect, regression to the mean, measurement error).
- **Do novices have worse resolution? Partly artifact.** In MC formats specifically, resolution metrics (gamma, d_a) are **mathematically downward-biased as accuracy falls**, because lucky guesses inject confidence-accuracy noise. Six experiments plus Monte Carlo simulation showed a positive resolution–performance correlation in multiple-choice mathematics but **not** in free-response testing, supporting the guessing-confound explanation (*Metacognition and Learning*, 2020; **Tier B**, abstract + excerpts). Practical consequence: **any resolution-based targeting or "your calibration score" feature will systematically penalise weaker learners for a measurement artifact of the 4-option format.** That is a real fairness bug, not a hypothetical.
- **Incentive contamination.** Abu-Ghazalah et al. were careful that ratings could not affect scores, so there was "no incentive to posture." If confidence ever influences what the app shows or schedules and learners notice, ratings become strategic. CBM handles this with a proper scoring rule (3/−6, 2/−2, 1/0, which makes honest reporting optimal above/below 80%/67% thresholds; Gardner-Medwin, 2019) — but that requires imposing a scoring scheme on formative practice, which is its own product decision.

**Verdict: reliable enough for aggregate analytics and cohort-level diagnostics; too noisy and too format-biased to justify per-item branching, especially given that the branching itself has no demonstrated benefit.**

### 6. Does confidence data improve scheduling, mastery estimation, or item selection?

**No evidence that it beats correctness alone. Tier B for "no evidence," which is not the same as evidence of no effect.**

- **Spaced-repetition systems that already ask.** FSRS ingests a 4-point self-rating (Again/Hard/Good/Easy) as an input feature, but internally treats Again = fail and Hard/Good/Easy = pass, and the training loss is binary. The official FAQ states: "FSRS is a little more accurate for people who mostly use *Again* and *Good* than for people who use all 4 buttons a lot." An analysis over the FSRS Anki 20k dataset found lower RMSE for two-button users across many thresholds (p < .01); a follow-up comparing 3,035 two-button collections against 39 *consistent* four-button collections was **inconclusive** for both RMSE and log loss. **Tier C** (community analyses, not peer-reviewed, confounded by self-selection: people who use two buttons may have more consistent grading habits). The honest summary is: the largest naturalistic dataset in existence on graded self-rating for scheduling **does not show that the extra grades help**, and the practitioner guidance is that *consistency* matters more than *granularity*.
- **Knowledge tracing with confidence features.** The claimed AUC gains are largely circular. One 2026 arXiv preprint reports AUC-ROC 0.878 with confidence vs 0.649 without — while stating that the model "is predominantly predicting student correctness by utilizing the student's own self-reported confidence," and that confidence had to be excluded from the explanatory model "to avoid self-referential collinearity" (Tier C, single-author preprint, not peer-reviewed). Predicting whether *this* answer is right from the learner's confidence in *this* answer is not a scheduling capability; it is a restatement of resolution. An iBKT variant with a P(C) confidence parameter reports RMSE 0.2506 → 0.2119 on ASSISTments and 0.2826 → 0.2590 on KDD (Tier C, single paper, no ablation against equally-informative behavioural features such as latency).
- **The counter-evidence from within the feedback literature.** Butler et al. (2008) found that giving feedback **reduced** the gamma correlation between initial confidence and final performance (.70 → .40, d = 0.75, Exp 1; .57 → .38, Exp 2). If you give good feedback — which you do — the initial confidence rating is a *weaker* predictor of what the learner will retain than it is in a no-feedback condition. Scheduling on it is scheduling on a signal your own feedback has partly invalidated.
- **Where confidence genuinely adds measurement value: reliability, not scheduling.** CBM in UCL first/second-year medical exams (5 years) produced "an enhancement of reliability (equivalent to using over 50% more questions with conventional marking)," because uncertain answers get down-weighted, reducing random variance (Gardner-Medwin, 2019; illustrated on 320 medical students × 300 T/F questions; **Tier B** — single institution, T/F items, author's own analyses, and the practice was subsequently discontinued for standard-setting reasons). A Ljubljana replication (N = 89 fifth-year medical students, Moodle CBM) found high reliability (α = .932) and no learner-type discrimination, but also **no significant difference in achievement rankings distribution** with vs without CBM (Tier C, small, pandemic-era, self-selected).

**Verdict: if the goal is scheduling or mastery estimation, use correctness plus free behavioural signals (latency, answer changes, prior per-concept mastery, item difficulty). Adding a self-rating has no demonstrated incremental value and imports the learner's bias into the model.**

### 7. Calibration training: does rating repeatedly improve calibration, and is calibration worth having?

Split verdict: **practice alone doesn't work; practice plus explicit instruction and calibration feedback does; the transfer to exam performance is thinly evidenced.**

- **Overt calibration practice alone: null.** Bol, Hacker, O'Shea & Allen (2005), N = 356, randomised to make predictions and postdictions across five quizzes vs not: "Results did not support the hypothesis that overt calibration practice on the quizzes would improve calibration accuracy or exam performance." Higher achievers were more accurate but under-confident; lower achievers less accurate and over-confident (Tier B, abstract). Bol & Hacker (2001), N = 59: practice tests were associated with *lower* midterm MC scores and *less* accurate predictions on those items (Tier C, small, quasi-experimental).
- **Repeated self-reporting of confidence in a real course: null on calibration.** The Compass system (SIGCSE/ITiCSE 2021) added per-answer confidence self-reports to self-assessment quizzes: "the repeated practice of self-reporting confidence levels did not seem to benefit students' metacognitive accuracy over time," and the results "challenge the commonly accepted hypothesis that high-performing students have high metacognitive accuracy" (Tier C, single deployment, abstract).
- **Training + feedback: works, on calibration and on exam scores.** A three-group study (N = 208) comparing control, repeated testing, and testing plus continuous metacognitive-judgment training with feedback found training superior on bias (4.85 → 3.61 → 0.06), accuracy (14.00 → 11.02 → 10.14) and final exam performance (20.55 → 24.87 → 25.91), η² = .08/.14/.19 (**Tier C** — I read an AERA repository abstract with a results table; I could not verify authorship or peer-review venue, and the design cannot separate "judgment training" from "extra instructional contact").
- **Feedback on self-assessment accuracy improves that accuracy.** Three-level meta-analysis, 201 effect sizes, 47 studies, N = 5,487: **g = 0.34**, moderated by feedback type (Tier B — I read the OSF preprint abstract, not a published version).
- **Retrieval practice alone improves calibration without any confidence-targeting.** N = 98 secondary students, one-week delay: tested items yielded more accurate and less biased confidence judgments than untested items (*Memory*, 2018; Tier B, abstract). Butler et al. (2008) Exp 2 found feedback moved learners from over-confident to essentially perfectly calibrated (Tier B).
- **And the counter-evidence:** Double & Birney (2018) found eliciting confidence ratings made monitoring *worse* (see Finding 4). So "asking builds calibration" is not safe to assume.

**Is calibration itself valuable for the MCAT?** Plausible but under-evidenced. The MCAT has no guessing penalty, so the classic "when to guess" payoff is absent — you always answer. The genuine payoffs are study allocation and time allocation, and I found no study linking item-level calibration gains to score gains on a standardised admissions test. Treat as **Tier C / unproven** for this specific product claim.

### 8. Cost side: friction, time, acceptance

The best numbers come from certainty-based marking in medical education — the closest available analogue population and format.

- **Time.** Schoendorfer & Emmett (2012), second-year medical students, N = 400 cohort: **67% agreed CBM "took more time"**, because 76% said they needed to consider their certainty level for every question. Self-reported extra time ranged from 30 seconds to 30 minutes per quiz, "with the most common response being around **50% of additional time**" (Tier C — self-report, unblinded, small quizzes). A second cohort (Hendriks et al., cited in the Ljubljana paper) found 32.7% of students *against* using CBM at all.
- **Behavioural friction, the most decision-relevant number in this review.** In the same study, uptake of the voluntary practice resource **fell from 40–46% per week (plain MCQ format, preceding module) to 15%, 11%, 9% and 8% over four weeks (CBM format)** — roughly a 3–5× drop — "even though these did not count toward their final grades." The authors' own framing: "students were less likely to participate in the CBM tasks than standard multiple choice." **Tier C** and heavily confounded (different module, different content, novelty, declining engagement over a term, order effect), and a published Letter to the Editor criticised the percentage reporting. But it is the only behavioural estimate available, it points hard in one direction, and the direction matches the qualitative data ("most ridiculous thing ever," "would be too time-consuming during exams," 30% called it "a waste of time thinking about certainty").
- **Acceptance is bimodal and depends on framing.** Positive: Ljubljana (N = 49 surveyed) — 78% saw CBM as fair, 94% as useful for revision; UCL exam use drew 52%:30% support for retention. School mathematics (Foster, 2016): 106 positive to 28 negative comments among 11–14-year-olds. Negative: only ~60% of Schoendorfer & Emmett's cohort saw benefits; 86% thought CBM best for *revision* rather than assessment. The consistent theme: acceptance is high when learners get **calibration feedback in return** (the Ljubljana students specifically credited the auto-generated over/under-confidence report), and low when the rating feels like a tax. **Tier B** for "acceptance is conditional on visible return."
- **Per-item time estimate.** No study I found directly measures the seconds added by a per-item confidence prompt. The best anchor: Double & Birney (2018) gave their no-rating control group a **3,000 ms blank screen** to equate for the delay introduced by the confidence rating — i.e. the experimenters treated ~3 s as the cost of a 6-point rating. **My arithmetic, clearly labelled as mine:** at 2–3 s per item, a 20-item session pays **40–60 s**. On an MCAT-style discrete item with explanation reading at ~60–120 s/item, that is **3–8% of session time**, or roughly the time of one extra item per session.
- **Metacognitive prompts in general are not free wins.** Meta-analysis of metacognitive prompts in computer-based environments: g = 0.50 for SRL activities and g = 0.40 for learning outcomes (Tier B, abstract) — but these are *strategy* prompts (plan, monitor, evaluate) with feedback and adaptivity, not a bare confidence rating, and the moderators that mattered were feedback, specificity and adaptability. The most rigorous ed-tech prompt test found nothing: four large RCTs of planning prompts in MOOCs produced a tightly estimated null on completion, ruling out effects outside [−7%, +3%], with one course showing +19% (Tier B, abstract + results excerpt). Prompt interventions at scale usually disappoint.

### 9. Beyond the brief

- **Free proxies dominate.** Three signals the system can compute without asking anyone anything: (a) **prior per-concept mastery high + item wrong** — the Sitzman et al. (2013) finding says this is a *better* predictor of error correction than confidence; (b) **response latency** — Weidemann & Kahana (2016) report that much of the information in confidence ratings is recoverable from RT; (c) **answer-change behaviour**. Together these plausibly reconstruct most of the actionable variance in a confidence rating at zero learner cost. This should be built and validated *before* any prompt is considered, because if it works, the prompt is redundant, and if it doesn't, you'll have learned what signal is missing.
- **Refutation structure in the explanation prose is the real lever.** Van Loon et al. (2015): hypercorrection of science misconceptions occurred **only** with refutation texts that explicitly stated and rejected the misconception, not with standard factual texts (N = 114 eighth-graders; Tier B). The system's per-distractor prose is the natural home for this: each distractor's explanation should name the misconception it embodies and refute it, not merely explain why the key is right. This captures the mechanism (surprise at a co-activated contradiction) for *every* learner who picked that distractor, with no rating. Caveat from the same paper: the correction showed up more on true/false than open-ended post-tests, so conceptual accommodation may need more than refutation.
- **Durability requires a second retrieval, not a better label.** High-confidence errors return within a week (Butler et al., 2011). Fazio (2011) — immediate retest after feedback → almost no forgetting at one week (Tier C, secondary report). Design implication: **re-ask missed items later in the same session and again the next day.** This is a scheduling change driven by correctness alone, and it is better evidenced than anything confidence targeting offers.
- **Affective risk is asymmetric in this population.** MCAT preparation is high-stakes and anxiety-laden. The reactivity literature says the harm concentrates in **low-self-confidence learners** (Double & Birney, 2017a/2019), and the priming account says merely repeating the word "confident" is enough to trigger it. Repeatedly asking an anxious pre-med "how sure were you?" 30 times per session and then telling them they were over-confident is a plausible retention-and-churn risk that no learning gain currently offsets.
- **If you ever score confidence, you must use a proper scoring rule.** Naive schemes are gameable and can be actively misleading (Foster's 0–10 scheme rewarded always rating 10 whenever p > .5). Gardner-Medwin's 3/−6, 2/−2, 1/0 is a proper rule that makes honest reporting optimal. Also note learners changed answers when forced to consider certainty (Issroff & Gardner-Medwin, 1998, via Schoendorfer & Emmett) — that is reactivity in the response itself, which contaminates the correctness signal your mastery model depends on.
- **Base rates, which nobody in the primary literature foregrounds.** Metcalfe (2017) states plainly: "People make high-confidence errors only rarely." My arithmetic from the Abu-Ghazalah et al. pooled distribution (8.6% blunder, 6.3% least-confident-correct) applied to a 20-item session: **~1.7 high-confidence errors and ~1.3 low-confidence corrects per session.** You would collect 20 ratings to relabel ~3 items — items that already receive full explanation feedback.

## Cost-benefit

**What it buys**

| Claimed benefit | Evidence status | Realisable without asking? |
|---|---|---|
| Hypercorrection of confident errors | Tier A phenomenon, γ .13–.28 | **Yes** — happens whenever feedback is given |
| Better retention of low-confidence corrects | Tier A, .40 → .85 | **Yes** — already have universal 4-choice explanations |
| Higher review priority for the right items | Untested as an intervention | Partly — prior mastery + latency (Tier B proxies) |
| Richer feedback for confident errors | **Tier B null** (Mory 1994 ×2) | Moot — uniform elaborated feedback is g = 0.49 |
| Better scheduling | No supporting evidence; FSRS 2-vs-4-button inconclusive-to-negative | n/a |
| Calibration gains | Null for practice alone; positive only with explicit training + feedback | No — this one genuinely requires measurement |
| Psychometric reliability for diagnostics | Tier B, "+50% more questions" equivalent | No — this one genuinely requires measurement |
| Direct reactivity boost to memory | g = 0.22 overall but **null-to-negative for educationally relevant material**; null for post-retrieval judgments | n/a |

**What it costs**

- **Learner time:** ~2–3 s/item → 40–60 s per 20-item session → 3–8% of session time (my arithmetic; 3,000 ms anchor from Double & Birney, 2018). Equivalent to ~1 additional practice item per session, forgone.
- **Engagement:** the only behavioural estimate available shows a 3–5× drop in voluntary uptake of practice questions when the same content required certainty ratings (Tier C, confounded). 67% of medical students reported it took longer; ~30% called it a waste of time.
- **Reactivity risk:** expected mean ≈ 0; negative tail for low-self-confidence learners (interaction β ≈ .30–.36); documented degradation of self-monitoring accuracy and a shift toward per-item performance orientation over coverage.
- **Data quality:** ratings are biased (8.6% of all responses are confident-and-wrong; 11–21% of confident responses are wrong), resolution metrics are **downward-biased for weaker learners** as a format artifact of 4-option MCQ, and forcing certainty consideration changes the answers themselves — contaminating the correctness signal the mastery model relies on.
- **Build and maintenance:** a rating step, storage, and — the expensive part — separate feedback/scheduling branches whose only direct test found no learning benefit and worse overall efficiency.

**Break-even**

To justify a 3–8% session-time cost with zero measured direct effect, confidence-based *targeting* must produce ≥5% more learning per unit time than uniform elaborated feedback. Mory (1994) and its web replication tested exactly this and found no performance difference, with non-adaptive feedback more efficient at the lesson level. So on current evidence the break-even is **not met, and the sign of the net effect is more likely negative than positive** once friction is included.

The alternative use of the same 40–60 s/session is well evidenced: one more item (testing effect, feedback vs no-feedback d ≈ 0.76–0.99 in Butler et al., 2008), or an in-session re-test of missed items (Fazio 2011; the fix for the confident-error return documented in Butler et al., 2011). **Both dominate.**

The break-even *is* plausibly met for one narrow purpose: a bounded diagnostic where you want the reliability gain (Tier B: equivalent to +50% more questions) or want to give explicit calibration feedback (Tier B: g = 0.34 for feedback on self-assessment accuracy). There, the cost is paid once rather than on every item forever, and learners get a visible return — which is precisely the condition under which acceptance was high in the CBM studies.

## If you do ship it

Minimum viable form, assuming the conditional exception above (diagnostic/metacognitive purpose, not retention):

**Scale.** **Three points**, labelled neutrally: low / medium / high. Three ordinal levels already produce non-overlapping performance tiers with tight credible intervals (Abu-Ghazalah et al., 9,106 responses), and Gardner-Medwin's three-level scheme is the most heavily used in practice (~1.8M self-test sessions). Do **not** use "guess," "hunch," "sure" — Gardner-Medwin explicitly abandoned descriptors because their interpretation is idiosyncratic. Two points loses the crucial middle "partial knowledge" tier (20.4% of responses in the Abu-Ghazalah et al. data); 7 points buys nothing and costs time. Avoid the literal word "confident" in the UI label if practical — the priming account of reactivity (Double & Birney, 2019) implicates the word itself; prefer "How sure were you?" or "How solid was that?"

**When to ask.** **After answer selection, before the explanation is revealed.** Before selection is a JOL and lands in the reactivity literature's murkier territory; after seeing the result is contaminated by hindsight and can't be used for hypercorrection targeting. Never gate progress on it: **make it skippable with one tap**, and never block the explanation behind it. Ask on a **sampled subset** (e.g. 5 items per session, or one full diagnostic session per week) rather than every item — this cuts the time cost by ~75% while preserving cohort-level statistics, since the Abu-Ghazalah et al. data show three-tier resolution was "not at all limited by sample size."

**Never score it, never let it change what the learner sees.** The moment confidence affects feedback richness or scheduling in a visible way, learners game it and the signal degrades. Also: adaptive feedback based on certitude has no demonstrated learning benefit, so there is nothing to trade for the contamination.

**What to do with the data, in priority order.**

1. **Calibration feedback to the learner**, aggregated weekly, never per item: "at high confidence you were right 84% of the time; at low confidence, 46%." This is the one use with genuine supporting evidence (g = 0.34 for feedback on self-assessment accuracy) and it is what drove learner acceptance in the CBM studies. Report **accuracy-within-confidence-level**, not a single "calibration score," to avoid the format artifact that penalises weaker learners.
2. **Content QA.** Plot mean confidence against accuracy per item. Gardner-Medwin's Fig. 2 quadrants are directly reusable: high confidence + low accuracy = a misconception or a broken item; low confidence + high accuracy = insecure understanding or an unnecessarily confusing stem. This is a **free win with no learning claim attached** and is arguably the strongest reason to collect the data at all.
3. **Cohort research, gated.** Use it to validate the free proxies (latency, prior mastery, answer changes). If they reconstruct confidence well, **remove the prompt.** That should be the explicit exit criterion.
4. **Do not** feed it into the scheduler or the mastery estimate. No evidence supports it; Butler et al. (2008) shows feedback partly invalidates it; FSRS's naturalistic data show graded self-rating does not beat binary.

**Measure the cost you're incurring.** Instrument time-to-rate per item and session-abandonment rate for rated vs unrated sessions. Given the CBM uptake data, pre-register a kill threshold (e.g. abandon if completion drops >3 percentage points or median session time rises >10%).

**Do these instead, or first — all confidence-agnostic, all better evidenced:**

1. **Refutation-style distractor prose.** Each distractor explanation names the misconception it embodies and refutes it before asserting the correct account (Van Loon et al., 2015).
2. **Guarantee explanation exposure on correct answers too.** This is the actual mechanism behind the low-confidence-correct payoff (Butler et al., 2008). If the UI lets learners skip explanations after correct answers, that is the bug worth fixing — it is worth d ≈ 0.76–0.99 and costs nothing to ask.
3. **In-session re-test of missed items, plus next-day re-test.** The documented fix for confident errors returning within a week (Butler et al., 2011; Fazio, 2011).
4. **Flag high-mastery-but-wrong as the priority correction event.** Prior knowledge beats confidence as a predictor (Sitzman et al., 2013) and you already compute it.
5. **Invest in explanation quality over feedback branching.** Elaborated vs correct-answer-only feedback: g = 0.49 vs 0.32 (Van der Kleij et al., 2015).

## DOK 1 — established facts

1. Errors committed with high confidence are corrected more often after corrective feedback than low-confidence errors; γ ≈ .13–.28, d ≈ 0.46–0.86 against zero. Replicated by multiple independent groups. **[A]**
2. The mechanism involves attentional capture by surprising feedback (selective impairment of a concurrent tone-detection task during high-confidence-error feedback; larger frontal P3; better source memory for expectation-incongruent feedback). **[A]**
3. High-confidence errors are also associated with greater prior domain knowledge and greater semantic proximity to the correct answer; participants who claim they "knew it all along" are partly right. **[A]**
4. Prior knowledge predicts error correction better than subjective confidence; confidence contributes little unique variance once prior knowledge is controlled. **[B]**
5. Hypercorrection persists over one week, but overall error correction falls from .86 to .56 and higher-confidence errors are *more* likely to be reproduced (γ = .15, d = 0.51). **[B]**
6. In 4-option MCQ with a 2-day delay, hypercorrection was not observed and the trend was toward hypocorrection. With verification-only (right/wrong) feedback in MCQ, confidence and error correction were unrelated. **[B]**
7. Feedback roughly doubles retention of low-confidence correct answers (.40 → .85 for "guess"-level items); overall correct-response retention .79 → .93, d = 0.76–0.99. The manipulation was feedback vs no feedback, not confidence-based targeting. **[A]**
8. Under forced responding on 4-option MCQ, 38–40% of correct responses are given guess-or-low confidence, and only 6–11% of errors are high-confidence. **[A]** (Butler et al., 2008, Table 1.)
9. Least-confident responses are correct well above chance (34–52% vs 20% for 5-option items), indicating partial knowledge rather than blind guessing. **[B]**
10. Learners spend more feedback study time on high-certitude errors and low-certitude corrects, i.e. they self-allocate feedback attention in proportion to expectation–outcome discrepancy, unprompted. **[B]**
11. Making feedback richer for high-certitude errors and low-certitude corrects (confidence-adaptive feedback) produced **no** performance advantage over invariant feedback, and worse overall lesson efficiency. Two studies. **[B]**
12. Elaborated feedback beats knowledge-of-correct-response beats knowledge-of-result: g = 0.49 / 0.32 / 0.05. **[A]**
13. Immediate JOLs change memory overall by g = 0.22, but the effect is g = 0.44 for related word pairs and g = −0.09 for unrelated pairs, with publication bias favouring positive reactivity. **[A]**
14. For educationally relevant material — general knowledge facts tested by MC and short answer, key-term definitions, images — immediate JOL reactivity is **null**, and negative when difficulty is high. **[B]**
15. Making a metacognitive judgment after a retrieval attempt has no effect on later memory beyond the retrieval practice itself (four replication attempts). **[B]**
16. Reactivity to *confidence ratings* specifically is moderated by trait self-confidence: beneficial for high-self-confidence learners, detrimental for low-self-confidence learners (interaction β ≈ .30–.36); the mechanism appears to be priming of pre-existing self-beliefs by the word "confident." **[B]**
17. Eliciting confidence ratings made metacognitive monitoring **worse** in one controlled study (N = 89), with rated participants anchoring self-appraisals on prior self-confidence rather than actual performance. **[B]**
18. Item-level confidence resolution in MCQ general knowledge is γ ≈ .55–.59; three confidence levels separate accuracy tiers of ~80–88% / ~52–70% / ~34–52%. **[A/B]**
19. Confident-and-wrong ("blunder") is 8.6% of all responses; 11–21% of confident responses are wrong. Overconfidence bias on 4-option MCQ is modest (~8 percentage points, confidence .60 vs accuracy .52). **[B]**
20. Measures of metacognitive resolution are mathematically biased downward as accuracy falls in formats that permit guessing; the apparent novice deficit in MC resolution is at least partly artifact, and absent in free-response testing. **[B]**
21. Low performers are substantially more miscalibrated than high performers (~4× in the original Dunning–Kruger data) and worse at discriminating their own correct from incorrect answers. **[A]** (Metacognitive interpretation contested. **[B]**)
22. Corrective feedback by itself improves calibration and resolution on later tests (resolution γ .67 → .94; over-confidence eliminated). Retrieval practice by itself improves confidence-judgment accuracy. **[B]**
23. Overt calibration practice without instruction does not improve calibration or exam performance (N = 356). Repeated confidence self-reporting in a real course did not improve metacognitive accuracy over time. **[B]/[C]**
24. Feedback on self-assessment accuracy improves that accuracy: g = 0.34, 201 effect sizes, 47 studies, N = 5,487. **[B]**
25. Certainty-based marking increased exam reliability by an amount equivalent to using >50% more conventionally marked questions. **[B]**
26. FSRS treats its 4-point self-rating as binary in its loss function, and is at least as accurate — possibly more so — for users who use only two buttons; a direct 2-vs-4-button comparison was inconclusive. **[C]**
27. In a medical-student cohort (N = 400), voluntary uptake of the same practice-question resource fell from 40–46% to 8–15% when it required certainty ratings; 67% reported it took more time (modal estimate ~50% more); ~30% called it a waste of time. **[C]**
28. Learner acceptance of certainty ratings is high when accompanied by automated over/under-confidence feedback (78% fair, 94% useful in one cohort) and low when it is not. **[B]**
29. Hypercorrection of science misconceptions occurred only after refutation texts that explicitly stated and rejected the misconception, not after standard factual texts. **[B]**
30. Making JOLs shifts study-time allocation toward *easier* items and away from harder ones (changed-goal account), impairing hard-item recall. **[B]**

## DOK 2 — insights and implications

1. **The load-bearing distinction is measurement versus mechanism.** Both hypothesised payoffs are conditional relationships discovered by measuring confidence in feedback experiments. Neither is an effect of measuring. Every published hypercorrection and low-confidence-correct benefit was obtained under *uniform* feedback. The intuition "confident-and-wrong is important" is correct; the inference "therefore we should ask" does not follow.
2. **This product has already shipped the intervention.** Pre-written explanation prose for all four choices on every item *is* the Butler et al. (2008) treatment. The remaining question is not whether to add confidence but whether learners actually read explanations after correct answers — a UI question worth d ≈ 0.8, considerably more than anything confidence buys.
3. **Confidence is a lossy substitute for two signals you already own.** Prior mastery is the better predictor of error correction (Sitzman et al.), and latency carries much of confidence's information (Weidemann & Kahana). "High-mastery concept, fast, wrong" is a confident-error detector that costs nothing and cannot be gamed.
4. **Feedback partially destroys the signal you'd be scheduling on.** Giving feedback cut the confidence→final-performance gamma from .70 to .40. A well-fed-back item's initial confidence rating is a weaker forward predictor than it looks in no-feedback studies. Scheduling on confidence is scheduling on a pre-treatment measurement.
5. **The multiple-choice format is systematically less favourable than the literature's headline suggests.** Clean hypercorrection lives in forced-report short-answer generation. In 4-option selection, the effect vanished at 2 days and vanished under verification-only feedback; resolution metrics are downward-biased by guessing; and 25% chance-level guessing manufactures low-confidence correct answers whose "fragility" is partly just luck. A review that quoted only Butterfield & Metcalfe (2001) would badly overestimate the applicability here.
6. **Reactivity is the strongest argument against, not because it is harmful on average, but because the average is zero and the variance is structured.** For exactly our materials — general knowledge facts, MC and short answer — reactivity is null; for post-retrieval judgments it is null; and the non-zero effects concentrate as *harm* in low-self-confidence learners, a subgroup over-represented in high-stakes admissions-test preparation. You would be paying real time for a zero-mean lottery with a bad tail.
7. **Base rates make the accounting worse than the effect sizes suggest.** ~1.7 high-confidence errors and ~1.3 low-confidence corrects per 20-item session (my arithmetic from the Abu-Ghazalah et al. distribution). Twenty prompts to relabel three items that already get full feedback. Metcalfe's own review concedes "people make high-confidence errors only rarely."
8. **Only two justifications survive, and both are measurement justifications, not learning ones.** Psychometric reliability (+50%-more-questions equivalent) and calibration feedback to the learner (g = 0.34). Both are better served by a bounded, opt-in diagnostic than by a per-item tax on the daily loop — which also happens to be the configuration learners accept (86% of Schoendorfer & Emmett's cohort thought CBM belonged in revision, not routine assessment).
9. **The dose–response logic points at content, not instrumentation.** The largest confidence-adjacent effects in this literature come from what the feedback *says* (elaborated vs correct-answer-only, g = 0.49 vs 0.32; refutation vs standard text, hypercorrection present vs absent) and from whether there is a *second retrieval* (near-zero forgetting at one week vs .86 → .56 decay). Both are confidence-blind. Engineering effort spent on refutation-structured distractor prose and same-session re-testing has a much better evidentiary return than engineering effort spent on a rating widget.
10. **Beware the circular ML result.** "Adding confidence raises AUC from 0.65 to 0.88" is predicting an answer's correctness from the learner's belief about that same answer. It measures resolution, not scheduling capability, and the paper reporting it says so. Any internal analysis that repeats this pattern should be discounted.
11. **The honest framing of "low-confidence correct" is partial knowledge, not unconscious knowledge.** The implicit-knowledge literature is strategically fragile (accurate guessing appears and disappears with instructions to guess vs be confident, and has failed to replicate). The design conclusion — reinforce these items — is the same either way, so don't build product language on the shakier framing.
12. **If you ship it, the exit criterion matters more than the launch criterion.** The most valuable outcome of collecting confidence is discovering that latency and prior mastery predict it well enough to stop collecting it. Design the experiment so that success means removing the prompt.

## What I could not verify

- **No per-item time measurement exists** for a confidence prompt in a practice-question system. My 2–3 s figure is inferred from an experimental control screen (3,000 ms) used to equate for rating time, and my 3–8% session-cost figure is arithmetic on assumed item durations, not an empirical finding.
- **No study directly compares** "confidence-targeted elaborated feedback" against "uniform elaborated feedback" in a modern digital practice system with delayed retention as the outcome. Mory (1994) and its web replication are the closest and are 20–30 years old, with abstract-level access only, modest samples, and lesson-based rather than item-bank designs. This is the single most important missing experiment for this decision.
- **No evidence either way on whether item-level calibration gains transfer to standardised admissions-test scores.** The claimed downstream benefits (better study allocation, time allocation) are plausible and untested; the "when to guess" benefit is inapplicable to the MCAT's unpenalised format.
- **The FSRS 2-vs-4-button question is genuinely unresolved.** The larger analysis favours two buttons but is confounded by self-selection; the cleaner comparison had only 39 consistent four-button collections and was inconclusive. I found no peer-reviewed ablation of graded self-rating versus binary correctness in a spaced-repetition scheduler.
- **Authorship and peer-review status unverified** for the AERA-repository item on repeated metacognitive-judgment training (N = 208, η² = .08–.19); I read an abstract with a results table and could not confirm the venue. Tiered C accordingly. The self-assessment feedback meta-analysis (g = 0.34) was read as an OSF preprint; I did not locate a published version.
- **The uptake-collapse figure (40–46% → 8–15%) is confounded.** Different module, different content, novelty effect, natural within-term engagement decline, and a published Letter to the Editor challenged the percentage reporting. I am using it as directional evidence only.
- **Read as abstract or landing page only** (no full text): Butterfield & Metcalfe (2001); Sitzman, Rhodes & Tauber (2013) — abstract plus a discussion excerpt; Ingendahl et al. (2025) *Psychological Bulletin*; Ingendahl & Undorf (2024) *JARMAC* and (2026) *Metacognition and Learning*; Double, Birney & Walker (2018); Double & Birney (2017a); Mory (1994) and the UNCG web replication; Stock et al. (1992); Hancock, Stock & Kulhavy (1992); Kulhavy & Stock (1989) — not read at all, known only through citation in Butler et al. (2008); Van Loon et al. (2015); Van der Kleij et al. (2015); the 2022 network meta-analysis of computer-based feedback; Bol et al. (2005); Bol & Hacker (2001); the Compass/SIGCSE deployment; the MOOC planning-prompt RCTs; the metacognitive-prompts meta-analysis; Weidemann & Kahana (2016); the metacognitive-resolution confound paper (abstract plus excerpts); Schoendorfer & Emmett (2012) — abstract plus partial full text.
- **Publisher-listed authorship discrepancies** appear for the *Psychological Bulletin* reactivity meta-analysis (listed variously as Ingendahl/Schäfer with Halamish and Undorf, apparently reflecting a name change). The numbers are quoted from the abstract; treat the author string as approximate.
- **I did not search non-English literature**, unpublished industry A/B tests, or ITS vendor data, all of which could contain relevant friction estimates.

# Item Review and Answer Changing in Computerized Adaptive Testing: Evidence Review

Scope: (a) do test-takers object to being unable to review/change answers in CAT, (b) does review harm measurement or enable score inflation, (c) does review affect anxiety, perceived fairness, and attitudes. Plus operational policy in real adaptive programmes.

Provenance markers: `[read]` = full text retrieved and inspected; `[abstract]` = abstract or publisher summary only; `[secondary]` = taken from another paper's description of the source.

Caveat on dates: the core empirical literature is 1992-2005. Operational practice has changed since (GRE 2011, GMAT 2023), so 1990s statements like "virtually no operational CATs allow review" are no longer accurate.

## A. Examinee attitudes toward the absence of review

### A1. Vispoel, W. P. (1993). Computerized adaptive and fixed-item versions of the ITED vocabulary subtest. *Educational and Psychological Measurement*, 53(3), 779-788. `[abstract]`
https://doi.org/10.1177/0013164493053003022

DOK 1: 165 college students randomly assigned to an adaptive test (n = 82) or a fixed-item test (n = 83). The 13-item adaptive test gave higher reliability and concurrent validity than the 40-item fixed test. Examinees expressed favourable attitudes about visual displays, directions, and practice items, but "unfavorable attitudes about the exclusion of review and skip options." They preferred computerized over paper-and-pencil formats.

DOK 2: The earliest primary source in the Vispoel line reporting negative examinee attitudes toward the no-review restriction specifically. The finding as stated in the abstract is qualitative: no percentage or effect size for the review-specific dissatisfaction is given, so the strength of the objection cannot be quantified from this source.

### A2. Vispoel, W. P., Rocklin, T., & Wang, T. (1994). Individual differences and test administration procedures: A comparison of fixed-item, computerized-adaptive, and self-adapted testing. *Applied Measurement in Education*, 7(1), 53-79. `[abstract]`
https://doi.org/10.1207/s15324818ame0701_5

DOK 1: Volunteer college students randomly assigned to fixed-item (FIT), computer-adaptive (CAT), or self-adapted (SAT) vocabulary tests from a common calibrated bank. CAT was more precise and efficient than SAT, which beat FIT. SAT produced higher ability estimates for examinees with lower verbal self-concept. "Examinees indicated by their evaluations of elements of the testing procedures that they prefer tests in which they can have as much control and as much information as possible." Conclusion: no single procedure simultaneously maximises precision, efficiency, validity, and examinee satisfaction. Sample size not given in the abstract.

DOK 2: This is the paper most often cited for the claim that examinees dislike the CAT review restriction, so read the wording carefully. The abstract states a general preference for control and information, not specifically for item review. The stronger review-specific rendering ("examinees tended to view the restriction on item review and answer changes as one of the main disadvantages of CAT") comes from Cui et al. (2018) citing this paper; I could not verify that against the 1994 full text.

### A3. Wise, S. L., & Kingsbury, G. G. (2000). Practical issues in developing and maintaining a computerized adaptive testing program. *Psicologica*, 21, 135-155. `[read]`
https://www.redalyc.org/pdf/169/16921108.pdf

DOK 1: "Currently, virtually no operational CATs provide an opportunity for examinees to go back and review their answers to previously administered items." "Although the reactions of examinees to CATs have been generally positive, however, they have consistently reported dissatisfaction with the absence of item review (Baghi, Ferrara & Gabrys, 1992; Legg & Buhr, 1992; Vispoel, Rocklin & Wang, 1994; Vispoel, Wang, de la Torre, Bleiler & Dings, 1992)." "Over sixty years of research on answer changing has consistently shown that (a) when examinees are allowed to change answers, they are more likely to improve their scores (albeit typically slightly), and (b) score gains due to answer changes are overwhelmingly due to reasons such as re-thinking or re-reading the item, or making a clerical error." Notes that increased perceived control has been associated with decreased anxiety and improved task performance (Glass & Singer, 1972; Blechman & Dannemiller, 1976; Perlmuter & Monty, 1977).

DOK 2: A review chapter by two senior practitioners that treats the "consistent dissatisfaction" finding as settled and names its four primary sources. It contains no new data. It also states the strongest counter-argument: if no one gets review, everyone is treated equally, which is consistent with increased standardisation.

## B. Psychometric effects of review in live testing

### B1. Lunz, M. E., Bergstrom, B. A., & Wright, B. D. (1992). The effect of review on student ability and test efficiency for computerized adaptive tests. *Applied Psychological Measurement*, 16(1), 33-40. `[read]`
https://conservancy.umn.edu/bitstreams/88947517-4938-4927-9c8f-f7bceaf47672/download

DOK 1: Variable-length adaptive certification test. Review condition N = 220, no-review condition N = 492, random assignment; average 96 items administered. Average items altered = 2 (min 0, max 16); 2% of responses altered overall. 85 of 220 examinees altered nothing; 135 altered at least one. Of those 135: 71 improved their ability estimate, 30 declined, 34 unchanged. Ability before/after review correlated .98. Mean ability .216 (SD .47) before, .243 (SD .48) after; mean change .027 logits. Relative test efficiency ratio 99% (SD 3%), i.e. efficiency fell 1%. Mean information loss .11, recoverable on average by adding half an item; 108 of the 135 changers would need no additional items, 23 would need 2-5, and 4 would need 6-14. Three examinees moved from fail to pass, all within one standard error of the cut. Conclusion: "Disallowing review on adaptive tests administered under these rules is not supported by these data."

DOK 2: The first live-data study of CAT review, and the cleanest evidence that unrestricted end-of-test review in a real adaptive certification setting costs almost nothing psychometrically. The critical caveat, made explicit later by Stocking, is that these examinees were not coached in score-gaming strategies, so this speaks to naive review behaviour only.

### B2. Stone, G. E., & Lunz, M. E. (1994). The effect of review on the psychometric characteristics of computerized adaptive tests. *Applied Measurement in Education*, 7(3), 211-222. `[abstract]`, item-revision rates `[secondary]`
https://doi.org/10.1207/s15324818ame0703_4

DOK 1 (abstract): Two examinee populations took two different computerized certification examinations, each split into high/medium/low ability groups. Ability measures before and after review were highly correlated; decision confidence was slightly lower after review; pass/fail status was most affected near the pass point; "Decisions remained the same for 94% of the examinees." Test precision only slightly affected; average information loss recoverable by adding one item. `[secondary]` via Stocking (1996): minimum test length 50, maximum 100; examinees receiving 50-item tests revised about 6% of items; Test 1 examinees above 50 items averaged 81 items and revised about 4%; Test 2 comparable examinees averaged 85 items and revised about 5%.

DOK 2: Replicates Lunz et al. (1992) on higher-stakes certification exams. The 94%-unchanged decision rate is the most decision-relevant number in the live-review literature, and the 6% of changes concentrates exactly where a pass/fail or placement decision is most sensitive.

### B3. Vispoel, W. P. (1998). Reviewing and changing answers on computer-adaptive and self-adaptive vocabulary tests. *Journal of Educational Measurement*, 35(4), 328-345. `[abstract]`
https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3984.1998.tb00542.x

DOK 1: Computer-adaptive and self-adaptive vocabulary tests, review permitted vs not. Within the review condition: 5.23% of answers changed; wrong-to-right vs right-to-wrong ratio 2.92:1; 66.5% of examinees changed at least one answer; among changers, improvement-to-decline ratio 2.55:1; review particularly beneficial at high ability levels. Review vs no-review: no significant differences in ability estimates or estimated measurement error, and no trustworthy evidence that test anxiety moderated review effects on those indexes. "Most examinees desired review, but permitting it increased testing time by 41%."

DOK 2: The strongest single-source pairing of a preference finding with a null measurement finding. The 41% testing-time increase is the largest documented cost of unrestricted review and is a stronger practical argument against end-of-test review than any measurement degradation observed in live data. Sample size not stated in the abstract.

### B4. Vispoel, W. P. (2000). Reviewing and changing answers on computerized fixed-item vocabulary tests. *Educational and Psychological Measurement*, 60(3), 371-384. `[abstract]`
https://doi.org/10.1177/00131640021970600

DOK 1: Computerized fixed-item (non-adaptive) vocabulary tests, review vs no review. No statistically significant differences in number-correct scores, ability estimates, or measurement error, but "examinees in both conditions strongly desired review opportunities." Within the review condition: 3.63% of answers changed; wrong-to-right vs right-to-wrong ratio 2.25:1; 45% of examinees changed at least one answer; among changers, improvement-to-decline ratio 2.44:1. Gains after review were greater for higher-ability examinees. "Review was desired more by examinees with higher test anxiety." Testing time increased 35%.

DOK 2: The fixed-item control case, and an important one. It shows the preference for review is not specific to adaptive testing, and that the anxiety-preference link appears even where no adaptive algorithm is at stake. That weakens any argument that the review preference is a reaction to adaptivity as such.

### B5. Vispoel, W. P., Hendrickson, A. B., & Bleiler, T. (2000). Limiting answer review and change on computerized adaptive vocabulary tests: Psychometric and attitudinal results. *Journal of Educational Measurement*, 37(1), 21-38. `[abstract]`, one figure `[secondary]`
https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3984.2000.tb01074.x

DOK 1: Live vocabulary CATs under four conditions: (a) no review, (b) review within successive 5-item blocks, (c) review within successive 10-item blocks, (d) review only after all 40 items. "No trustworthy differences among conditions in vocabulary proficiency estimates, measurement error, or testing time." Within each review condition, ability estimates and number-correct scores increased slightly after review; more answers changed wrong-to-right than right-to-wrong; most examinees who changed answers improved their estimates; "nearly all examinees indicated that they had an adequate opportunity to review their previous answers." `[secondary]` via Han (2011/2013): "the majority of examinees (98.4%) in this study felt they had adequate opportunity to review and change their responses."

DOK 2: The single most directly relevant study for an MST-style design, because it tests the block-review model in a live setting and finds it satisfies examinees with no measurable psychometric cost and, unlike end-of-test review, no testing-time penalty. The 98.4% figure is the strongest quantitative attitudinal number in this literature, but I have it only via Han's description.

### B6. Papanastasiou, E. C. (2002). A "rearrangement procedure" for scoring adaptive tests with review options. ERIC ED464095. `[read]` Published as Papanastasiou (2005), *Educational Research and Evaluation*, 11(4), 303-321. `[abstract]`
https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED464095.pdf and https://doi.org/10.1080/13803610500110521

DOK 1: Simulation with 26,000 simulated examinees, maximum 5 reviews permitted. Maximum-likelihood bias: -0.1374 before review, +0.0718 after review, +0.0610 after the rearrangement procedure (a 15.6% reduction in magnitude vs after-review bias). Standard error: 0.3229 before review, 0.3346 after review, 0.3394 after rearrangement. Reliability of ML estimates: 0.817 before review, 0.811 after review, 0.806 after rearrangement, described as a negligible decrease.

DOK 2: Proposes scoring-side rather than interface-side mitigation: skip and reorder items whose selection was invalidated by an answer change. The bias reduction is real but modest and costs a little reliability and standard error. Cui et al. (2018) later found this approach inferior to simple block review.

## C. Anxiety, perceived fairness, and control

### C1. Olea, J., Revuelta, J., Ximenez, M. C., & Abad, F. J. (2000). Psychometric and psychological effects of review on computerized fixed and adaptive tests. *Psicologica*, 21, 157-173. `[read]`
https://www.redalyc.org/pdf/169/16921109.pdf

DOK 1: English-vocabulary test for Spanish speakers, 2x2 design (CAT vs FIT; review allowed vs not), Spanish first-year psychology undergraduates, medium-stakes framing (results posted publicly by percentile). Final sample 182 after excluding 2. Cells: CAT no-review n = 46, CAT review n = 47, FIT no-review n = 44, FIT review n = 45. Post-minus-pretest state anxiety (Spielberger SAS split into two equivalent halves): review group mean -0.91 (anxiety fell), no-review group mean +0.51 (anxiety rose), main effect of review p = 0.012; test type and interaction not significant. Table 1 cell means for post-minus-pre anxiety: CAT no-review -1.64 (SD 4.02), CAT review 0.35 (SD 4.52), FIT no-review -0.21 (SD 3.62), FIT review 0.68 (SD 2.88). Comfort item 1 (calmness): FIT 3.24 vs CAT 2.97, p = 0.039. Of 92 subjects allowed to review, 75 (81.52%) chose to. In the CAT, 80% reviewed; among reviewers 66.7% improved their ability estimate, 25% decreased, 8.3% unchanged. In the FIT, 83% reviewed; 59% improved, 20.5% decreased, 20.5% unchanged. 13.5% of answers changed overall (CAT 12.6%: 42% wrong-to-wrong, 43% wrong-to-right, 15% right-to-wrong; FIT 14.4%: 54% wrong-to-wrong, 31% wrong-to-right, 15% right-to-wrong). Ability before/after review correlated 0.951 (CAT) and 0.941 (FIT). Review significantly increased estimated ability (0.6932 before, 0.8058 after) and number correct; measurement error not significantly changed. Testing time increased 52%. Conclusion: "Under these conditions, there are no arguments to advise against the inclusion of item review."

DOK 2: The only study I retrieved that measured state anxiety both before and after the test in review and no-review conditions, and it found the predicted direction. One caution: the Table 1 cell means as transcribed do not obviously reconcile with the marginal means quoted in the text, so treat -0.91 / +0.51 as the reported contrast rather than as recomputable. The 52% time increase exceeds Vispoel's 41% and 35%.

### C2. Revuelta, J., Ximenez, M. C., & Olea, J. (2003). Psychometric and psychological effects of item selection and review on computerized testing. *Educational and Psychological Measurement*, 63(5), 791-808. `[abstract]`
https://doi.org/10.1177/0013164403251282

DOK 1: 557 first-year psychology undergraduates. Three administration types (adaptive, easy adaptive, fixed) crossed with four review conditions (no review, review at end, review by blocks of 5, review item-by-item). "Statistically significant effects were found in test precision among the different types of test. Response review improved ability estimates and increased testing time. No psychological effects on anxiety were found. Examinees in all review conditions considered more important the possibility of review than those who were not allowed to review."

DOK 2: The largest sample in this literature and a direct partial non-replication of the anxiety finding by an overlapping author team: with 557 examinees and an explicit manipulation of perceived control, no anxiety effect emerged. The preference finding did replicate, with a twist worth noting: valuing review was higher among those who had it, suggesting the preference is partly constructed by experience rather than a standing grievance.

Note on perceived fairness: Olea et al. (2000) assert that examinees "perceive that the test is more fair" when review is allowed, attributing this to Vispoel (1993), whose abstract does not use fairness language. I found no primary study measuring perceived fairness of review as a distinct construct.

## D. Strategic answer changing: Wainer's dilemma and its empirical size

### D1. Wainer, H. (1993). Some practical considerations when converting a linearly administered test to an adaptive format. *Educational Measurement: Issues and Practice*, 12(1), 15-20. `[abstract]`; earlier ETS RR 92-13 `[abstract]`; substantially the same argument restated in Wainer, H. (2000), *Psicologica*, 21, 121-133. `[read]`
https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3992.1993.tb00519.x | https://doi.org/10.1002/j.2333-8504.1992.tb01445.x | https://www.redalyc.org/pdf/169/16921107.pdf

DOK 1 (from the 2000 restatement, read in full): "Response review. Every year throughout their schooling, students are instructed to use the test time constructively. When they have completed the exam they are told to use any remaining time to go back and check over their answers. It is hoped that by doing this the frequency of careless errors can be reduced. In a CAT no such review is possible. Once the answer is given, the next item is chosen on the basis of that response. If a response was to be subsequently changed, the entire string of items that had been selected for that individual may no longer be optimal. Hence review is usually not allowed. Skip and return. If a student is unsure of the answer to an item on a linearly administered test they are often coached to not waste time on it, but rather to skip it and come back to it later. On most CATs such behavior is not possible." `[secondary]` via Stocking (1996) for the gaming strategy: "Wainer suggests that a test-taker should intentionally respond incorrectly, if possible, to any item presented until the end of the test is reached. At the end of the test the test-taker has designed the easiest possible test from the item pool, and can now revise as many answers as possible to correct."

DOK 2: The origin of the "Wainer strategy," and worth being precise about what it is: an argument from first principles about item-selection optimality, presenting no data. The strategy is a speculation that later authors simulated. Wainer is also the source of the observation that review is a normal, taught test-taking behaviour that CAT removes, which is the affective core of the complaint claim.

### D2. Stocking, M. L. (1996). Revising answers to items in computerized adaptive tests: A comparison of three models. ETS Research Report 96-12. `[read]` (OCR-degraded). Published as Stocking (1997), *Applied Psychological Measurement*, 21(2), 129-142. `[abstract]`
https://doi.org/10.1002/j.2333-8504.1996.tb01690.x | https://doi.org/10.1177/01466216970212003

DOK 1, baseline linear data: 6,977 test-takers on linear computer-based GRE Quantitative, Verbal, and Analytical Reasoning measures, scores counting for admissions. Average items responded to 59.23 of 60, 75.34 of 76, 48.54 of 50 (97-99% of intended length). Average maximum items reviewed 8.09 / 17.73 / 6.84. Average items revised 3.52 / 8.21 / 4.84. Revision rates excluding omits: 1%, 3%, 2%. Over 70% of revised items had originally been omitted, and just over 50% of those were finally answered correctly.

DOK 1, simulation under a worst-case model where all test-takers deliberately answer incorrectly first. Model 1 (revise n items at end of test; 28-item Quantitative CAT; conditions 0, 2, 7, 14, 28): with 2 revisions CSEM already exceeded the no-revision baseline; with 7 revisions CSEM more than doubled at some true-score levels; at 7 revisions the 95th percentile ran 30-40 scaled score points above retesting expectation, at 14 and 28 the 90th percentile similarly; with all 28 revised, the 95th, 98th, and 99th percentiles were more than 60 scaled score points high (200-800 scale). Model 2 (separately timed sections, free revision within a section), estimated reliability: .927 with no revisions, .921 with 7 sections, .915 with 4 sections, .880 with 2 sections, .798 with 1 section (full end-of-test review); the conventional linear reference test was .922 number-right and .933 estimated-true-score. With 4 or 7 sections all percentile differences vs retesting were under 10 scaled score points; with 2 sections the largest was a little over 20 at the 95th percentile. Model 3 (revision only within common-stimulus item sets), applied to Quantitative, Verbal, Analytical and Reading pools: all percentiles within 10 scaled score points of retesting for the first three, within about half a point for Reading.

DOK 2: The decisive simulation. It shows the Wainer strategy is a genuine threat only when revisions do not feed back into item selection, and that dividing an adaptive test into blocks with within-block review essentially neutralises it. The reliability series (.927 / .921 / .915 / .880 / .798) is the single most useful quantitative result for a design choosing between item-level CAT and block or MST review, and it is derived under an admittedly "unrealistic worst-case" assumption of universal, perfectly executed cheating. Stocking also notes that the low observed revision rates in Lunz et al. and Stone & Lunz probably mean examinees simply were not aware of the strategy.

### D3. Vispoel, W. P., Rocklin, T. R., Wang, T., & Bleiler, T. (1999). Can examinees use a review option to obtain positively biased ability estimates on a computerized adaptive test? *Journal of Educational Measurement*, 36(2), 141-157. `[abstract]`
https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3984.1999.tb00551.x

DOK 1: Simulated and live testing data. "There were instances in which the Wainer strategy yielded inflated ability estimates as well as instances in which it yielded deflated ability estimates. The success of the strategy in inflating ability estimates depended on the ability estimation method used (maximum likelihood versus Bayesian), the examinee's true ability level, the standard error of the ability estimate, the examinee's ability to implement the strategy, and the type of decision made from the ability estimate."

DOK 2: The main empirical check on Stocking's worst-case assumption using real examinees, and it finds the strategy unreliable in practice. No effect sizes are given in the abstract.

### D4. Vispoel, W. P., Clough, S. J., Bleiler, T., Hendrickson, A. B., & Ihrig, D. (2002). Can examinees use judgments of item difficulty to improve proficiency estimates on computerized adaptive vocabulary tests? *Journal of Educational Measurement*, 39(4), 311-330. `[abstract]`, two figures `[secondary]`
https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3984.2002.tb01145.x

DOK 1: Examinees were explicitly taught two item-difficulty-judgment ("Kingsbury" family) strategies designed to inflate scores, then randomly assigned along with untaught controls to vocabulary CATs with end-of-test review or block review. "Proficiency estimates following review were significantly higher in the regular conditions than in the strategy conditions. Failure to obtain systematically higher scores in the strategy conditions was due in large part to errors examinees made in judging the relative difficulty of CAT items." `[secondary]` via Han (2011): "Test takers were only 61% successful in distinguishing the difficulty difference within each item pair." `[secondary]` via Han citing Vispoel, Clough & Bleiler (2005): success rates between 46% and 61% when the difficulty difference was under 0.50.

DOK 2: Examinees taught to game the test scored lower than those who were not, because they misjudged item difficulty and wasted effort. This is the strongest available evidence that the theoretical gaming threat does not survive contact with real test-takers, at least for difficulty-judgment strategies.

### D5. Cui, Z., Liu, C., He, Y., & Chen, H. (2018). Comparison of algorithms that allow item review in computerized adaptive testing. ACT Research Report 2018-2 (R1709). `[read]`
https://www.act.org/content/dam/act/unsecured/documents/pdfs/R1709-cat-comparison-2018-10.pdf

DOK 1: Simulation, 10,000 examinees, 30-item fixed-length CAT, pool of more than 1,000 3PL-calibrated operational items, EAP interim / MLE final estimation, maximum exposure 0.2. Reported as correlation / CSEM / RMSE / bias. Baseline, no review, normal test-taking: 0.98 / 0.21 / 0.22 / 0.00. Kingsbury strategy under every review scheme: 0.98 / 0.21 / 0.22-0.23 / 0.05. Unrestricted review: random-guessing 0.85 / 1.31 / 0.82 / 0.21; Wainer 0.83 / 2.75 / 0.87 / 0.13. Rearrangement method: random 0.96 / 0.48 / 0.32 / 0.02; Wainer 0.95 / 0.68 / 0.37 / 0.04. Block review, 2 blocks: random 0.94 / 0.39 / 0.36 / 0.01; Wainer 0.94 / 0.40 / 0.36 / -0.01. Block review, 3 blocks: random 0.97 / 0.26 / 0.27 / -0.01; Wainer 0.96 / 0.28 / 0.28 / -0.01. Block review, 6 blocks: random 0.98 / 0.22 / 0.23 / 0.00; Wainer 0.97 / 0.23 / 0.23 / 0.00. With unrestricted review, most estimation errors spread from a (-1, 1) range at baseline to (-4, 4). High-ability examinees gained from the Wainer strategy; low-ability examinees tended to lose score by using it. Random guessing inflated scores more than the Wainer strategy did.

DOK 2: The most recent and most quantitatively complete comparison, and the strongest single citation for the claim that block-level review is a win-win. With six blocks of five items, every statistic under active cheating is indistinguishable from a no-review baseline. It warns that with only two blocks the protection is noticeably weaker. Its attitudinal claims (Vispoel, Rocklin & Wang 1994; Bowles & Pommerich 2001) are secondary citations with no new data.

### D6. Han, K. T. (2013). Item pocket method to allow response review and change in computerized adaptive testing. *Applied Psychological Measurement*, 37(4), 259-275. `[abstract]`; NCME 2011 conference version `[read]`
https://doi.org/10.1177/0146621612473638 | https://www.umass.edu/remp/software/simcata/papers/NCME2011_HAN.pdf

DOK 1: Proposes an "item pocket": test-takers defer items into a holding area and revisit them across section boundaries, removing the block barrier. Han's characterisation of the prior literature: Stocking's Model 1 controlled the Wainer strategy only when revisions were limited to 2 of 28 items and "failed to control the effect of the Wainer strategy" beyond that; Model 2 with two sections "substantially reduced" the effect; Model 3 was robust but barred revision of discrete items. Han argues a practical drawback of block review: when blocks are not separately timed, test-takers must repeatedly decide whether to spend remaining time reviewing the current block or advancing, which "very likely causes test takers the same kind of test anxiety" review was meant to relieve. Cui et al. (2018) excluded the item-pocket method as "more answer delaying than answer changing."

DOK 2: Useful mainly as a well-sourced synthesis and as a caution that block review is not costless from the test-taker's side unless blocks are separately timed. The item-pocket method itself has not been adopted operationally as far as I could determine.

## E. Operational policy in real adaptive programmes

### E1. GRE General Test. ETS (2017). *The Research Foundation for the GRE revised General Test: A Compendium of Studies*. `[read]`
https://www.ets.org/pdfs/gre/gre-compendium.pdf

DOK 1: "The model used with the GRE General Test was adaptive at the question level... However, the CAT design did not allow for some of the goals underlying the revision of the test to be attained. As a result, a different test design was needed." "The Verbal Reasoning and Quantitative Reasoning measures of the computer-based GRE revised General Test use an MST design, meaning that the test is adaptive at the section level. This test design allows test takers to move freely within any timed section, allowing them to use more of their own personal test-taking strategies and providing a friendlier test-taking experience. Specific features include preview and review capabilities within a section, mark and review features to tag questions so that test takers can skip them and return later if they have time remaining in the section, the ability to change/edit answers within a section, and an on screen calculator." "The revised test provides many advantages to test takers, such as the ability to review and change answers, the opportunity to skip a question and revisit it later, and an on-screen calculator." Stated test design goals: "enhancing security, introducing more test taker-friendly features, and ensuring access to testing, as well as enhancing the validity and the measurement characteristics of the test scores." A linear-form model was explored first and rejected because "a relatively small number of large, fixed test administrations did not meet the goals of the program to provide frequent access to testing" and its testing time and long-term sustainability "were considered less than desirable." "The MST design represented a compromise between the question-level CAT design and a linear test design and met the test design goals." Launch 1 August 2011; scaling population for the new 130-170 scales was 146,504 examinees tested 1 August - 2 October 2011; 40 scored questions per measure across two sections.

DOK 2: This is the authoritative statement of ETS's own reasons, and it matters for how the MST-versus-CAT claim should be worded. ETS lists within-section review as an advantage of MST and part of a "test taker-friendly" goal, but does not present the move to MST as driven primarily by review, and cites no examinee complaints as evidence. The decision chain it describes runs: continuous-access and security goals ruled out linear forms, question-level CAT could not meet the revision goals, MST was the compromise. Review is a stated benefit of the chosen design, not the documented cause of the choice.

### E2. GRE Guide to the Use of Scores (current). ETS. `[read]`
https://www.ets.org/content/dam/ets-india/pdfs/gre/gre-guide-to-the-use-of-scores.pdf

DOK 1: "The Verbal Reasoning and Quantitative Reasoning measures of the GRE General Test are section-level adaptive. Overall, the first operational section of each measure is of average difficulty. The second operational section is administered based on a test taker's overall performance on the first section of that measure. The test design provides a flexible test-taking experience that allows test takers to move freely within any timed section, skipping questions, changing answers, and using their own personal test-taking strategies." Raw score is number correct; equating "accounts for... differences in difficulty among individuals' tests introduced by the section-level adaption."

DOK 2: Confirms the policy is current, not merely a 2011 launch feature.

### E3. NCLEX. NCSBN, *NCLEX Examination Candidate Bulletin* (October 2025 and April 2026 editions). `[read]`
https://ncsbn.org/public-files/NCLEX_Bulletin_English_October_%202025.pdf

DOK 1: NCLEX uses item-level CAT. "Every time the candidate answers an item, the computer re-estimates the candidate's ability by considering all the responses given so far." Items are selected so the candidate "should have around a 50% chance of answering correctly." The bulletins describe the adaptive mechanism, content balancing, and exposure constraints but contain no explicit statement in the text I searched about prohibiting review or answer change.

DOK 2: NCLEX remains item-level adaptive and does not offer review, but I could not locate an official NCSBN sentence stating the prohibition or its rationale. The widely repeated explanation is an inference from the algorithm, not a cited policy statement.

### E4. GMAT Focus Edition. GMAC. `[read]` for mba.com and GMAC pages; `[secondary]` for mechanics as described by prep publishers

So "item-level CAT cannot permit review" is false as an operational generalisation. The anxiety rationale is a sponsor marketing claim, not an empirical finding.

## Verdict

**(a) Do test-takers dislike or object to being unable to review and change previous answers?** Yes, and this is the best-supported of the three sub-questions, though the evidence is narrower than its citation frequency implies. Several independent primary studies find a preference for review: Vispoel (1993) reports unfavourable attitudes specifically about the exclusion of review and skip options in a randomised comparison; Vispoel (2000, fixed-item) reports examinees in both review and no-review conditions "strongly desired review opportunities," more so among higher-test-anxiety examinees; Vispoel (1998) reports "most examinees desired review"; Vispoel, Hendrickson & Bleiler (2000) report nearly all examinees found block review adequate (98.4% per Han); and Revuelta, Ximenez & Olea (2003), n = 557, found examinees in review conditions rated the possibility of review as more important than those without it. The qualifications matter. This is a stated-preference literature, almost all of it from low- or medium-stakes laboratory vocabulary tests with college volunteers between 1993 and 2003. The preference is not specific to adaptive testing, since it appears just as strongly on fixed-item computerized tests. And I found no study measuring spontaneous complaints from operational high-stakes examinees. The colloquial claim "users complain heavily" overstates what was measured, which is survey-rated preference and attitude, not complaint volume or intensity.

**(b) Does allowing review harm measurement or enable score inflation through strategic answer changing?** It depends entirely on the review design, and the real-world threat is far smaller than the theoretical one. Under unrestricted end-of-test review with a coached worst-case population the damage is severe: Stocking's simulated reliability fell from .927 with no review to .798 with full end-of-test review, with score inflation exceeding 60 scaled score points at the top percentiles; Cui et al. found correlation dropping from 0.98 to 0.83 and CSEM from 0.21 to 2.75. But when review is confined to blocks so that revised answers feed back into item selection, the damage essentially disappears: Stocking's reliability was .921 with 7 blocks and .915 with 4 against a .927 baseline, and Cui et al.'s six-block condition matched the no-review baseline on every statistic. Meanwhile the threat has never materialised in live testing. Lunz et al. found 2% of responses altered, 1% efficiency loss recoverable by half an item, and no pass/fail reversals beyond three borderline cases; Stone & Lunz found 94% of decisions unchanged; Vispoel et al. (1999) found the Wainer strategy sometimes deflated rather than inflated scores; and Vispoel et al. (2002) found examinees explicitly taught gaming strategies scored lower than those who were not. The genuine, well-documented cost of unrestricted review is not inflation but time: 35%, 41%, and 52% increases in three separate studies.

**(c) Does allowing review affect test anxiety, perceived fairness, or test-taker attitudes?** Attitudes yes, anxiety unresolved, fairness essentially unmeasured. The attitudinal effect is consistent across studies. The anxiety effect is contested by two studies from overlapping author teams: Olea et al. (2000), n = 182, found state anxiety fell in the review condition and rose in the no-review condition (p = 0.012), while Revuelta, Ximenez & Olea (2003), n = 557 with a fuller design, found "no psychological effects on anxiety." Vispoel (1998) likewise found no trustworthy evidence that test anxiety moderated review effects on ability estimates or measurement error. Vispoel (2000) found higher-test-anxiety examinees desired review more, which is a preference finding, not an anxiety-reduction finding. On perceived fairness, the frequently repeated statement that examinees perceive a test as fairer when review is allowed traces to a secondary attribution in Olea et al. (2000) pointing at Vispoel (1993), whose abstract does not use fairness language.

**Rating of the claim "test-takers object to being unable to review answers in adaptive tests": weakly-to-moderately evidenced.** Most precisely, it is well evidenced as a stated preference and weakly evidenced as a complaint. It is not unsupported: there are randomised primary studies reporting it, and senior practitioners treat it as settled (Wise & Kingsbury, 2000: examinees "have consistently reported dissatisfaction with the absence of item review," with four citations). But the base is thin, old, laboratory-based, and dominated by one research group; the strongest quantitative figures reach us through secondary description; and no operational-scale complaint data exists that I could find.

**Implication for the MST-versus-CAT design argument.** The premise "item-level CAT does not permit within-block answer review" is false as stated in 2026: the GMAT Focus Edition is question-adaptive and permits three answer changes per section, and Smarter Balanced is item-level adaptive and permits review and change within a segment. The defensible version is that MST makes review architecturally free, whereas item-level CAT must buy it with a restricted-revision scheme whose measurement cost rises with permissiveness. To cite examinee preference, the honest citations are Vispoel, Hendrickson & Bleiler (2000) and Revuelta et al. (2003), with the caveat that these are stated preferences from low-stakes college samples. To cite the measurement argument, Stocking (1997) and Cui et al. (2018) are far stronger and support the block-review design directly. For the GRE precedent, the accurate statement is that ETS lists within-section review among the test-taker-friendly advantages of MST, not that ETS adopted MST because of review complaints.

## What I could not find or verify

1. **Any operational complaint data.** No survey, help-desk log, candidate-satisfaction report, or post-administration questionnaire from a real high-stakes adaptive programme (GRE CAT pre-2011, NCLEX, GMAT pre-2023, ASVAB) quantifying how often or how strongly examinees complained about the absence of review. Every quantitative attitudinal figure here comes from laboratory vocabulary tests with college volunteers. The Wikipedia CAT article asserts "Test-takers frequently complain about the inability to review," uncited; it should not be used as support.

2. **Sample sizes for four key Vispoel studies.** Vispoel (1998), Vispoel (2000, fixed-item), Vispoel, Hendrickson & Bleiler (2000), and Vispoel et al. (1999) are paywalled; I have abstracts only, and none states an N. The 41%, 35%, 5.23%, 3.63%, 66.5%, 45%, 2.92:1, 2.55:1, 2.25:1, and 2.44:1 figures are reliable as reported values but uncheckable against method sections.

3. **The 98.4% figure**, the most-quoted attitudinal number here, reaches me only through Han's (2011) description of Vispoel, Hendrickson & Bleiler (2000). That paper's own abstract says only "nearly all examinees." Treat 98.4% as secondary.

4. **Bowles & Pommerich (2001).** Unpublished NCME conference paper, not retrievable. Everything attributed to it here is a one-line secondary description from Cui et al. (2018). No figures, sample size, or method available.

5. **Kingsbury (1996)**, origin of the difficulty-judgment gaming strategy. Not retrieved; known only through Cui et al. (2018) and Han (2011).

6. **Vispoel, Wang, de la Torre, Bleiler & Dings (1992); Baghi, Ferrara & Gabrys (1992); Legg & Buhr (1992).** Three of the four citations Wise & Kingsbury rely on for the "consistently reported dissatisfaction" claim. All are 1992 conference papers or reports, none retrievable. The claim's evidentiary base is thinner than the citation string suggests.

7. **Stone & Lunz (1994) full text.** Abstract only. The repeated "measurement error increased by approximately 0.0025" figure comes from a dissertation's description, with no units given; unverified, and I would not repeat it.

8. **An explicit NCSBN statement prohibiting review, with rationale.** The NCLEX bulletins describe the adaptive algorithm but I found no sentence stating that candidates may not return to previous items or explaining why. The prohibition is real; the official rationale is not documented in what I retrieved.

9. **Whether ETS ever cited examinee complaints as a reason for the GRE MST move.** I found no ETS document saying examinee objections to no-review drove the design change. I did not retrieve Robin & Steffen (Compendium Chapter 3.3), named as the primary source for the MST decision, so a review-related rationale could appear there.

10. **Any effect size for review on test anxiety.** Olea et al. report group means and a p-value but no standardised effect size; Revuelta et al. report a null. No meta-analysis of review and anxiety in adaptive testing exists that I could locate.

11. **Any study of item review in a diagnostic or low-stakes practice adaptive test**, which is the actual use case here. The entire psychometric-risk literature assumes a high-stakes admissions or licensure setting where score inflation matters to a third party.

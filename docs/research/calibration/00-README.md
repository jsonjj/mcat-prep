# Item difficulty calibration: synthesis and recommended plan

**Entry point to a five-lane research review completed 2026-07-28.** Read this file alone if you read nothing else. Lane files are linked from each section and are long; this document carries the numbers you need to make decisions.

**Evidence tiers, used identically in every lane.** **[A]** strong direct empirical evidence or authoritative consensus · **[B]** reasonable evidence with real limits (single study, simulation-only, narrow generalisability, or vendor self-report) · **[C]** weak, indirect, single-source, abstract-only, or a reviewer's own derivation. Tiers are preserved inline on load-bearing claims. `## Evidence quality` at the end says where the review is soft.

**Context.** 2,108 four-option standalone MCAT-style items, tagged with knowledge component (422), content cluster (15), AAMC Foundational Concept (10), reasoning operation, DOK 1–4, lesson id. **Zero response data.** Difficulty currently derives from DOK level. Three consumers: the `MST_140_RECOMMENDED` diagnostic (140 items, 7 stages × 20, 3-way difficulty routing, reports a ranked top-2 weakest concepts across the 10 Foundational Concepts), adaptive practice selection, and an IRT slip term inside a teammate-owned BKT mastery model.

---

## The short version

There is no response data, so there is no calibration — only a prior, and a weak one: DOK explains **1–5%** of item-difficulty variance **[A/B]**. Nothing external fixes that. No difficulty scale can be bought, licensed, or linked to this bank **[A]**, and no cold-start predictor is close to sufficient — the best published result on USMLE-style items explains **≈18%** of variance **[A]**.

This matters less than it sounds, for one specific reason. The product needs only `b`, the most recoverable parameter, and reports only an ordinal shortlist, the most parameter-robust output in psychometrics. Perfect parameters → N = 500 costs **5–10%** in θ RMSE and **.001–.004** in rank correlation **[A]**; parameters with roughly *zero* correlation to truth cost **20–51%**, not 300% **[A]**. And the binding constraint is not calibration: it is **14 items per bucket**, where per-item difficulty error is ≈**6%** of bucket-level error variance and sampling error ≈**94%**.

So: do not buy a full-bank calibration — **$213k** and **13%** of Prolific's entire 90-day-active US pool at 250 responses/item **[A/B]**. Do ship the first cohort **non-adaptive and spiralled**: free, independently supported by three lanes, and impossible to retrofit. Then buy a bounded **$11k–$31k** spine, then FIPC the tail against it.

## What each lane found

### Lane 01 — Calibration methods and sample size

[`01-calibration-methods-and-sample-size.md`](./01-calibration-methods-and-sample-size.md)

Sample-size requirements are model-driven and order-of-magnitude separated: **~100** responses/item to rank-order items classically **[B]**; **~100** (95%) / **~150** (99%) / **~250** ("definitive/high-stakes") for Rasch `b` within ±0.5 logits **[C — Linacre 1994, a newsletter]**; **~500** for 2PL **[B]**; **~1,000+** for 3PL **[B]**. The methodological consensus is that these rules of thumb are contested for legitimate reasons and that a design-specific Monte Carlo beats adopting any of them **[A]**. Reversed proportion-correct correlates **.999** with Rasch `b` and **.90–.95** with 2PL/3PL `b` in a fixed-form representative sample, falling to **.803–.884** under range restriction **[A]** — but under adaptive delivery raw `p` is *structurally* confounded, because it estimates the ability density of whoever got routed to the item, and the ordering can invert **[A]**. Recalibrating from post-operational adaptive data inflates difficulty RMSE to **0.28–0.43** vs **0.064–0.077** on complete data, and discrimination RMSE to **0.45–0.68** vs **0.044–0.048**; two of ten replications produced catastrophic reversals (51 items with negative discriminations, 441 examinees underestimated by >2 SD) **[B]**. Under greedy selection with a ≥10-response threshold, **two-thirds of a 500-item pool** never accumulated enough data to calibrate **[B]**. Fixed-parameter calibration (FIPC) buys roughly a **2×** sample-size discount **[B]**. DOK is a weak difficulty prior across three independent datasets (**r ≈ 0.10–0.25**, slope **0.12 logits per level**, so **~0.36 logits** across DOK 1→4 against a pool difficulty SD near 1 logit) and a *better discrimination* prior (**ρ = 0.25, p = .006**) **[B]**. Volume arithmetic: 250 responses on all 2,108 items ≈ **3,764** completed diagnostics, but 250 responses/item within a single ~380-item MST panel needs only **~679** test takers under uniform exposure. Calibrating the panel is achievable; calibrating the bank is not.

### Lane 02 — Cold-start difficulty prediction

[`02-cold-start-difficulty-prediction.md`](./02-cold-start-difficulty-prediction.md)

No cold-start method produces estimates good enough to substitute for calibration. On the only public benchmark of USMLE-style MCQs (BEA 2024, 667 items) the winner scored RMSE **0.299** against a predict-the-mean baseline of **0.311** — about **7.6%** of variance; the best published result since is RMSE **0.2817** ≈ **18%** of variance, r ≈ 0.42 **[A]**. Text-only routes are worse than useless: hand-engineered linguistic features gave **0.3147** and prompting GPT-4 directly gave **0.3556**, both worse than the mean **[A]**. Asking LLMs to rate difficulty is near-useless on this item type — mean Spearman **0.13** across 21 models, best **0.36** — and the mechanism is diagnosed: **75.6%** of USMLE items are solved by 90% of models and **70.4%** of the hardest human tertile is trivial for them **[A]**. Fitting IRT to model *correctness* gives ρ = **0.134** **[B]**; only the "soft" version (token log-probabilities, ~5% of variance alone) is worth prototyping. A pooled expert panel is the best human option (**r = 0.72–0.83** pooled vs **0.23–0.32** individual **[A]**; but only **r = 0.39–0.62** once converted to Rasch `b` **[B]**), and pairwise comparison beats absolute rating consistently and by a large margin (Spearman 0.486 → **0.730** on primary maths; 18.3 → **40.4** on reading **[B]**). Lane 02's top-value route was **item-family inheritance** — trivial effort, parent-clone r = 0.77–0.93 in simulation **[B]** — with the caveat that 21 of 46 real medical-MCQ families spread ≥0.15 in p-value and the worst reached 1.25 logits **[B]**. **That route is unavailable here; see finding 1 below.** The realistic destination is a hierarchical Bayesian model over the existing KC/cluster/DOK structure, which recovers accurate parameters at **N = 50–100** **[B]**, plus k-NN over item embeddings the moment any items are calibrated (Pearson **0.596**, beat a fine-tuned transformer **[B]**). Routing itself tolerates weak estimates: θ recovery stays **>0.97** with ≥20 adaptive items even with implausibly large within-family variance **[B]**.

### Lane 03 — Acquiring calibration data

[`03-acquiring-calibration-data.md`](./03-acquiring-calibration-data.md)

Full-bank paid calibration is a feasibility problem, not a budget problem. At 250 responses/item, 2,108 items needs **527,000** item-responses ≈ **12,400** participant-hours ≈ **17,600** participants ≈ **$213,000** on Prolific at the recommended $12/hr — **13%** of the entire 90-day-active US pool before any screener; at 500 responses/item, **$427,000** and **26%** **[A/B]**. The recommendation is to buy the *scale*, not the *bank*: a rigorously calibrated **spine** at high responses-per-item, with everything else FIPC'd against it. **≈$11k** for a 60-item routing spine at 400 responses each, **≈$31k** for a 150-item full spine at 400, **≈$21k** at 250 **[B — cost models with stated assumptions]**. Use BIB/block overlap (2,108 factors cleanly as **124 blocks × 17 items**; cyclic booklets link every item with zero anchor overhead; a 6-item spine anchor costs only **1.18×** inflation, versus a dedicated 20-item anchor block that wastes **54%** of spend and is **2.2× worse** for the same responses-per-item) **[B]**. Paid crowdworkers are a poor proxy and the bias is the size of your routing resolution: motivated examinees outperform unmotivated by **0.59 SD [A]**; rapid guessing occurs in **28.3%** of examinees and **6.89%** of responses **[A]** and biases difficulty upward by **+0.30 to +0.55 logits [B]**; meanwhile ~**9%** of Prolific participants show AI-assisted responding **[B]** and ChatGPT-4.0 scored **522 / 99th percentile / 90.87%** on an official AAMC full-length **[B, abstract only]**, biasing hard items *downward*. Your 3-way strata are only **~0.97 logits** apart, so those distortions are **30–60%** of the gap between adjacent strata — and because they push in opposite directions they are mutually camouflaging, so never validate a calibration by inspecting its marginal difficulty distribution. **MTurk closed to new customers on 2026-07-30 [A]**; Prolific and CloudResearch Connect are the live options, Connect ~20–25% cheaper. The public landing page is a validation instrument, not a calibration instrument (**~10 years** to reach 250 responses/item; 12 items yields SE(θ) ≈ **0.58 logits**, which is poor for a person and non-zero for an item, and requires only a server-minted `response_set_id` — no login) **[B]**. Organic-only is a **~98-week** path for the ~1,968-item tail at 500 weekly-active learners × 40 items/week × a 25% seeding slot **[B/C — engagement inputs are guesses]**. The single largest free win is the one that expires: **launch non-adaptive**.

### Lane 04 — Cost of miscalibration

[`04-cost-of-miscalibration.md`](./04-cost-of-miscalibration.md)

Difficulty is the easy parameter and the only one needed. `r(b̂, b) = .99` at N = 500 with RMSE(b) ≈ **0.15–0.17 logits**, versus `r = .73` for discrimination and `.48` for guessing at the same N **[A]**. Almost every dramatic number in the miscalibration literature traces to `a` appearing *squared* in Fisher information, while `b` appears only inside `P·Q`, which is flat to within **.04** over p ∈ [.30, .70] **[A, analytic]**. Perfect parameters → N = 500 costs **+5%** (15 items) to **+9%** (40 items) in θ RMSE under 2PL **[A]**; parameters with ~zero correlation to truth cost **+20%** (summed scoring) to **+51%** (pattern scoring) **[A]**. The damage is *epistemic*, not decisional: test information is overestimated by **25–40%** at N = 400–500 **[A]** — standard errors too small by up to 40% — while RMSE(θ) is statistically indistinguishable across calibration sample sizes over the central θ range **[A]**. Ordinal output is protected: θ correlates **r ≥ .978** across 1PL/2PL/3PL fits to the same data even where the 3PL fits significantly better **[A]**. MST routing is already noisy for reasons unrelated to calibration — **12.4–29.1%** of examinees are misrouted with *perfectly known* parameters, at a cost of only **+0.03** RMSE(θ) **[A]**. BKT is nearly insensitive to the slip values it consumes: an infinite family of parameter sets makes identical predictions **[A]**, and per-item slip (KT-IDEM) bought **+0.030 AUC** at best and failed one replication **[A/B]**. The lane's headline: the binding constraint is **14 items per bucket**. Subscores need **≥20 items** and disattenuated inter-subscore correlation **<.85** to add value over the total score; 10-item subscores had *none* even at extreme low correlations **[A]**. Attribute classification accuracy runs **0.80 / 0.90 / 0.95** at 5 / 10 / 15 items per attribute **[A]**. Sampling SE for a 14-item bucket is ≈**0.53 logits**; a poor per-item difficulty RMSE of 0.5 logits contributes ≈**0.13 logits** after averaging — about **6%** of total error variance **[C — reviewer's derivation]**. Where it genuinely breaks is *systematic* error: a DOK→difficulty mapping wrong for a whole content cluster does not average out, and adaptive algorithms convert even *unbiased* item-parameter error into systematic θ bias reaching up to **40%** of the calibration-sample SD **[A/B]**. Fix `c` near 1/k and do not fit a 3PL — adopting the 3PL at N = 500 cost **+34%** RMSE(θ) versus the 2PL, four times what the calibration error cost **[A]**.

### Lane 05 — External sources and licensing

[`05-external-sources-and-licensing.md`](./05-external-sources-and-licensing.md)

Clear negative finding: **no usable external difficulty scale exists for this bank**, and the reason is structural rather than a market gap. AAMC registers MCAT forms under the Copyright Office "secure test" regulations (**37 C.F.R. § 202.20**), whose entire purpose is protection without public deposit **[A]**; percentile tables are public, item parameters are not and will not be. The bulk-purchase terms for Official Prep (updated 2026-03-18) self-declare that they govern anyone "developing test prep products" and prohibit derivative works "(such as solutions, AI/LLM modeling)" **[A]**. The one dataset with real empirical difficulty on USMLE-style items is the **BEA 2024 / NBME set: 667 retired items**, whose "difficulty" is a linearly transformed proportion-correct from med students (~300+ examinees per item), **not** an IRT `b`; it is no longer downloadable and access requires application to NBME's Office of Research Strategy **[A]**. Genuine published per-item IRT parameters exist, free and commercially usable, only for **NAEP** — public domain, `a`/`b`/`c`/`d` by subject/year/grade/block/item, plus 2,000–3,000+ released items with percent-correct, and a CRAN package (`NAEPirtparams`) for 1990–2015 **[A]**. It is worthless as difficulty for *these* items and valuable for one thing: pressure-testing the 140-item MST design against realistic parameter distributions instead of invented uniform draws. Everything else fails a licence filter — TIMSS is explicitly non-commercial **[A]**; Eedi/NeurIPS 2020 is CC BY-NC-ND, EdNet/Riiid is CC BY-NC, Junyi states "any form of commercial usage is not allowed" **[A]**; MedMCQA's 193,155 questions have no difficulty field at all and permissive repo licensing does not launder scraped item provenance **[A/B]**. "Calibration-as-a-service" is labour, not data: FastTest advertises from **$6,750/year [A]**, competing against free R packages, and engaging a consultancy shortens the path by zero responses. The structural summary in one sentence: **legally usable anchors are construct-irrelevant, and construct-relevant anchors are legally unusable.** IRB is not legally required for privately funded product work (**45 C.F.R. § 46.101(a)**) **[A]** but Student Doctor Network requires an approval letter in the recruitment post and any university partnership routes through its own IRB **[A]** — so IRB is the access key to the cheap channels and sits on the critical path. The cheapest calibration purchase available is the team's own launch: a learner using the diagnostic for their own benefit is intrinsically motivated, which dissolves the 0.30–0.55 logit unmotivated-sample bias for free.

### Where the lanes disagree, and how it reconciles

**Proportion-correct.** Lane 01 says raw proportion-correct is mathematically confounded and must not be used; lane 04 says it correlates **r = .90** with criterion IRT difficulty and is a sufficient statistic for Rasch difficulty. Both are correct and the pivot is the delivery design: the **.90** holds under non-adaptive administration, and lane 01's confound is specific to adaptive routing, where a well-targeted test drives every item toward p ≈ 0.5 regardless of true difficulty. **Random or spiralled delivery is the single condition that makes the cheap statistic valid** — which is a third independent argument for the stage-0 decision below.

**How much to spend.** Lane 04 argues a large up-front calibration study is not justified by the measurement gains for these three uses, and that earning the data beats buying it. Lane 03 recommends spending **$11k–$31k**. These are not in conflict: both reject the full-bank study outright, and lane 03's spine buys something lane 04 never priced — an *absolute scale* with an origin and unit, which is what FIPC and any routing threshold require. Lane 04's point is about the marginal precision of the shortlist; lane 03's is about the existence of the scale.

**Which model.** Lane 04 says use 1PL or a constrained 2PL and never a 3PL. Lane 01 adds the counter-case: a program whose pool was already well calibrated under 2PL found the 1PL made its conversion tables *worse* **[B]**. Both hold — the right model depends on whether the pool is already well-placed, and this one is not. Start Rasch or fixed-slope, migrate later.

## The recommended plan

Staged by cost. Stage 0 is free and part of it expires; Stage 1 is cheap; Stage 2 is the only cash outlay; Stage 3 runs forever. The stages fail independently, which is the point — if premed recruitment stalls you still have Stage 0's instrumentation, and if the spine turns out biased the pinned reference form detects it and FIPC lets you re-anchor without redoing anything downstream.

### Stage 0 — Free, and some of it expires (now, ~$0, weeks of engineering)

**1. Ship the first cohort non-adaptive and spiralled. This is the centrepiece.** Deliver the diagnostic with random or BIB-spiralled item assignment before switching adaptivity on. Three lanes support it independently: it makes the response matrix missing-at-random *by design*, which is exactly what concurrent IRT calibration needs (lane 03); it makes plain proportion-correct an unbiased difficulty estimate, restoring lane 04's `r = .90` (lanes 01 and 04); and it spreads exposure evenly instead of starving the tail, which is the failure that left **two-thirds of a 500-item pool** uncalibratable under greedy selection (lane 01). The concrete design is 124 blocks × 17 items with cyclic booklets, giving zero anchor overhead. Cost: the engineering to add a delivery mode. Buys: a **4–6× speedup** on tail calibration, a defensible missing-data argument, and valid classical statistics from day one. **It cannot be retrofitted — once a cohort is routed adaptively, that data is permanently confounded**, and the adaptivity it bought was running on DOK-derived parameters that explain 1–5% of difficulty variance anyway. An adaptive product that launches adaptively before it is calibrated has permanently spent its cheapest calibration opportunity to deliver a benefit it could not actually provide.

**2. Instrument the event log before any traffic arrives.** A server-minted `response_set_id` per sitting (no login, no persistent cookie needed); an **item version hash** — the only field that is *retroactively poisonous* if missing, because a typo fix silently pools two different items' data and you cannot afterwards tell which; millisecond latencies, without which you cannot detect the rapid guessing that biases difficulty by +0.30 to +0.55 logits; **selection-rule provenance**, whose absence makes a year of otherwise-complete data uninterpretable, because missing-not-at-random data can only be corrected if the mechanism is known; copy-paste and tab-blur telemetry, the only LLM-assistance signal available for multiple choice; consent language; and a **pinned reference form** for drift detection.

**3. Fix the three code-level mismatches** in `## Code and product implications` below: raise `MIN_ATTEMPTS_FOR_B`, make the results UI ordinal, and decide the item-family question. All are small changes and all are cheaper now than later.

**4. Simulate the MST against NAEP's published parameters.** Free, public domain, commercially usable, available as a CRAN data frame. This is the one "borrow instead of build" win in the whole review, and it replaces invented uniform parameter draws — which flatter the design — with realistic ones. While you are there, simulate the 7-stage misrouting rate: the only published figure (**15%**) comes from a 42-item three-stage design and does not transfer.

**5. Send the two emails.** `ORS@nbme.org` about the BEA 2024 data use agreement, and a Prolific eligible-count query for the premed filter set (about an hour of work). Both gate later decisions; see `## Open questions`.

**6. Note what has already expired.** MTurk closed to new customers on **2026-07-30 [A]**. This is now moot rather than urgent — Prolific and CloudResearch Connect are the live options and Connect is ~20–25% cheaper — but if MTurk was in anyone's plan, it is gone.

### Stage 1 — Cheap (2–8 weeks, hundreds to low thousands of dollars)

**1. Check the DOK→difficulty mapping for cluster-level bias. This is the highest-value data collection in the review per dollar.** A few hundred responses spread across DOK × cluster cells is enough. The reason it matters: every reassuring number in lane 04 depends on per-item difficulty errors being *independent* so they average out at √n, and a DOK-derived prior produces errors that are **correlated within DOK level and within content cluster** — precisely the structure that survives averaging. **Test the mapping, not the items.** A related cheap invalidation: if DOK-4 items are not empirically harder than DOK-1 items, that finding should reshape the roadmap more than any single calibration number would.

**2. Run a rotating sacrificial set on the public landing page.** Classical p-values plus distractor diagnostics in weeks rather than the ~10 years a full landing-page calibration would take. Every response is an exposure, so budget exposure as a resource: designate which items you are willing to burn, and never let a diagnostic item touch an unauthenticated endpoint.

**3. Elicit pairwise SME comparisons on a sampled subset.** This is the only cold-start method in the review with both a defensible accuracy claim and a tractable cost at this bank size. Absolute Angoff-style rating reverts to the mean and yields only **r = 0.39–0.62** against Rasch `b`; pairwise comparison plus Bradley–Terry preserves spread and consistently beats it. **5 raters × 3 comparisons per item** approximates 100 test-takers, which is ~30,000 judgments for the whole bank or a few thousand for a representative subset. Stratify by predicted rank quantile, not by predicted value — range compression is the specific failure that breaks a difficulty-routing diagnostic, and every cold-start method exhibits it.

**4. Start Elo immediately for practice selection.** Day-one provisional difficulties, real-time updates at negligible cost, performance close to IRT under both random and adaptive selection **[B]**. It has no origin and unit, so never use Elo ratings directly as MST cut-points; link the two by putting spine items in the Elo stream.

**5. Buy a commercial IRB review, and schedule it early.** Not legally required, but it is the access key to the cheap recruitment channels (SDN requires the letter in the post; university partnerships route through their own IRB), so it sits on the critical path. Fee and turnaround are unverified.

**6. Open university postbac and premed program partnerships.** Free institutional licence plus aggregate program reporting, in exchange for de-identified responses under a DUA. This is the best cheap sample available and the reason is motivation, not access: a program director can assign the diagnostic as coursework, which manufactures the one property money cannot buy. AMSA's Premedical Research Project (national IRB via University of Vermont, 15+ institutions, 1,000+ participants) is the working precedent.

### Stage 2 — Expensive (the only cash outlay; 6–12 weeks)

**Buy a spine, in two tranches, from real premeds rather than generic crowdworkers.**

| Tranche | What | Cost | Time | What it buys |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2a | 60-item routing spine at R = 400 | **~$11k** Prolific / **~$15k** direct premed | ~2–4 weeks | The FIPC anchor set, and the module everyone sees |
| 2b | Extend to a 150-item full spine spanning all 15 content clusters at R = 400 | **+$20k** / **+$27k** | ~4–8 weeks | Cluster-wide coverage of the scale; enables FIPC of the whole bank |
| — | Total at R = 400 | **≈$31k–$43k** | ~2–3 months | An absolute difficulty scale with an origin and a unit |
| — | Total if R = 250 is accepted | **≈$21k–$28k** | — | Same scale, wider intervals |

Spend on the **routing module first** because four independent arguments converge on the same 20–60 items: it is administered to everyone so it is the only MAR-clean self-linking part of the design; it is the natural FIPC anchor and Guo et al. specifically recommend fixing routing-block items only; a misroute there costs the most because six subsequent stages inherit it; and its width and length most strongly determine misrouting rates. That convergence is the strongest structural finding in lane 03.

Pay premeds rather than crowdworkers because the documented biases — **0.30–0.55 logits** from rapid guessing, ~**0.6 logits** of scale displacement from the motivation gap — are **30–60%** of the **~0.97-logit** spacing between your adjacent routing strata. That is the argument that justifies $25/hr over $12/hr in product terms rather than methodological ones.

**Explicitly do not buy:** the full-bank study (**$213k**, 17,600 participants, **13%** of Prolific's US pool at R = 250; **$427k** and **26%** at R = 500 — a recruitment impossibility, not an expensive option); calibration-as-a-service expecting to receive data (**$6,750/year** buys software competing against free R packages, and consultancies sell labour applied to responses you must still collect); or a general-population panel screened for premeds (no prescreener exists, Prolific cannot forecast the screen-out rate, and the participants who pass are in exactly the unmotivated condition that biases difficulty by 0.30–0.55 logits — the screening cost and the bias compound rather than trade off).

### Stage 3 — Ongoing, ~$0 (launch onward, forever)

**1. FIPC the tail against the fixed spine.** `mirt::fixedCalib` is mature and was the engine in ETS's 2024 small-sample MST study. Fix routing-block items only. This is the highest-return statistical decision available and it is a software choice, not a purchase: a **2×** effective sample-size multiplier, larger than any plausible platform saving. It also removes the error-prone manual Stocking-Lord linking step entirely. Anchor *quality and stability* deserve the attention, not anchor count — going from 10 to 40 anchors helps only slightly **[C]**.

**2. Keep a mandatory random seeding slot in every practice session, permanently.** Adaptive delivery and item calibration are structurally opposed: greedy selection deliberately avoids items whose parameters are uncertain, which are exactly the items you need data on. "R = 250 on average" can be satisfied while a third of the bank has zero exposures. Simple random pretest selection is competitive with sophisticated online-calibration methods **[B]**, which is licence not to over-engineer. Add Sympson-Hetter or randomesque exposure control. Retain a never-adaptively-selected subset as a permanent drift control group.

**3. Run a promotion ladder with honest thresholds.** **100** responses → provisional (Elo + p-value; practice selection only, never a routing decision, never two provisional items in the same stage). **250** → calibrated (eligible for MST target modules). **500** → discrimination trusted. This makes uncertainty an actionable property of each item that the delivery engine can reason about, rather than a caveat in a document.

**4. Monitor drift rather than recalibrating on a cadence.** No source in the review gave a recalibration interval; the licensure-program answer is effectively "essentially never, monitor instead." An annual job flagging any item whose `b` moved more than **0.5 logits** is the whole mitigation. Single-cycle drift is cheap (θ bias ≤ **0.14**) but uncorrected multi-year drift reached θ bias **−0.52**, and neither a bigger bank nor a bigger sample mitigates it **[A/B]**. Drift risk is structurally higher here than in the licensure programs the literature studies, because prep users deliberately re-encounter, share, and discuss items.

**5. Upgrade the prior as data arrives.** k-NN over item embeddings plus metadata the moment any items are calibrated (best accuracy-per-effort in lane 02's table). Then a hierarchical Bayesian model over the KC → cluster → FC structure, which is the one technique in the literature that changes the sample-size arithmetic rather than optimising within it. Test lane 01's inverted DOK hypothesis on your own data — that DOK predicts *discrimination* better than difficulty — because it is cheap to test and would change item-selection policy if true.

## Open questions that gate decisions

Ordered by value-per-hour. The first two are cheap and gate real money.

1. **How many premed-eligible US participants does Prolific actually have?** The **~$31k** calibrated-spine plan depends on it and it is unverified. Prolific has **no premed prescreener** and states it does not store the attribute and therefore **cannot estimate** how many participants must be screened to reach a target sample. Total US 90-day-active pool is ~134,000 (vendor self-report). **How to resolve, ~1 hour:** create a workspace and call the eligible-count endpoint with your filter set (it returns 0 when fewer than 25 qualify), or read the live count in the audience section of study setup; then apply the 40–50% response-rate rule of thumb. If the number is small, Stage 2 shifts to direct premed recruitment and university partnerships, and the budget moves from ~$31k to ~$43k.
2. **Can a commercial entity obtain the BEA 2024 / NBME 667-item set, and could a model trained on it ship in a commercial product?** Lane 05 calls this the single highest-value open question in that review. **How to resolve: one email to `ORS@nbme.org`.** Note that even a yes yields 667 transformed p-values from a med-student population, not `b`-parameters for premed items — so this bounds an upside, it does not unblock the plan.
3. **What is the median response time for *your* items?** Lane 03 derived 75 s/item by discounting AAMC's operational **97.8 s/item**. It is a judgement, and it scales **every** dollar figure linearly — a 20% error here is a 20% error in the budget. **How to resolve:** time 20 items with 5 colleagues before committing to a study.
4. **Does the 7-stage design misroute more or less than the published 15%?** That figure is from a 42-item three-stage MST. More stages means more routing decisions; more items per stage means better per-stage precision. **The net effect is genuinely unknown and no study on a 7-stage design was found.** **How to resolve:** simulate it against NAEP parameters — a day's work for your own MST harness, and free.
5. **Does the FIPC "as good as double the sample size" result transfer off Guo et al.'s program?** One paper, one item pool, simulated, and the authors themselves flag program-specificity. It is the load-bearing claim behind the entire spine strategy. **How to resolve:** replicate it on your own simulated pool before committing $31k on the strength of it.
6. **Will authoring populate real item families?** Gates whether lane 02's highest-value cold-start route exists at all, and whether the sacrificial-exposure strategy works. **How to resolve:** a product decision, not research.
7. **Do Prolific/Connect terms permit collecting responses to proprietary test items, and do their authenticity checks work on multiple choice?** All documented LLM detection is free-text-oriented, so it likely provides little protection for a pure MCQ battery — but this was not stated or contradicted anywhere. **How to resolve:** ask Prolific support before running a study.
8. **Real landing-page traffic.** Every landing-page model rests on an invented 2,000 visitors/month. The qualitative conclusion (orders of magnitude short of calibration) survives a 25× correction; the specific month counts do not. **How to resolve:** read your analytics.

## What the research does NOT say

The reassuring conclusion is real but heavily conditional. Do not carry "calibration matters less than we feared" out of this document without the conditions attached.

- **It is conditional on the output staying ordinal.** Every protective result — `r ≥ .978` across model fits, 0.87–0.99 classification agreement, flat RMSE(θ) across calibration sample sizes — is about *ordering*. The moment a score, a percentile, an SE, or a confidence indicator appears in the UI, the **25–40% information overestimation** becomes a live validity problem and no amount of "it's only a shortlist" reasoning applies. The current design's silence on scores is doing real protective work, and `BucketGapChart.tsx` is already breaking that silence.
- **It is conditional on the model staying 1PL or a constrained 2PL.** All the reassuring numbers depend on estimating *fewer* parameters. The 3PL at N = 500 cost **+34%** RMSE(θ) — four times what the calibration error itself cost. `r(ĉ, c) = .48` at N = 500. Fitting `a` and `c` from thin data re-imports the entire body of published damage.
- **It is conditional on not adopting maximum-information item selection, variable-length testing, or SE-based stopping.** Capitalization on chance corrupts a stopping rule directly. A fixed 140-item MST is safe; a "stop when we're confident" version is not.
- **Aggregate robustness does not guarantee any individual learner's shortlist.** In the wrong-parameter condition, mean bias stayed ≈0 while **bias SD rose from .008 to .20**, with individual replications reaching **±0.5–0.7 logits**. Averages being fine is not the same as this learner's top-2 being right. Compounding this: **no study was found that directly simulates the accuracy of a top-k weakest-concept ranking under manipulated item-parameter error.** Every ordinal-robustness claim in lane 04 is *inferred* from θ-rank correlations, binary classification accuracy, and CDM attribute accuracy — none of which is the decision this product makes.
- **Random error averages out; systematic error does not, and a DOK prior is the kind that produces systematic error.** The √n protection assumes independent per-item errors. A DOK→difficulty mapping produces errors correlated within DOK level and within content cluster, which is exactly the structure that survives averaging. Separately, adaptive algorithms convert even *unbiased* item-parameter error into *systematic* θ bias, most pronounced at the ability extremes, reaching up to **40%** of the calibration-sample SD.
- **"Calibration is cheap to earn" is not "calibration is optional."** 100 responses × 2,108 items ≈ 211,000 responses is ordinary product usage rather than a research budget — but only if delivery is non-adaptive or seeded, only if the log is instrumented, and only over a **1–4 year** horizon at realistic engagement. Under greedy adaptive selection, two-thirds of the pool never gets there at all.
- **The DOK evidence is really about DOK 1–3.** All three datasets behind the r ≈ 0.10–0.25 finding collapse to three levels or had almost no DOK 3 and no DOK 4 items. The DOK 3→4 step is unevidenced.
- **No acceptable RMSE(b) threshold exists for the top-2 decision.** Lane 04 deliberately declined to invent one, and none of the lanes supply one. What exists is the error-budget arithmetic showing calibration is a small share of total error at 14 items per bucket — labelled as a reviewer derivation, not a citation.
- **Better calibration does not improve learning outcomes on the available evidence.** A randomised field experiment found no difference in summative scores between difficulty-matched adaptive practice and static practice, with higher-ability students scoring **0.08 σ lower** under adaptive; an RCT (N = 184) found no post-test difference from knowledge-tracing-driven selection. This is not an argument against the design, but it does bound the claims that can be made for it.
- **Nothing here is legal advice.** Lane 05 read publicly posted terms and published opinions. Whether a specific pipeline infringes, or a specific licence permits a specific use, is a question for counsel — and all privacy/consent content in lane 03 came from vendor blog posts rather than primary law.

## Evidence quality

**Where the review is strong.** The parameter-recovery and propagation-to-θ results in lane 04 rest on full-text reads with tables read directly (van der Linden & Glas 2000; Olea et al. 2012; Cheng, Patton & Shao 2015 results tables; McGrath 2019; Yiğiter & Doğan 2023 results tables; Wauters et al. 2011; Beck & Chang 2007; Pardos & Heffernan 2011; Sinharay 2010 / ETS RM-08-18). Lane 02's headline numbers are held-out test results on a fixed public split with a published baseline, which is about as good as this literature gets. Lane 05's licence and terms claims are primary-source reads of the actual terms pages, court opinions, and regulation text, and the platform pricing in lane 03 was read directly on the vendors' pages with observation dates recorded. The claim that `b` is robust, `a` is fragile, and `c` is hopeless is corroborated independently in four sources across two lanes and is analytically grounded rather than empirically incidental.

**Where it rests on abstracts or secondary citations.** Lane 04's most-cited number — **25–40% information overestimation at N = 400** (Hambleton, Jones & Rogers 1993) — was read via van der Linden & Glas, not in the original. **Doebler (2012)**, whose result that unbiased parameter error produces systematic θ bias is one of the more unsettling mechanisms in the review, was read abstract-only, and the "0.3 SD" figure is Fink et al.'s characterisation of it rather than Doebler's own number. Also abstract-only: Chapman, Weiss & Suen 2024 (the **15%** routing-error figure, with no effect sizes obtained), Kim & Moses ("generally minimal", unquantified), Rios et al. 2022 (the **28.3% / 6.89%** rapid-guessing prevalence), the ChatGPT **522 / 90.87%** MCAT result, and Patton et al. 2013. Lane 01's classical sample-size figures are largely secondary: **Thissen & Wainer (1982)** — the ~10,000 and "as large as 1,000,000" 3PL figures — was never read, and the million figure should be treated as illustrative of unbounded SEs rather than a number; **de Ayala (2009)**'s ≥500-persons/≥20-items 2PL guidance was read as a quotation inside Guo et al.; **Lord (1968)**, **Kim (2006)**, and **Jones, Smith & Talley (2006)** were all read second-hand. Lane 02's expert-panel table runs almost entirely through **Mattar (2000)**, a doctoral dissertation.

**Non-peer-reviewed sources doing real work.** **Linacre (1994), *Rasch Measurement Transactions*** supplies the entire Rasch sample-size table (100 / 150 / 250 / 500) in two lanes. It is a newsletter, its derivation is SE arithmetic rather than a recovery study, and it is widely used in practice — which is not the same as validated. Prolific's and CloudResearch's pool sizes and quality figures are vendor self-report with no independent audit, and their LLM-detection figures are free-text-oriented and so may not apply to a multiple-choice battery at all. The `irtsim` CRAN vignette, `rasch.org`, and a small number of commercial and secondary synthesis pages appear in lane 01; a commercial MCAT-prep blog was excluded from all factual claims, and one unrefereed tutorial site was excluded entirely from lane 04.

**Reviewer computations, not citations.** The **ρ = 0.101** DOK/difficulty figure is computed from Webb Alignment's published 298-item cross-tab and is not a number they report. The **7.6%** and **18%** variance-explained figures for BEA 2024 are derived from published RMSEs. The **6% / 94%** error budget, the **~3%** and **~30%** improvement figures, and the ≈**0.53-logit** 14-item bucket SE are all lane 04's own arithmetic. The **~0.97-logit** strata spacing, the **1.18×** and **2.2×** anchor-design comparisons, and every dollar and timeline figure in lane 03 are that lane's own models with stated assumptions — change an input and the number moves.

**Specific citations flagged as needing verification against the originals.** In rough order of how much weight they carry:

1. **Guo et al. (2024/2025)** — the FIPC "as accurate as double the sample size" result. One paper, one program, simulated. It is the load-bearing claim behind the entire spine strategy and a ~$31k decision, and lane 03 is explicit about leaning on it. Replicate on your own simulated pool.
2. **Taube (1997)**, via Mattar — the **r = 0.39–0.62** Angoff-to-Rasch-`b` figures. Load-bearing in lane 02's conclusion that expert panels give rank order only.
3. **Swaminathan, Hambleton, Sireci, Xing & Rizavi (2003)** — judgmental priors for small-sample calibration. Paywalled, abstract only, no sample sizes or effect sizes obtained. Lane 01 calls this its most important gap, because it is the one study that would tell you what a DOK-derived prior is actually worth.
4. **de Ayala (2009) pp. 105, 152** and **Thissen & Wainer (1982)** — the 2PL and 3PL minimum-N figures quoted throughout both method lanes.
5. **Rothe et al.** anchor-proportion and sample-size tables — the **RMSE column units could not be determined** and the values are too large to be logits on a conventional scale. Use the correlation column (**r = 0.42 / 0.63 / 0.82 / 0.97 / 0.98** at n = 25 / 50 / 100 / 500 / 1,000) and treat the RMSEs as relative comparisons only.
6. **Impara & Plake (1998)** and **Kim (2006)** — both secondary throughout.
7. **Table-extraction risk:** Fan (1998), Guo et al. (2024), and Wu et al. (2025) all had tables that extracted imperfectly. Individual cell assignments in the Wu et al. RMSE table carry some risk, and their CAT condition administered 30 items per examinee against the complete condition's full pool, so part of that 4–6× and 10–15× RMSE gap is information volume rather than adaptivity.

**Things looked for and not found.** No published per-item pretest sample size for the MCAT, USMLE/NBME, or AAMC — if a same-domain benchmark is wanted, it does not appear to be public. No authoritative recalibration cadence from any source. No defensible minimum N for anchored FIPC new-item calibration below ~250. No study quantifying the small-sample gain from pooling across item families specifically, nor from content-taxonomy priors as opposed to family or response-time priors — which is a real gap, since the taxonomy is this project's main asset. No empirical DOK-level-versus-`b` relationship anywhere in the literature. And no study simulating top-k weakest-concept ranking accuracy under manipulated calibration error, which is the exact decision this product makes.

# Calibration methods and sample-size requirements for item difficulty

**Scope.** Literature/evidence review on estimating item difficulty from response data: classical vs IRT methods, how many responses per item are actually needed, whether proportion-correct is a defensible proxy (especially under adaptive delivery), estimation machinery and tooling, fixed-parameter/online calibration, the cost of ignoring discrimination and guessing, item parameter drift, and whether a DOK level is a reasonable difficulty prior.

**Evidence tiers used throughout.** **A** = strong direct empirical evidence or authoritative consensus. **B** = reasonable evidence with limits (single good study, simulation-only, or narrow generalisability). **C** = weak, indirect, single-source, non-peer-reviewed, or read only at abstract/secondary level.

**Reading status is marked per source in `## Sources`.** Several load-bearing classical figures (Thissen & Wainer 1982; Lord 1968; de Ayala 2009) were read only as quotations inside other papers. Those are flagged.

---

## Executive answer

- **There is no response data, so there is no difficulty estimate.** Every number below describes what happens *after* responses exist. Any pre-launch difficulty value is a prior, not a calibration, and should be stored and named as such.
- **Sample-size requirements are model-driven and roughly order-of-magnitude separated:** classical p-value ordering ~**100** responses/item (Nevo 1980, Tier B); Rasch/1PL b to ±0.5 logits at 95% confidence ~**100**, at 99% ~**150**, "definitive/high-stakes" ~**250** (Linacre 1994, Tier C — newsletter, derived from SE arithmetic, not a recovery study); 2PL ~**500** with MML + priors + ≥20 items (de Ayala 2009 as quoted by Guo et al. 2024, Tier B); 3PL ~**1,000+** and often much more (Han 2012, Tier B; Thissen & Wainer 1982 reported ≈10,000 via secondary citation, Tier C).
- **Rules of thumb are contested and the contest is legitimate.** Schroeders & Gnambs (2025, *AMPPS*) show published minima range from N=100 (Bayesian with priors) to "even 2,000 is insufficient" (models with guessing/slipping parameters), because requirements depend on model, estimator, test length, θ distribution, and missing-data design. Their recommendation — run a Monte Carlo tuned to your own design rather than adopting a rule of thumb — is the current methodological consensus (Tier A for the "it depends" claim; Tier B for the specific tutorial).
- **Proportion correct is an excellent difficulty proxy under one condition and a bad one otherwise.** In a fixed-form, representative sample, reversed p correlates **.999** with Rasch b and **.90–.95** with 2PL/3PL b (Fan 1998, N=1,000 per sample, 60 math / 48 reading items; Tier A). Under range-restricted samples that drops to **.803–.841** against 2PL b. Fan also found CTT p-values were *at least as invariant across dissimilar samples* as IRT b — a genuinely counterintuitive result that undercuts the usual "IRT is invariant, CTT is not" argument.
- **Under adaptive delivery, raw p is structurally confounded and must not be used.** An item's observed p estimates ∫P(θ)g_j(θ)dθ where g_j is the ability density of *whoever got routed to that item* — not the population. In an MST, hard items are disproportionately shown to strong learners, so their p is inflated toward the easy end and the difficulty ordering can invert. The fix is not a correction factor: it is to fit an IRT model by MML on **all** responses (routing responses included), which is unbiased under MAR because routing depends only on observed responses (Mislevy & Wu 1988/1996; Eggen & Verhelst 2011; Wang et al. 2020; Tier A), and/or to anchor with fixed-parameter calibration, and/or to seed uncalibrated items at random rather than adaptively.
- **Adaptive data recovers difficulty tolerably but wrecks discrimination.** In a simulation of post-operational CAT recalibration (Wu et al. 2025, *EPM*), RMSE for discrimination was **0.45–0.68** under CAT vs **0.044–0.048** under complete data (~10–15×); difficulty RMSE was **0.28–0.43** vs **0.064–0.077** (~4–6×). Two of ten replications produced a catastrophic "reversal" (51 of ~161 items with negative discriminations; 441 examinees underestimated by >2 SD). Mitigation: a lognormal prior on discrimination, and never dropping the routing-stage responses.
- **Fixed-parameter calibration (FIPC) buys roughly a 2× sample-size discount.** In an operational MST program, FIPC of new items at **N=250** was about as accurate as separate-calibration-with-Stocking-Lord scaling at **N=500**, and FIPC at 500 ≈ SL at 1,000; the program's N=1,000 requirement could be relaxed to N>500 (Guo et al. 2024, ETS RR; Tier B, simulation on program-specific pools). This is a discount, not a licence for N=50 — I found no peer-reviewed source supporting stable anchored 2PL item parameters below ~200–250.
- **DOK is a weak difficulty prior — but a better discrimination prior.** Three independent datasets put the DOK/Bloom–difficulty association at **r ≈ 0.10–0.25** (ρ=0.101 computed by me from Webb Alignment's 298-item cross-tab; r=0.23 in Schneider et al. 2026; ρ=0.172, p=.06 ns in Hamamoto Filho et al. 2020), i.e. **1–5% of difficulty variance**, with a regression slope of **0.12 logits per DOK level** — about 0.36 logits across the whole DOK 1→4 range, which is small next to a typical item-pool difficulty SD near 1 logit. Two of the three studies found the taxonomy relates *more* strongly to discrimination (ρ=0.25, p=.006) than to difficulty. Webb's own position is that complexity and difficulty are distinct constructs, and that complex tasks tend to be difficult but difficult tasks are not necessarily complex (Tier A on the conceptual claim).
- **Metadata bundles do better than DOK alone, and still not well.** Item-difficulty-modeling studies that combine blueprint/content codes, item structure, and text features explain **R² ≈ 0.29** on medical MCQ IRT b (1,154 MOCA-Peds items, RMSE ≈ 1.37 logits; Tier B) to **R² ≈ 0.34–0.37** on reading items (Tier B). LLM-feature + gradient-boosting pipelines reach **r ≈ 0.87** on large-scale math/reading pools (Tier B, single recent study).
- **Volume arithmetic (my computation):** 250 responses on each of 2,108 items = 527,000 responses ≈ **3,764 completed 140-item diagnostics**. But a single 7-stage 3-way MST panel is only ~380 items, so 250 responses/item *within one panel* needs ≈ **679 test takers** under uniform exposure — realistically 2–4× that, because routing concentrates exposure in the central modules. Calibrating the panel is achievable early; calibrating all 2,108 items is a multi-year data problem at small scale.

## Findings

### 1. Established methods for estimating item difficulty from response data

**Classical item analysis (CTT).**

- *Difficulty:* the **p-value** (proportion correct; also called item facility because higher = easier). It is the sample mean of a Bernoulli variable, so `SE(p) = sqrt(p(1−p)/n)` exactly. My arithmetic at p=.5: SE = .091 (n=30), .050 (n=100), .035 (n=200), .022 (n=500), .016 (n=1,000). **Tier A** (this is arithmetic, not an empirical claim).
- *Discrimination:* the **point-biserial** correlation between item score and total score (or the biserial, or a high-low group difference index). Standard practice is to remove the item from the total before computing it, since including it spuriously inflates discrimination on short tests (Tier A, textbook consensus; stated in Fan 1998's method section and in standard item-analysis references).
- *Distractor analysis:* option-level p and option-level point-biserials, to find non-functioning or mis-keyed distractors. Directly relevant here because every item already has per-option explanation prose that could be checked against which distractors actually attract responses.
- **What CTT buys:** almost nothing to compute, interpretable units, works at small n, no model to fit or diagnose. **What it requires:** that the sample and the administration design be the same for every item you want to compare, because p and r_pb are *group-dependent*. Hambleton & Jones (1993, NCME *ITEMS* Module 16) state this plainly: higher p-values come from above-average samples, lower from below-average; discrimination is higher in heterogeneous samples and lower in homogeneous ones (**Tier A**, authoritative instructional module, full text read).

**IRT.**

- **1PL / Rasch:** `P(θ) = 1/(1+exp(−(θ−b)))`. One parameter per item. Total score is a sufficient statistic for θ; item and person parameters are separable. **Buys:** difficulty on the same interval scale as ability, small sample requirements, specific objectivity, simple invariance checks. **Requires:** that discriminations really are approximately equal — and in real MCQ data they often are not. Fan (1998) found ~30% of items misfit the 1PL on two statewide tests while only one or two items misfit the 2PL/3PL (N=6,000 fit sample; **Tier B**, one testing program).
  - Terminology note that matters operationally: "Rasch" and "1PL IRT" differ in whether persons are individually parameterised (Rasch, JML/CML, Winsteps) or summarised by a mean and SD (1PL, MML, BILOG-style). Scale origin differs too: mean item difficulty (Rasch, criterion-referenced) vs mean person ability (1PL, norm-referenced). **Tier C** (rasch.org comparison table).
- **2PL:** adds a discrimination slope `a`. **Buys:** item information weighting — which is exactly what adaptive selection and any IRT-derived "slip" term depend on. Without `a`, all items carry identical information and adaptive selection reduces to difficulty matching. **Requires:** ability *spread* to estimate the slope. Only examinees whose ability differs from the item's difficulty are informative about the slope, and slope uncertainty propagates into difficulty uncertainty (Berger 2021 restates this from Thissen & Wainer 1982 and Stocking 1988; Berger 1991 reportedly found slope-estimation efficiency is low for SD(θ)≈0, peaks near SD(θ)=1, and declines again toward SD(θ)=2 — **Tier C**, secondary citation).
- **3PL:** adds a lower asymptote `c` ("pseudo-guessing"). **Buys:** better fit to multiple-choice data, and honest treatment of the fact that a 4-option item has a non-zero floor. **Requires:** a lot of low-ability data, because `c` is only identified by examinees far enough below the item that the curve has bottomed out. See §6.
- **Relation between p and b:** Lord showed the relation is monotonic when all items are equally discriminating (Rasch-like), and depends on the biserial r when discriminations differ (Hambleton & Jones 1993, **Tier A**). This is the theoretical reason p works as a proxy under Rasch conditions and degrades under 2PL/3PL conditions — confirmed empirically in §3.

### 2. How many responses per item are actually needed

This is the crux, so the numbers are given per model with their provenance and their limits.

#### 2a. Classical p-values

| Purpose | Responses/item | Source | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rank-order items by "true" difficulty (accuracy index ≥ .90) | **~100** | Nevo (1980), 5,20x applicants, 3 MC tests, samples of 16…1,024, 8 draws per size | B |
| Reproduce a *specific, exact* p-distribution shape | **500–1,000** | Nevo (1980) | B |
| Stable item-level CTT statistics for item selection | **≤100 stable; 100–200 "reliable item selection decisions"** | Jones, Smith & Talley (2006), as summarised in a UNC dissertation | C (secondary) |
| Historical pretesting norms | Henryson: 300 min.; Nunnally: "at least 300, preferably 1,000+"; Conrad: 500; **ETS SAT pretests: 1,000–3,000** | All via Nevo (1980)'s literature review | C (secondary) |

Nevo's headline finding is the useful one: **for three of five accuracy indices, the required sample was considerably smaller than commonly recommended.** The pattern generalises — *ordering* items needs far less data than *estimating* each item's level precisely, and product decisions that only need ordering (e.g. "which of these two variants is harder") are cheap.

Caveat I could not resolve: I read Nevo's abstract and body excerpts returned by search, **not** the full article.

#### 2b. Rasch / 1PL

The precision is analytic, not empirical. Wright & Stone's result (via Linacre) is that for a reasonably targeted sample, modelled item SE falls in **2/√N to 3/√N logits** — lower bound at 40–60% success rates, upper bound at success rates beyond 15%/85%. My arithmetic from that: SE ≈ 0.20–0.30 logits at N=100, 0.13–0.19 at N=250, 0.089–0.134 at N=500.

Linacre's table (**Tier C** — *Rasch Measurement Transactions*, a newsletter; derived from SE arithmetic, and the ±1-logit/95% row is reported as confirmed empirically by Azizan et al. 2020, which I did not read):

| b stable within | Confidence | Range (best→poor targeting) | "Most purposes" |
|---|---|---|---|
| ±1 logit | 95% | 16–36 | 30 |
| ±1 logit | 99% | 27–61 | 50 |
| ±½ logit | 95% | 64–144 | 100 |
| ±½ logit | 99% | 108–243 | 150 |
| Definitive / high stakes | 99%+ | 250 – 20×test length | 250 |
| Adverse circumstances | robust | 450+ | 500 |

He adds a useful floor: **at least 8 correct and 8 incorrect responses** for any confidence that b is within 1 logit, and inflate by 10–40% if there are unmodelled disturbances (different conditions, different curricula — for a prep app, read: different cohorts, different study stages).

Textbook-level statements: 1PL/Rasch "100 to 200 people per item may suffice" vs 500+ for 2PL/3PL (Albano's open measurement text, citing de Ayala 2009; **Tier C**). A rasch.org comparison table lists minimum reasonable N as 30 (Rasch) vs 200 (1PL, attributed to Downing 2003) — **Tier C, unverified attribution**.

O'Neill et al. (2020) used a resampling design on a 240-item linear test and found empirically that **even imprecise Rasch calibrations could sometimes be used for equating without much damage to person ability estimates** when enough items were involved (via Guo et al. 2024's literature review; **Tier C**, secondary). That is the strongest argument for tolerating noisy per-item b in a long test: item-level noise partially averages out at the test level.

#### 2c. 2PL

- **de Ayala (2009, p. 105), quoted verbatim inside Guo et al. (2024):** "assuming MMLE, the use of a prior distribution for a, and favorable conditions (e.g., θ/prior distribution match, etc.), it appears that a calibration sample size of at least **500 persons** and instruments of **20 or more items** tend to produce reasonably accurate item parameter estimates." **Tier B** (read as a quotation, not in the book). Note the three preconditions — MML, a prior on `a`, and distribution match — which are frequently ignored when this number is cited.
- **König, Spoden & Frey (2020), *Applied Psychological Measurement* 44(4), 311–326:** an optimised Bayesian **hierarchical** 2PL (non-centred reparameterisation, separated covariance/variance components, half-Cauchy or exponential hyperpriors on variance components) obtains accurate item parameters and trait scores **at N=100**. Taking N=500 as the nominal level, average bias in item parameters "does not considerably increase until N=100." The non-hierarchical 2PL underestimated discrimination at almost all N and test lengths; ULSMV/WLSMV were outperformed by the hierarchical Bayesian specs whenever N<500. **Tier A/B** — a clean crossed simulation, but simulation-only and the accuracy claim is contingent on the hierarchical shrinkage being appropriate.
- **Robustness follow-up (*Behavior Research Methods*, 2023):** fully crossed N ∈ {50, 75, 100, 150, 200, 500} × k ∈ {25, 50} × three variance-component settings × two correlations × three hyperprior families. The optimised H2PL gave essentially unbiased discrimination estimates across nearly all conditions, and showed **clear advantages over MML below N=150** — partly because MML produced substantially more inadmissible solutions there. **Tier B.**
- **Stone (1992), via Guo et al.:** under MML with varying distributional assumptions, **b estimates were generally precise and stable in small samples**; `a` estimates were precise only when the true distribution was normal. **Tier C** (secondary). This asymmetry — b is robust, a is fragile — recurs everywhere in this literature and is the single most reliable qualitative finding in it.
- **Seong (1990), via Guo et al.:** with matched prior and true distributions, accuracy improved monotonically with N (45-item test). With *mismatched* distributions, correctly specifying the prior helped at N=1,000 but **did not help item parameter estimation at N=100**. **Tier C** (secondary). Implication: priors rescue you at moderate N, not at tiny N.

#### 2d. 3PL

- **Consensus minimum ~1,000** for MLE (JML or MML), per multiple reviews (de Ayala 2009; Hambleton 1993; Jones et al. 2006; Yen & Fitzpatrick 2006 — all as compiled in a UNL dissertation; **Tier C**, secondary compilation, though the convergence of independent reviews raises confidence).
- **Historical grid** (all via a Turkish real-data study's literature review, **Tier C**): 1,000 with 40 items (Patsula & Gessaroli 1995; Tang et al. 1993; Yen 1987), 50 items (Lord 1968), 60 items (Hulin et al. 1982), 75 items (Yoes 1995); 500 with 30 items (Akour & Al-Omari 2013), 80 items (Ree & Jensen 1980); 300 with 50 items (Chuah, Drasgow & Luecht 2006); 200 with 25 items (Weiss & Minden 2012). The spread of 200–1,000 for comparable test lengths is itself the finding: these studies differ in estimator, prior specification, θ distribution, and what counted as "accurate."
- **Şahin & Anıl (2017)**, using *real* language-test data (population N=5,000; 9 sample sizes crossed with 20/30 item tests, stratified resampling) concluded **N=350 is viable for 3PL with 30-item tests** — smaller than the conventional 500, and explicitly framed as a call for more research rather than a definitive figure. **Tier B**, and the authors flag that findings may be limited to language tests and ≤30 items.
- **Han (2012)**, PARE 17(1), simulation study 2: N ∈ {300, 600, 1,000, 2,000} × 40/80 items × normal vs uniform θ × true c ∈ {…, 0.25, 0.50} × sparseness ∈ {0%, 25%, …}, 100 replications, PARSCALE, MML/EM. Findings: 3PL calibration **often failed to converge** when true c > 0.25 and/or N was small; convergence was much better under uniform θ than normal θ; convergence *dropped* when test length went 40→80. His conclusion: **3PL is feasible only when c ≤ 0.25 and N > 1,000, or when the sample is uniformly distributed.** c-parameter estimates were inaccurate whenever θ was normal and N ≤ 600, and biased even at large N for low-discrimination and/or easy items. **Tier B** (simulation, one software package, full text read).
- **Thissen & Wainer (1982):** the sample size needed for MMLE/EM 3PL estimates with acceptable standard errors is reported as **~10,000, and for some items as large as 1,000,000**, with their conclusion that "naked maximum likelihood estimation for the three-parameter model is not a technique that is likely to give happy results." **Tier C — I read this only as quoted in Frontiers (2017); I did not read Thissen & Wainer.** Treat the 1,000,000 figure as an illustration of unbounded SEs on `c` for easy items, not as a design target.
- **Tay et al. (2016):** recommended **N=20,000** when fitting a 3PL with covariates. **Tier C** — secondary citation only, unverified.

#### 2e. How estimation error behaves as n grows

Three distinct behaviours, and conflating them causes bad decisions:

1. **Sampling error shrinks as 1/√n.** Halving SE costs 4× the data. Rasch b: SE ≈ 2–3/√N logits. This is the only component that more data fixes.
2. **Bias from model misspecification does not shrink with n.** Fitting Rasch to 2PL-generated data forces b to absorb discrimination heterogeneity; items with extreme true discriminations acquire a persistent bias that no amount of additional data removes (irtsim vignette reproducing Schroeders & Gnambs's design; **Tier C** — a software vignette, not peer-reviewed, but the mechanism is standard). Guo et al.'s Study 3 shows the operational version: conversion-table MAEs from the 2PL shrank as N grew from 100 → 1,000 while **1PL MAEs stayed large**.
3. **Non-convergence and inadmissible solutions increase sharply at small n**, especially for 3PL (Han 2012) and for MML 2PL below N=150 (König et al. 2023). This shows up as missing or absurd estimates rather than as noisy ones, and it is the failure mode most likely to surprise a small team.

**Where sources disagree, and why.** Schroeders & Gnambs (2025) enumerate the reasons directly: item type, response model, estimation method (MML vs JML vs Bayesian), dimensionality, latent trait distribution, item pool size and homogeneity, and test design including missingness and item coverage. They note published minima ranging from **N=100 with priors** (König et al. 2020; Sheng 2013) or even **N=25** with missing-robust estimators (Finch & French 2019) up to **"even 2,000 may be insufficient"** for models with guessing/slipping parameters (Cuhadar 2022) or latent-class mixtures (Kutscher et al. 2019; Sen & Cohen 2023). Their conclusion — that general rules of thumb are impractical and a design-specific Monte Carlo is required — is the defensible position. **Tier A for "it depends"; Tier B for the tutorial itself.** Their own worked example uses **MSE of item difficulty < .05** as the acceptance criterion and notes that extreme-difficulty items cannot be recovered as precisely as medium ones.

### 3. Is proportion-correct a defensible difficulty proxy?

**Under a fixed form and a representative sample: yes, nearly perfectly.** Fan (1998), *Educational and Psychological Measurement* 58(3), used a statewide assessment (60-item math, 48-item reading, 193,000+ examinees taking both) and drew 20 samples of N=1,000 under three sampling plans of increasing dissimilarity. Average within-sample correlations between reversed CTT p and IRT b (**Tier A** — large real dataset, replicated across samples; full text read, though the extracted tables were partly garbled so I report only values I could read unambiguously):

| Comparison | Random samples | Truncated-ability samples |
|---|---|---|
| Normalised p ↔ **1PL** b | **.999** (math and reading) | **.998–.999** |
| Raw p ↔ 1PL b | .990 math / .980 reading | .953 (high-ability reading) – .991 |
| Raw p ↔ **2PL** b | .910–.920 | **.803–.841** (high-ability) |
| Raw p ↔ **3PL** b | .931–.934 | .877–.884 (high-ability) |
| p_bis ↔ 2PL a | .788–.900 | **.507** (high-ability reading) |
| p_bis ↔ 3PL a | .260–.688 | .635–.829 |

Fan's own conclusion is worth quoting for its bluntness: because Rasch b is almost perfectly related to CTT p, "the one-parameter model provides almost the same information as CTT with regard to item difficulty but at the cost of considerable model complexity."

**The invariance surprise.** Fan's Research Questions 4–5 tested the premise that motivates IRT. Between-sample correlations of *difficulty* indices were very high for both frameworks even across high- vs low-ability samples, and **"if there is any trend at all, it appears that CTT item difficulty estimates are slightly more invariant than IRT item difficulty estimates in almost all conditions."** For *discrimination*, invariance degraded in both frameworks as samples grew more dissimilar; in the worst cell (reading, high vs low ability) CTT point-biserials collapsed to r = .102/.106, the 2PL slope held moderate invariance at r = .636, and the **3PL slope also collapsed at r = .020**. **Tier B** — one testing program, two tests, and Fan himself calls some results "puzzling" and "contrary to both intuition and theoretical expectation." Do not over-read this into "IRT is unnecessary"; the real reading is that IRT's advantage is not in cross-sample difficulty invariance but in *incomplete-design* estimation, information weighting, and adaptive scoring — the things a linear-form CTT analysis cannot do at all.

**Where p breaks down.**

1. **Non-representative or range-restricted samples.** Fan's truncated-ability rows above. Also structural: p is higher in above-average samples and lower in below-average samples (Hambleton & Jones 1993, **Tier A**). For a prep app whose early users self-select toward motivated high scorers, every early p is biased easy relative to the eventual population.
2. **Adaptive administration — the confound, precisely.** Let item *j* be shown to a subpopulation with ability density g_j(θ). Then

   `E[p_j] = ∫ P_j(θ) g_j(θ) dθ`

   In a fixed form with random assignment, g_j = g (the population density) for every item, so p_j is a monotone function of b_j and the ordering is preserved. **Under adaptive routing, g_j depends on b_j by construction:** a hard item is placed in a high module and shown mostly to θ ≫ 0, so its p is pulled up; an easy item placed in a low module is shown mostly to θ ≪ 0, so its p is pulled down. The two effects push in the *same* direction — toward compressing, and at the limit inverting, the apparent difficulty ordering. A perfectly targeted adaptive test drives every item toward p ≈ 0.5 regardless of b; that is the *design goal* of adaptive delivery and simultaneously the destruction of raw p as a difficulty statistic. The same argument applies to any adaptive practice engine: `p` is a statistic about the pairing of items to learners, not about items.

   Empirically, Wu et al. (2025), *EPM*, state the consequence directly: in CAT, "the correlation between percent correct and item difficulty is diminished because of the targeted administration of the items," and "item responses are observed over a restricted range of abilities, reducing item-total score correlations" — so point-biserial discrimination is damaged too. **Tier A** for the mechanism (it is a mathematical identity plus a widely restated result); **Tier B** for the specific simulation magnitudes.

3. **What corrects it.**
   - **Fit an IRT model by MML on all responses.** Because adaptive/MST routing depends only on responses already observed, the missingness is MAR and ignorable for likelihood inference, so MML recovers unbiased item parameters (Mislevy & Sheehan 1989; Mislevy & Wu 1988, 1996; Glas 1988; Eggen & Verhelst 2011; Wang, Chen & Jiang 2020). **Tier A** — a derived result restated independently across five decades of primary sources, several of which I read directly or in full-text preprint.
   - **The MAR condition is fragile in two specific, documented ways.** (a) You must include **all** responses that fed the routing decision; dropping stage-1 responses or filtering low-exposure items breaks ignorability. Wu et al. retained only items with ≥10 responses and explicitly acknowledge this "implied a slight violation of MAR." (b) If routing is multidimensional but you calibrate each dimension with a separate unidimensional model, MAR is violated and estimation degrades badly (Jewsbury & Van Rijn 2020; Wang et al. 2020; reproduced by Wu et al.). **This matters here directly:** a 7-stage MST that routes on overall performance while reporting 10 per-concept buckets is exactly the multidimensional-routing/unidimensional-analysis configuration that breaks.
   - **Anchor the scale (FIPC).** See §5.
   - **Seed uncalibrated items non-adaptively.** Random seeding removes the confound by design rather than modelling around it. Wu et al. observed that the "reversal" pathology "was never observed in the random missing data condition because there was no segregation of item difficulties and ability levels." NCSBN's practice for suspected-drifted items is the same instinct: pull them and **re-pretest non-adaptively**.
   - **Constrain discrimination.** A lognormal prior on `a` forces positivity and is Wu et al.'s recommended mitigation for reversals.

4. **Quantified cost of calibrating from adaptive data** (Wu et al. 2025, 30-item CAT, pools of 100/500/800, 10 replications, `catR` + `mirt`; **Tier B**):

   | Parameter | Complete data | Random selection | CAT |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | Discrimination RMSE | 0.044–0.048 | 0.09–0.29 | **0.45–0.68** |
   | Difficulty RMSE | 0.064–0.077 | 0.12–0.40 | **0.28–0.43** |

   Difficulty RMSE under CAT *improved* with larger pools (0.43 → 0.31 → 0.28) as targeting sharpened, while under random selection it *worsened* (0.12 → 0.40) as the matrix grew sparser. Discrimination RMSE under CAT worsened with pool size (0.45 → 0.68). Caveat: the CAT condition administered 30 items per examinee vs the complete condition's full pool, so part of the gap is simply information volume, not adaptivity per se. Also note the exposure toll: at a ≥10-response threshold, roughly **2/3 of a 500-item pool had too little data to calibrate at all** under a greedy (non-exposure-controlled) selection rule.

**Verdict.** Proportion correct is defensible as a *reporting* statistic and as a difficulty proxy for fixed-form, randomly-assigned administration. It is not defensible as a difficulty estimate under adaptive delivery, and it is not defensible for cross-cohort comparison in a product whose user population is changing. Store raw p alongside the exposure distribution that produced it, and never let an adaptive selector read raw p.

### 4. Estimation approaches and their practical implications

| Approach | What it does | Sample-size implication | Practical notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **JML** (joint MLE, "Birnbaum paradigm") | Alternates item and person parameter estimation, treating θ as fixed effects | Item parameter estimates are not consistent as N→∞ with fixed test length (incidental-parameters problem); Rasch software applies an (L−1)/L-style correction | Standard textbook result; **I did not verify it against a primary source in this session — Tier C as cited here.** Winsteps-family software is JML |
| **CML** (conditional MLE) | Conditions on total score; Rasch only | Efficient, no θ distribution assumption | Justification under incomplete designs must be argued differently from MML — it cannot simply inherit Rubin's ignorability result (Eggen & Verhelst 2011, full text read; **Tier B**) |
| **MML/EM** (Bock & Aitkin 1981) | Integrates θ out over an assumed (or estimated) population distribution; EM for the maximisation | The workhorse figures in §2 mostly assume MML | **This is what makes incomplete/adaptive designs legitimate** (the MAR result in §3). Sensitive to θ-distribution misspecification at small N (Seong 1990) |
| **Bayes modal / MAP with priors** (Mislevy 1986; Swaminathan & Gifford 1985, 1986) | Adds priors on item parameters; point estimate at posterior mode | Priors on `a` are part of de Ayala's N≥500 2PL claim; priors on `c` are how 3PL is made to converge at all | Cheap, deterministic, available in `mirt`. The obvious home for a DOK/metadata-derived prior |
| **Fully Bayesian MCMC / hierarchical** (Sheng 2013; König et al. 2020) | Item parameters drawn from a common distribution; information pools across items | **N=100 for 2PL** with an optimised hierarchical spec (König et al. 2020); N=25 for some models with missing-robust estimation (Finch & French 2019, secondary) | Highest small-sample payoff and the highest tooling/skill cost. Requires Stan/PyMC plus convergence diagnostics. Shrinkage is a real assumption, not free lunch |

**Open-source tooling and maturity.**

- **R `mirt`** (Chalmers 2012, *Journal of Statistical Software* 48(6)): unidimensional and multidimensional; EM (Gauss-Hermite quadrature) and MHRM stochastic estimation; bifactor/two-tier dimension reduction; multiple-group and mixed-effects designs for DIF; latent-class/DINA models. Has `fixedCalib()` for FIPC. **The most mature open-source option, and it is what ETS's own 2024 MST small-sample study used** for calibration — a strong maturity signal. **Tier A** for maturity.
- **R `TAM`, `ltm`, `eRm`, `sirt`, `irtoys`:** `ltm` and `eRm` are effectively unidimensional-only (Chalmers's own comparison); `irtoys::sca()` provides Stocking-Lord scaling and was used alongside `mirt` in the ETS study; `TAM` and `sirt` are the large-scale-assessment workhorses (Robitzsch). **Tier B.**
- **R `catR`:** CAT/MST simulation and data generation; used by Wu et al. Essential for the design-specific Monte Carlo that Schroeders & Gnambs recommend. `mirtCAT` for CAT with `mirt` models.
- **R `irtsim`:** a sample-size-planning simulator (`irt_design` → `irt_study` → `recommended_n`) built around the Schroeders & Gnambs procedure, with MSE/bias/coverage criteria and a 0.05 default threshold. Useful, but a **CRAN vignette is not peer-reviewed evidence — Tier C.**
- **Commercial/legacy:** Winsteps, RUMM, ConQuest (Rasch); BILOG-MG, PARSCALE, MULTILOG, flexMIRT, ICL. Most published FIPC recipes (Kim 2006; Ban et al. 2006) are written against BILOG/PARSCALE/ICL, so translating them to `mirt` requires care.
- **Python:** `girth` (unidimensional MML, stable GRM), `py-irt` (Pyro-based Bayesian). A package published on PyPI as **`mirt`** advertises 1PL–4PL, MIRT, EM/MHRM/MCMC, fixed-item calibration, Stocking-Lord equating, and a Rust backend. **I found no peer-reviewed validation, no citation record, and no independent benchmark for it — Tier C, and I would not calibrate anything decision-bearing on it without first reproducing a published result.** For fully Bayesian work, Stan/`brms`/`cmdstanr` or PyMC are the better-evidenced route.

**For a small cost-sensitive team, the implication is narrow:** R + `mirt` (+ `catR` for design simulation, + Stan only if you go hierarchical) covers everything in this document, is free, and is what the ETS study itself used. Python-only is viable for scoring but currently weaker for calibration.

### 5. Fixed-parameter and online calibration

**Fixed-parameter calibration (FIPC).** You hold the item parameters of already-calibrated items fixed and estimate only the new items (plus, optionally, the group's ability distribution). Because the fixed items pin the scale, the new items land on the existing scale automatically — no separate scaling/linking step. Mechanically it is **MML/EM with a subset of parameters constrained** (Kim 2006; Wang et al. 2020, as summarised in Guo et al. 2024). Originated with Wainer & Mislevy (1990); the standard variants and their comparison are in Kim (2006).

**How much data does it take when the scale is already anchored?** The honest answer is *a factor, not a fixed floor*:

- **Guo et al. (2024), ETS Research Report** (operational MST program; 2PL; 15- or 30-item routing blocks; N ∈ {250, 500, 1,000}; item pool with a ~ N(1.00, 0.50) and b ~ N(−0.70, 0.70); skewed as well as symmetric θ; `mirt::fixedCalib`): "item estimation from **FIPC with 250 test takers was nearly as accurate as those from calibrations with SL scaling with 500** students. Similarly, item estimation from FIPC with 500 students was nearly as accurate as (sometimes more accurate than) those from calibrations with SL scaling with 1,000." Their operational recommendation: **the program's N=1,000 requirement could be relaxed to N>500.** For scale, at N=250 the separate-calibration-with-SL MAE on new-item b was ~0.211/0.220 logits (positively/negatively skewed θ) while FIPC did better; at N=1,000 SL(b) MAE fell to ~0.181/0.191. **Tier B** — simulation, one program's pool, and the authors flag generalisability. Two design choices they justify and that transfer: **fix only the routing-block items** (stable, ≥20% anchor proportion per Kolen & Brennan, and satisfies MAR), and don't use target-block items as anchors because they are unstable under small samples with missing-by-design.
- **FIPC across multiple administrations (Guo et al. Study 2):** concurrent FIPC pooling two administrations materially improved new-item accuracy over one administration, though less than a doubled sample would (because MST missing-by-design reduces the second administration's per-item information). **Tier B.** Practically: an item that is under-calibrated after one cohort can be topped up rather than re-pretested from scratch.
- **Kim (2006):** compared five FIPC methods across N = 300 → 3,000; accuracy increased with N, and improved *slightly* with more fixed items (10 → 40). The **multiple-weights-updating with multiple EM cycles (MWU-MEM)** variant was robust across θ distributions. **Tier C** (secondary, via Guo et al. and the ETS abstract). The "more anchors helps only slightly" result is worth knowing: anchor *quality and targeting* matters more than anchor count.
- **Ban, Hanson, Wang, Yi & Harris (2001), *JEM* / ACT RR 2000-11** — online calibration in CAT, five methods, N ∈ {300, 1,000, 3,000}: **MEM (MML with multiple EM cycles) produced the smallest parameter estimation error at every sample size** and requires no anchor items. **Stocking's Method B** (fixed θ̂ + anchor items + Stocking-Lord transformation) also worked very well but lengthens the test. **OEM** (one EM cycle) is mathematically similar to MEM but consistently worse. **Stocking's Method A** (treating θ̂ as true θ) had the largest error and a theoretical defect. The **BILOG/Prior method failed at small samples.** The 2002 sparse-data follow-up (240 pretest items) reproduced the ordering and added that MEM and Method B maintained the scale while OEM's scale drifted. **Tier B** — I read the abstracts and substantial excerpts of both ACT reports, not the complete reports.
- **Multi-group FIPC** performed "nearly equally to or better than" separate calibration with scaling at **N=500 and N=2,000** for recovering both ability distributions and new item parameters (Kim & Kolen-lineage work, *Applied Measurement in Education* 2019). **Tier C** — abstract only.
- **Bias-corrected FIPC (BCFIPC)**, *Stats* 2025, varied N ∈ {125, 250, 500, 1,000, 2,000, 4,000} and reports success even at **N=125**. **Important caveat: this study's target parameters are the group's μ and σ under random DIF, not new-item parameters.** Do not cite N=125 as a new-item calibration floor. **Tier C** for our purposes.

**What I could not find:** a peer-reviewed source giving a defensible minimum like "N=100 suffices for an anchored 2PL b." The smallest credible new-item figures I located are **N=250** (Guo et al., FIPC, MAE on b in the 0.09–0.19 range depending on method and θ shape) and **N=300** (Kim 2006; Ban et al. 2001). Combined with the Rasch SE arithmetic (2–3/√N), a reasonable synthesis is: **with the scale anchored and only b free (Rasch), ~100–150 responses gets you ±0.5 logits; with `a` also free (2PL), plan on 250–500 unless you go hierarchical Bayesian.** That synthesis is mine, not a quotation — **Tier B/C**.

**Online/continuous calibration** is the operational form: seed uncalibrated items into live administrations, accumulate responses, estimate with θ̂ or by MML on the joint matrix, and promote items once precision thresholds are met. Key operational parameters from the literature: **minimum exposure threshold** (Wu et al. used ≥10 responses and called it "somewhat arbitrary"; production programs use far higher), **seeding location** and **sampling method** (random vs adaptive/D-optimal), and **termination rule**. One dissertation-level finding worth flagging: when item parameter drift is present in the operational pool, the **random seeding method is more robust across conditions**, while D-optimal targeted seeding wins only when drift is absent. **Tier C** (dissertation abstract). This aligns with the anti-confound argument in §3: random seeding is the design that survives the most things going wrong.

### 6. Discrimination and guessing

**What does ignoring discrimination cost?**

The evidence points in *both* directions depending on sample size and design, and this is genuinely contested rather than unsettled-by-neglect.

*Costs of assuming Rasch when discriminations differ:*
- **Structural, non-vanishing bias in b.** The 1PL forces equal slopes, so b absorbs slope heterogeneity; items with extreme true discriminations acquire bias that does not shrink with N, while items near the constraint are fine and are actually estimated with *lower* variance than under a correct 2PL. RMSE alone hides this because it conflates the two components. **Tier C as sourced** (irtsim vignette extending Schroeders & Gnambs's design), though the mechanism is textbook.
- **Real-data misfit.** ~30% of items misfit the 1PL on both a math and a reading statewide test where the 2PL/3PL fit nearly perfectly (Fan 1998, N=6,000 fit sample). **Tier B.**
- **Operational cost.** Guo et al. Study 3: at N=100, 1PL FIPC produced *larger* conversion-table MAE than 2PL across easy/medium/hard target blocks; as N grew, 2PL MAEs shrank while **1PL MAEs stayed large** — so the simpler model did not pay off even in the small-sample regime it was proposed for, *for that program's already-2PL-calibrated pool*. **Tier B**, and the authors are explicit that this is program-specific.
- **Lost information weighting.** Without `a`, all items carry the same information at their difficulty, so adaptive selection cannot prefer a sharp item over a flat one at the same b, and any IRT-derived slip/guess term collapses toward a constant.

*Costs of insisting on 2PL:*
- **Lord (1983):** for 5-, 10-, and 15-item forms drawn from a 50-item vocabulary test, the Rasch raw sum score may be **slightly superior** to the 2PL a-weighted score as an approximation to true scores at θ ∈ {−2,−1,0,1,2} **when N < 100–200**. **Tier C** (read as summarised in Guo et al.).
- **de Ayala (2009):** Rasch yields good ability estimates when discriminations are roughly equal *or when the number of items is large*. **Tier C** (secondary).
- **Berger et al. (2021), *Frontiers in Education*:** in MST designs where items are, for practical reasons, **non-optimally placed**, the 2PL recovered item parameters *worse* than Rasch — especially at the margins of the difficulty range. Their framing: "the higher flexibility of 2PL modeling, where item discrimination is allowed to vary, seems to come at the cost of increased volatility in parameter estimation. Although the overall bias may be modest, single items can be affected by severe biases." **Tier B** — simulation, but directly on-point for MST with imperfect item placement, which is the realistic case for a new product. The mechanism is the one from §1: slope estimation needs ability spread, and MST modules deliberately narrow the spread each item sees.

**Synthesis (mine, Tier B):** the choice is not 1PL-vs-2PL in the abstract but *1PL-vs-2PL given your ability spread per item*. In a well-spread, randomly-seeded calibration sample with ≥250 responses/item, 2PL is worth it. Inside narrow MST modules at small N, Rasch is defensible and possibly better, and the hybrid used by several programs — Rasch or fixed-slope during early calibration, 2PL once volume permits — is well-supported by the pair of results above. If you go 2PL at small N, use a prior on `a` (that precondition is baked into de Ayala's N≥500) or go hierarchical (König et al. 2020).

**Guessing (`c`) with limited data — it is worse than people expect.**

- **Why it is unstable:** `c` is the lower asymptote, so it is informed only by examinees far enough below the item that the ICC has flattened. Easy items simply have no such examinees. Consequences documented repeatedly: **very large standard errors on `c`** (Thissen & Wainer 1982, via Han 2012 and via ETS RM 1985 — **Tier C**, secondary), and a **substantial covariance between `c` and `b`** that propagates `c`'s error into difficulty. Han's simulation confirms the propagation: "the SEEs for b parameter were dramatically increased as the SEEs for c parameter got larger with 3PLM, especially when the sample size was small and/or the sparseness was serious." **Tier B.**
- **Lord's rule of thumb:** `c` can be estimated for an individual item only if the item is hard enough, `a` is steep enough that the curve reaches the asymptote while examinees still exist below it, and N is large enough to have observations down there — summarised as a criterion score around −3 (bendable to −4/−5 in very large samples, −2 or higher in small ones). **Tier C** — read in an ETS Research Report abstract/first pages, and it is a heuristic by its own description.
- **Quantified instability (Han 2012, Tier B):** MML/EM 3PL frequently **failed to converge** when true c > 0.25 and/or N was small; convergence dropped when test length went 40 → 80; feasible only at c ≤ 0.25 with N > 1,000, or with uniformly distributed θ. `c` estimates were **inaccurate whenever θ was normal and N ≤ 600**, and remained biased even at large N for low-discrimination or easy items.
- **Fixing `c` = 1/k** (the "FG3PL"): dramatically higher convergence rates even at N=300–600, and much lower standard errors of estimation for `a` and `b` — with, by construction, zero error on `c`. For 4-option MCQs that means c = 0.25. **Tier B.** Han's deeper objection is worth registering: **when `c` is freely estimated, `a` and `b` are not comparable across items** with different `c` values, so the free-`c` 3PL's "difficulty" and "discrimination" parameters lose their ordinary interpretation as indices.
- **Counter-evidence:** a comparison of 3PL against a common-`c` variant (C3PL) and a fixed-guessing variant (FG3PL) at N ∈ {1,200, …, 4,000} found the constrained models produced **larger** bias and RMSE than the full 3PL, and noted that none of them address ability-dependent guessing. **Tier C** — non-indexed journal, and the authors themselves note their sample sizes were all ≥1,200 so small-N behaviour was untested. Read this as: constraining `c` helps at small N and may hurt at large N.
- **Regularisation is standard practice:** a Beta prior on `c` centred near 0.20 is the common remedy, and "many testing programs use a constrained 3PL where the c parameter is fixed or given a strong prior." **Tier C** (reference-site synthesis, not a primary source), but consistent with everything above.

**Practical reading for a 4-option MCQ bank with no data:** free `c` estimation is out of reach for a long time. Fix c = 0.25 (or put a tight Beta prior around ~0.20–0.25) and spend the data budget on `b` first and `a` second. If a BKT slip term is derived from IRT, note that a slip/guess pair inherited from a poorly-identified `c` will be the least trustworthy quantity in the entire pipeline.

### 7. Item parameter drift

**What it is.** Systematic change over time in an item's statistical properties — usually b becoming easier through exposure, coaching, or content familiarity; sometimes harder through curriculum change; sometimes slope or asymptote change. In language testing, documented causes include over-exposure, practice effects, test-wise training, and shifts in the construct or the currency of specific content. **Tier B.**

**Why it matters more in adaptive programs than linear ones.** In a CAT/MST, drifted operational items corrupt the θ̂ used to place new items, so drift **propagates from the operational pool into every newly calibrated item** (GMAC RR-11-02; Iowa dissertation on IPD × online calibration). **Tier B.**

**How it is detected.**
- **Displacement / anchored-vs-free comparison:** calibrate with parameters anchored, then inspect the discrepancy between the anchored value and what the current data want. Winsteps-family programs report this as *displacement*; thresholds around 0.5–0.6 logits are used to trigger unanchoring. **Tier C** (practitioner convention; I did not find a peer-reviewed derivation of the 0.6 threshold).
- **Standard-error-scaled change:** NCSBN's NCLEX study flagged items whose estimate **changed by two or more standard errors** of the original adaptive estimate. **Tier B** (AERA paper, full text read).
- **Formal tests:** Wald and Lagrange-multiplier tests, likelihood-ratio tests, robust-z, mean infit/outfit shifts, D², CUSUM, and NCDIF/DFIT for the item-characteristic-curve-difference family. **Tier B/C** depending on the specific statistic.
- **Sequential monitoring:** Psychometrika-published **sequential generalised likelihood ratio tests** that surveil multiple item parameters jointly, continuously or intermittently, and signal drift "as soon as they identify significant parameter shift"; validated on simulated and real assessment data with "satisfactory detection power … while regulating error rates reasonably low," and reported as outperforming existing methods. **Tier B — abstract and reference list only; I did not read the full paper.** This is the right conceptual model for a product with continuous data: monitoring, not periodic batch recalibration.

**How often programs recalibrate.** This is where the literature is thinnest and I will not invent a number.
- **NCLEX (NCSBN, Rasch, high-stakes CAT):** "**Items generally are not recalibrated** unless changes to the item text or item formats justify obtaining new parameter estimates. As a result, some items that were calibrated ten years ago are still being used based on the original Rasch item difficulty estimates." Their audit found the active pool "relatively stable across time," with individual outliers identified, reviewed for content validity, and **re-pretested non-adaptively** to validate the change. Items that do not perform to expectation are routinely removed. **Tier B** — a single program, 2005, conference paper.
- **GMAC RR-11-02 (simulation):** even with **20% of the pool drifting by δ = −0.50 logits for 50% of test takers**, the effect on pretest item calibration was "so small that the second CAT administration … would not be influenced significantly in terms of score estimation." The authors immediately qualify: the effect is **cumulative**, drift from practice/curriculum change reaches increasingly many test takers over time, and their newly calibrated items were only 10% of a 1,000-item pool — so the impact "may rapidly become consequential as more pretest items are added." **Tier B**, and their own framing is "encouraging news, short term."
- I found **no authoritative recalibration cadence** ("every N months", "every N administrations"). The defensible operational stance from the above is **continuous monitoring with removal/re-pretest of flagged items**, not scheduled wholesale recalibration.

**Directly relevant to a prep product:** the drift mechanisms most cited — exposure, coaching, test-wise practice — are *maximised* by a product whose users deliberately re-encounter items, discuss them, and study explanations. Item-family variants partially mitigate this (a family can rotate) but also couple it: if one variant leaks, its siblings' parameters are suspect too, and family-level rather than item-level drift monitoring is the natural unit.

### 8. Beyond the questions asked

**Cold-start difficulty prediction from item features (item difficulty modeling, IDM).** This is the literature that speaks most directly to "we have 2,108 items and zero responses."

- **Medical MCQs, closest analogue found:** 1,154 MOCA-Peds items, features = lexical, cohesion (TAACO), **blueprint encodings**, BioMedBERT embeddings, semantic similarity, unsupervised semantic indicators. Regression on IRT b achieved **RMSE ≈ 1.37 logits and R² ≈ 0.29** (~15% improvement over baseline); ordinal classification hit **30–34% exact accuracy** (baseline 17%) with **~68% adjacent accuracy**. Strongest predictors: **blueprint encoding, number of answer options, and option length**, each ~10% of explanatory variance, plus embeddings. **Tier B** — a workshop paper, single dataset, but on medical MCQs with content-code features, which is precisely the available metadata here. Note what "R² = 0.29 / RMSE = 1.37 logits" means practically: usable for coarse bucketing and for priors, useless for anything requiring a point estimate.
- **Reading items:** Schneider, Chen & Heneger (2026, *PARE* 31(1)5) achieved **R² = 0.34–0.37** cross-validated, with achievement-level-descriptor match (0.74 logits/level) dominating and DOK contributing only 0.12 logits/level. Also relevant: Ferrara et al. reviewed >100 reading IDM studies; **13 explained ≥50% of difficulty variance but only 2 of those 13 cross-validated**, so the headline R² values in that literature are mostly training-set optimism. **Tier B, and this caveat should be applied to any IDM claim above ~40%.**
- **LLM-assisted IDM:** GPT-4o-extracted item features fed to random forests / gradient boosting reached **r ≈ 0.87** with true difficulty on math items, beating **direct** LLM difficulty ratings (r ≈ 0.72–0.87) and beating both TF-IDF baselines (no better than a dummy regressor) and metadata-only models. **Tier B** — one recent study; the field is moving fast and results are not yet replicated across pools. Two systematic reviews exist (AlKhuzaey et al. 2024, 55 studies; Peters et al. 2025, 43 regression studies) — I read neither in full.

**Judgmental priors — the formally correct way to use DOK.** Swaminathan, Hambleton, Sireci, Xing & Rizavi (2003), *Applied Psychological Measurement* 27(1): Bayesian item calibration with priors constructed from **judgmental** information. Their reported result: "Improvements in the one-parameter model were modest; considerable improvements with the two- and three-parameter models were observed." **Tier C — abstract only; the article is paywalled and I could not read the methods, sample sizes, or effect sizes.** The design principle it establishes is nonetheless the right one: a DOK level should enter as a **prior mean on b** (with a wide prior SD reflecting the weak r ≈ 0.1–0.25 relationship), not as a difficulty value. The data then overrides it as responses accumulate, automatically and in the correct proportion.

**Item families are a free source of statistical strength.** With 2,108 items in families of variants and 422 knowledge components, a hierarchical model with random effects at the family and/or KC level pools information across siblings, so a family with 8 variants and 40 responses each behaves closer to 320 responses for the family-level difficulty than to eight independent 40-response calibrations. This is the same shrinkage mechanism that gets König et al. (2020) to N=100. **I did not locate a study quantifying the gain for item families specifically — Tier C, flagged as a pointer rather than an evidenced claim.** The relevant literature to read next is explanatory/random-item-effects IRT (LLTM and descendants, De Boeck & Wilson) and item-family models (Glas & van der Linden).

**Volume arithmetic (my computation from the stated design).**

| Target | Total responses | Completed 140-item diagnostics |
|---|---|---|
| 50 / item across 2,108 items | 105,400 | ~753 |
| 100 / item | 210,800 | ~1,506 |
| 250 / item | 527,000 | ~3,764 |
| 500 / item | 1,054,000 | ~7,529 |

But a 7-stage, 3-way MST panel contains only **1 + 6×3 = 19 modules × 20 = 380 items**. Under uniform exposure, reaching 250 responses/item *within one panel* needs **~679 test takers** (380 × 250 / 140) and 500/item needs ~1,357. Routing concentrates exposure in central modules, so extreme modules need 2–4× that. **Conclusion: one MST panel is calibratable at low-hundreds-to-low-thousands of test takers; the full 2,108-item bank is not, and the bank must therefore be calibrated incrementally by seeding, with FIPC anchoring to the panel.**

## DOK 1 — established facts

1. CTT item difficulty is the p-value, the proportion of a given sample answering correctly; it is a sample statistic and is group-dependent. Higher p = easier. (Hambleton & Jones 1993, NCME *ITEMS* Module 16. **Tier A**)
2. `SE(p) = sqrt(p(1−p)/n)`; at p=.5 this is .050 at n=100, .035 at n=200, .022 at n=500. (Arithmetic. **Tier A**)
3. IRT difficulty `b` is the ability level at which P(correct) = 0.50 (1PL/2PL) and is on the same scale as θ. (Standard. **Tier A**)
4. CTT item difficulty and discrimination are group-dependent: higher p from above-average samples, lower from below-average; higher discrimination from heterogeneous samples, lower from homogeneous. (Hambleton & Jones 1993. **Tier A**)
5. Reversed, normalised CTT p correlates **.998–.999** with Rasch b within a sample, across random, gender-split, and truncated-ability samples. (Fan 1998, N=1,000 samples, 60/48-item tests. **Tier A**)
6. That correlation falls to **.910–.934** against 2PL/3PL b in random samples and to **.803–.884** in truncated-ability samples. (Fan 1998. **Tier A**)
7. CTT p-values were **at least as invariant across dissimilar samples as IRT b** in Fan's data; CTT and 2PL/3PL discrimination indices were both substantially less invariant than difficulty, collapsing to r ≈ .02–.11 in the worst high-vs-low-ability cell. (Fan 1998. **Tier B** — one program)
8. ~30% of items misfit the 1PL on two statewide tests where only 1–2 items misfit the 2PL/3PL (N=6,000). (Fan 1998. **Tier B**)
9. Rasch modelled item SE lies between **2/√N and 3/√N** logits for a reasonably targeted sample. (Wright & Stone via Linacre 1994. **Tier C** — newsletter; the derivation is transparent)
10. Linacre's table: b stable within ±½ logit needs ~100 (95%) or ~150 (99%); "definitive/high-stakes" 250; adverse circumstances 500. Minimum ~8 correct and 8 incorrect responses for ±1 logit. (Linacre 1994. **Tier C**)
11. de Ayala's 2PL guidance is **≥500 persons and ≥20 items**, conditional on MML, a prior on `a`, and θ/prior distribution match. (de Ayala 2009 p.105, quoted in Guo et al. 2024. **Tier B**)
12. An optimised Bayesian hierarchical 2PL yields accurate item parameters and trait scores at **N=100**, and beats MML below N=150 partly by avoiding inadmissible solutions. (König, Spoden & Frey 2020, APM 44(4):311–326; robustness follow-up in *Behavior Research Methods* 2023. **Tier A/B**)
13. 3PL consensus minimum is ~1,000 for MLE; Şahin & Anıl's real-data study argues N=350 suffices for 30-item tests; Thissen & Wainer reported ~10,000 for acceptable SEs. (**Tier B / B / C-secondary** respectively)
14. 3PL MML/EM frequently fails to converge when true c > 0.25 or N is small; feasible only at c ≤ 0.25 with N>1,000 or uniform θ; `c` is inaccurate when θ is normal and N ≤ 600, and biased even at large N for easy or low-discriminating items. (Han 2012, PARE 17(1). **Tier B**)
15. `c` has substantial covariance with `b`, so uncertainty in `c` inflates SE(b); fixing c = 1/k eliminates this and raises convergence rates markedly even at N=300–600. (Han 2012. **Tier B**)
16. Under CAT/MST, missingness is MAR and ignorable for MML **provided** routing depends only on observed responses and all such responses are included in the calibration. (Rubin 1976; Mislevy & Sheehan 1989; Mislevy & Wu 1988/1996; Glas 1988; Eggen & Verhelst 2011; Wang, Chen & Jiang 2020. **Tier A**)
17. Calibrating a multidimensional MST/CAT with separate unidimensional models violates MAR and produces serious estimation error. (Jewsbury & Van Rijn 2020; Wang et al. 2020; reproduced by Wu et al. 2025. **Tier A/B**)
18. Recalibrating from post-operational CAT data: discrimination RMSE 0.45–0.68 vs 0.044–0.048 complete-data; difficulty RMSE 0.28–0.43 vs 0.064–0.077. Two of ten replications produced "reversals" with 51 negative discriminations and 441 examinees underestimated by >2 SD; a lognormal prior on `a` mitigates. (Wu et al. 2025, *EPM*. **Tier B**)
19. Under a ≥10-response inclusion threshold and greedy (uncontrolled) item selection, roughly **two-thirds of a 500-item pool** had insufficient data to calibrate. (Wu et al. 2025. **Tier B**)
20. FIPC of new items at N=250 ≈ separate-calibration-with-Stocking-Lord at N=500; at N=500 ≈ SL at N=1,000; the studied program could relax N=1,000 to N>500. (Guo et al. 2024, ETS RR. **Tier B**)
21. In online CAT calibration, **MEM** (MML, multiple EM cycles) had the lowest error at N=300, 1,000, and 3,000 and needs no anchors; Stocking's Method B was second but requires anchors; OEM was worse; Stocking's Method A was worst; BILOG/Prior failed at small N. (Ban et al. 2001, *JEM* / ACT RR 2000-11; Ban et al. 2002. **Tier B**)
22. FIPC accuracy improves only *slightly* with more fixed anchor items (10 → 40). (Kim 2006, via Guo et al. **Tier C**)
23. In MST designs with non-optimal item placement, the **2PL recovered item parameters worse than Rasch**, especially at the difficulty margins; individual items can be severely biased. (Berger et al. 2021, *Frontiers in Education*. **Tier B**)
24. Lord (1983) found the Rasch sum score can slightly beat 2PL a-weighted scoring for short forms when N < 100–200. (**Tier C**, secondary)
25. NCLEX does not routinely recalibrate; items are flagged when the estimate changes by ≥2 SE of the original, then reviewed and **re-pretested non-adaptively**. Active pools were "relatively stable across time." (NCSBN/Gorham AERA 2005. **Tier B**)
26. Simulated drift of 20% of a 1,000-item pool by δ = −0.50 for 50% of test takers had a statistically detectable but not "consequentially meaningful" short-term effect on pretest item calibration; the authors emphasise cumulative long-term risk. (GMAC RR-11-02. **Tier B**)
27. **Webb's own framework holds that complexity and difficulty are distinct constructs:** complex tasks are generally difficult, but difficult tasks are not necessarily complex, and item difficulty does not predict complexity. (Webb Alignment, NCME 2023 session paper. **Tier A** for the framework's position)
28. In Webb Alignment's 298-item math/reading cross-tab, DOK 1 items split 27%/44%/29% across low/medium/high empirical difficulty; DOK 2 split 17%/54%/30%; DOK 3 split 6%/50%/44%. ~75% of DOK 1 items were medium or higher difficulty. **Spearman ρ = 0.101 (computed by me from their published counts, n=298, t ≈ 1.74).** (**Tier B** for their counts; the ρ is my calculation)
29. In a reading IDM study, r(DOK, item difficulty) = **0.23** (vs 0.43 for achievement-level-descriptor match, 0.30 Lexile, 0.33 passage word count); in regression, each DOK level added **0.12 logits** (SE 0.05, p=.01) in Model 1 and **0.11** (p=.02) in Model 2, versus **0.74/0.68 logits** per achievement-level-descriptor level. Full-model cross-validated R² = **0.34–0.37**. (Schneider, Chen & Heneger 2026, *PARE* 31(1)5. **Tier B**)
30. In a Brazilian ten-school medical progress test (119 items, 4 options), mean difficulty (proportion *incorrect*) by Bloom level was 0.29 / 0.34 / 0.36 for low / intermediate / high; **no significant difference (F=0.993, p=0.374) and no significant correlation (ρ=0.172, p=0.06)**. Mean discrimination by the same levels was 0.28 / 0.31 / 0.35, **significantly different (p=0.026) with ρ=0.25, p=0.006**. Expert panels matched actual difficulty category for 54% of items, overestimating difficulty for 34%. (Hamamoto Filho et al. 2020, *São Paulo Medical Journal* 138(1):33–39. **Tier B**)
31. Kibble & Johnson reported a 48% expert difficulty-estimation success rate and **no** correlation between item taxonomy level and either difficulty or discrimination. (**Tier C** — read only as cited by Hamamoto Filho et al.)
32. Medical MCQ difficulty prediction from metadata + embeddings: **R² ≈ 0.29, RMSE ≈ 1.37 logits** on IRT b (1,154 items); top features were blueprint encoding, number of options, and option length. (**Tier B**)
33. Of >100 reviewed reading IDM studies, 13 explained ≥50% of difficulty variance but **only 2 of those 13 cross-validated**. (Ferrara et al. 2022 via Schneider et al. 2026. **Tier B**)
34. R `mirt` (Chalmers 2012, *JSS* 48(6)) supports EM and MHRM estimation, multiple groups, and FIPC via `fixedCalib`, and was the calibration engine in ETS's 2024 MST small-sample study. (**Tier A** for maturity)
35. Sample-size requirements in IRT depend on item type, response model, estimator, dimensionality, θ distribution, pool size/homogeneity, and test design; published minima span N=25 to N>2,000 for different models, so design-specific Monte Carlo is the recommended practice. (Schroeders & Gnambs 2025, *AMPPS*. **Tier A** for the conclusion)

## DOK 2 — insights and implications

**1. "How many responses per item" is the wrong first question. "What decision does the number support?" is the right one.** The literature separates cleanly along this axis. *Ordering* items by difficulty needs ~100 responses (Nevo). *Estimating* each b to ±0.5 logits needs 100–150 (Rasch) or 250–500 (2PL). *Estimating discrimination* needs both volume and ability spread. *Estimating guessing* needs volumes a small team will never reach. So the practical design is a **staircase of decisions**, each unlocked at a different volume: coarse difficulty buckets first, then anchored b, then a, then never c.

**2. The single most transferable qualitative finding across every source: `b` is robust, `a` is fragile, `c` is hopeless.** Stone (1992): b precise in small samples, a precise only under normal θ. Fan (1998): difficulty indices highly invariant, discrimination indices collapsing across dissimilar samples. Wu et al. (2025): CAT difficulty RMSE 4–6× inflated, discrimination RMSE 10–15×. Han (2012): `c` biased even at large N. This ordering should drive the whole roadmap — and it means that an adaptive engine leaning heavily on `a` is leaning on the least trustworthy quantity available.

**3. The adaptive confound and the exposure problem are the same problem, and both argue for random seeding.** Adaptive selection makes p uninterpretable *and* concentrates exposure so that most of the pool never accumulates enough responses to calibrate (two-thirds of a 500-item pool below threshold in Wu et al.). Random seeding of uncalibrated items fixes both at once — it restores the population θ distribution for those items and spreads exposure evenly — and it is also the seeding method that proved most robust when the operational pool had drifted. With 2,108 items and adaptive practice as a delivery channel, a **random-seeding slot in every practice session is probably the highest-leverage single design decision** in this whole area.

**4. Fan's invariance result reframes why IRT is worth the trouble here — and it isn't the usual reason.** If CTT p-values are as invariant as IRT b, then "IRT gives sample-free difficulty" is not the justification. The actual justifications, all of which apply to this product and none of which CTT can deliver: incomplete-design estimation with a defensible missing-data argument (MST routing, adaptive practice); putting items and learners on one scale so routing and per-concept reporting are coherent; information-weighted item selection; and a principled slip/guess term. Conversely, this means that if the product were fixed-form, CTT p would be nearly sufficient — the MST design, not measurement theory per se, is what forces IRT.

**5. The MST design creates a specific, documented trap that combines two findings.** MML on MST data is unbiased under MAR **only if** all routing responses are included **and** the analysis model matches the selection model. A 7-stage MST that routes on overall performance while reporting 10 per-concept buckets is, structurally, multidimensional routing. Jewsbury & Van Rijn and Wang et al. both show that calibrating such data with separate unidimensional models per dimension violates MAR and produces serious error — and Wu et al. reproduced it, seeing negative discriminations and reversals. **The implication: whatever model produces the 10 per-concept reports must not be the model used to calibrate items from MST data, unless it is genuinely multidimensional.** Calibrate unidimensionally on the full response matrix; derive per-concept reporting downstream.

**6. FIPC is worth more than a 2× sample discount because it also removes a class of bugs.** Separate calibration plus scaling requires a linking step (Stocking-Lord or similar), anchor-set curation, and a scale transformation applied correctly every time. FIPC folds all of that into the estimation, which is why ETS recommends it for the small-sample MST case and why `mirt::fixedCalib` exists. The 2× accuracy discount is the headline; the elimination of an error-prone manual step is arguably worth as much to a small team. The corollary from Kim (2006) — that more anchors help only slightly — means anchor *targeting and stability* deserve the attention, not anchor count. ETS's specific choice (fix only routing-block items, never target-block items) is directly copyable.

**7. Rasch may be the right model for exactly the wrong-seeming reason.** The usual argument is "Rasch needs less data." The stronger argument from Berger et al. is that **2PL is more volatile than Rasch precisely in MST designs with imperfect item placement** — because slope estimation needs ability spread and MST modules deliberately narrow it. A new product's item placement will be imperfect (initial b values come from priors, not data). So the ordering that follows is: Rasch or fixed-slope during initial MST calibration; migrate to 2PL once volume and placement quality permit; and note Guo et al.'s counter-case, where a program whose pool was *already* well calibrated under 2PL found the 1PL made conversion tables worse and not better. Both can be true: the right model depends on whether your pool is already well-placed.

**8. DOK is a defensible prior only in the Bayesian sense, and its practical value is close to zero as a difficulty prior.** Three independent datasets converge on r ≈ 0.10–0.25 — **1–5% of difficulty variance** — and a slope of 0.12 logits per level, so the whole DOK 1→4 range spans ~0.36 logits against an item-pool difficulty SD typically near or above 1 logit. Put differently: **DOK 4 items and DOK 1 items overlap almost completely in difficulty.** Using DOK to set MST module targets or to order adaptive practice would be close to using noise. Using DOK as a **prior mean on b with a wide prior SD** is defensible, costs nothing, and is washed out by data quickly — which is exactly what you want from a prior you don't believe in.

**9. The genuinely useful, counterintuitive DOK finding is about discrimination, not difficulty.** Hamamoto Filho et al. found the taxonomy–discrimination association (ρ=0.25, p=0.006) *stronger and significant* where the taxonomy–difficulty association was not (ρ=0.172, p=0.06), with mean discrimination rising 0.28 → 0.31 → 0.35 across levels. Since discrimination is what determines an item's information value for adaptive selection and for a slip term, **the actionable use of DOK is to predict which items will be informative, not which will be hard.** That inverts the intended use in the brief. It rests on one study plus one corroborating null (Kibble & Johnson found neither), so treat it as a hypothesis to test on your own data — but it is cheap to test and would change item-selection policy if true.

**10. Metadata bundles are worth ~10× more than DOK alone, and are still only priors.** DOK alone gives R² ≈ 0.01–0.05; blueprint + item structure + embeddings gives R² ≈ 0.29 on medical MCQs; adding achievement-level-descriptor-style reasoning codes gives 0.34–0.37 on reading. The 422 knowledge components, 15 content clusters, reasoning-operation tag, item-family id, and option text are collectively a far better prior than the DOK level, and **item-family membership is likely the strongest single feature available** since siblings share difficulty by construction. Two cautions: apply the Ferrara cross-validation caveat (most published high-R² IDM results are training-set optimism), and remember that RMSE ≈ 1.37 logits — the medical-MCQ result — is larger than the entire useful range of an MST module's target difficulty.

**11. Explanation prose for all four options is an underused asset.** Every source that decomposed feature importance found **option-level features** among the top predictors — number of options and option length in the medical study; distractor plausibility throughout the MCQ-quality literature. This bank has written rationales for all four choices, which is a richer distractor-quality signal than most IDM studies had access to. That is a plausible edge for a cold-start difficulty prior, and it is testable as soon as any response data exists via distractor-level p and option point-biserials.

**12. Hierarchical models are the highest-leverage technical choice available given zero data and 422 KCs / item families.** König et al. reach usable 2PL estimates at N=100 by pooling across items. With families of variants and KC groupings, the pooling structure is already present in the metadata. The mechanism is the same one that makes a metadata prior work, expressed properly: instead of "DOK 3 → b = 0.4," you write b_i ~ Normal(family mean, τ) with family means themselves drawn from a KC-level distribution. The costs are real — Stan/PyMC, convergence diagnostics, and a shrinkage assumption that will systematically pull genuinely-outlying items toward their family — but this is the one technique in the literature that meaningfully changes the sample-size arithmetic rather than just optimising within it.

**13. The pool is too large to calibrate; the panel is not. That asymmetry should shape sequencing.** 250 responses on every one of 2,108 items requires ~3,764 completed diagnostics. 250 responses per item within a *single* 380-item MST panel requires ~679 test takers under uniform exposure. So: build and calibrate one panel first (FIPC-anchored, Rasch or fixed-slope), then use that anchored scale to absorb the remaining ~1,700 items by random seeding over time. Attempting to calibrate the full bank before launch is not a resourcing problem, it is an arithmetic impossibility.

**14. Drift risk is structurally higher for a prep product than for the licensure programs the drift literature studies.** NCLEX can conclude its pool is stable partly because its items are secure and single-encounter. A prep app's users deliberately re-encounter items, share them, and read the explanations — i.e. they enact every mechanism the language-testing drift literature names (exposure, practice, test-wise training). GMAC's reassuring "not consequentially meaningful" result was for 20% of a pool drifting once; it explicitly warns the effect is cumulative and grows as newly calibrated items enter the pool. The natural monitoring unit here is the **item family** rather than the item, since a leaked variant compromises its siblings. Combined with insight 3, this argues for retaining a permanently random-seeded, never-adaptively-selected subset as a drift control group.

**15. There is a real tension between "calibrate cheaply" and "the MST is a high-stakes-feeling onboarding diagnostic."** Linacre's own table puts "definitive or high stakes" at 250 responses/item and "adverse circumstances" at 500. A first-session diagnostic that reports per-concept weakness across 10 buckets from 140 items is, from the learner's point of view, consequential — they will act on it. The 10-bucket reporting also means each bucket rests on ~14 items, so **per-bucket reliability, not item-level calibration precision, is likely to be the binding constraint on whether those reports are defensible.** That is a separate analysis from this one and worth doing before the item-calibration budget is set.

14. **Linacre, J. M. (1994). Sample Size and Item Calibration or Person Measure Stability.** *Rasch Measurement Transactions*, 7(4), 328. https://rasch.org/rmt/rmt74m.htm (and https://www.rasch.org/rmt/rmt94h.htm) — **Tier C.** *Full text read.* The most-cited Rasch sample-size table (30/50/100/150/250/500) with its explicit derivation from SE = 2–3/√N and the 8-correct/8-incorrect floor. Non-peer-reviewed newsletter; use as SE arithmetic, not as a recovery study.
15. **NCSBN / Gorham, J. (2005). Examining Changes in Item Difficulty Estimates Across Years for a High Stakes Licensure CAT.** AERA paper. https://www.ncsbn.org/public-files/2005.04_Gorham_AERA_Examining_Changes_in_Item_Difficulty_Estimates_Across_Years.pdf — **Tier B.** *Full text read.* The only operational statement I found on recalibration practice: NCLEX does not routinely recalibrate, flags items changing by ≥2 SE, and re-pretests suspects **non-adaptively**. Rasch-based, Winsteps, quarterly pool calibrations anchored on final CAT θ̂.
16. **Guo, F., & Zhang, L. (2011) [GMAC RR-11-02]. Potential Impact of Item Parameter Drift Due to Practice and Curriculum Change on Item Calibration in Computerized Adaptive Testing.** https://www.gmac.com/-/media/files/gmac/research/research-report-series/rr1102_itemcalibration.pdf — **Tier B.** *Full text read.* Quantifies drift tolerance (20% of pool, δ=−0.50, 50% of takers → not consequentially meaningful short-term) and articulates the propagation mechanism from operational items into pretest calibration, with an explicit cumulative-risk warning.
17. **Chalmers, R. P. (2012). mirt: A Multidimensional Item Response Theory Package for the R Environment.** *Journal of Statistical Software*, 48(6). https://www.jstatsoft.org/v48/i06/ (repo: https://github.com/philchalmers/mirt) — **Tier A** for tooling maturity. *Abstract and README read.* The peer-reviewed basis for treating `mirt` as the default calibration engine, including its comparison against `ltm`, `eRm`, and MCMCpack.
18. **Şahin, A., & Anıl, D. (2017). The Effects of Test Length and Sample Size on Item Parameters in Item Response Theory.** *Educational Sciences: Theory & Practice*. https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1130806.pdf — **Tier B.** *Full text read.* Real-data (not simulated) sample-size study concluding N=350 is viable for 30-item 3PL tests, plus the most complete historical grid of 3PL sample-size recommendations (200–1,000 across studies) with citations.
19. **König, C., & colleagues (2023). Robustness of the performance of the optimized hierarchical two-parameter logistic IRT model for small-sample item calibration.** *Behavior Research Methods*. https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-022-02000-5 — **Tier B.** *Abstract and results excerpts read.* The fully crossed robustness follow-up (N=50–500 × k=25/50 × variance components × hyperprior families) establishing the advantage over MML below N=150 and the role of inadmissible solutions.
20. **Han, K. T. / anonymous (2017). Expectation-Maximization-Maximization: A Feasible MLE Algorithm for the Three-Parameter Logistic Model.** *Frontiers in Psychology*, 8, 2302. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.02302 — **Tier C** for the numbers I took from it. *Full text read, but used only as the vehicle for secondary citations* (Thissen & Wainer 1982's 10,000/1,000,000; Tay et al. 2016's 20,000; San Martín et al. on 3PL identifiability). Its own contribution — an EMM algorithm framing 3PL as a mixture — is a pointer for anyone who does eventually need 3PL.
21. **Webb Alignment (2023). Differentiating Difficulty from Complexity** (NCME session paper) and **Complexity and Difficulty in a Coherent Standards-Based Education System.** https://www.webbalign.org/ncme-2023-differentiating-difficulty-from-complexity , https://www.webbalign.org/difficulty-and-complexity — **Tier B** (their data) / **Tier A** (the framework's own position). *Full text read.* Source of the 298-item DOK × difficulty cross-tab from which I computed ρ=0.101, and of the authoritative statement that DOK measures complexity, not difficulty.
22. **"Medical Item Difficulty Prediction Using Machine Learning" (2025).** AIME-Con workshop paper. https://aclanthology.org/2025.aimecon-wip.22.pdf — **Tier B.** *Full text read.* The closest available cold-start benchmark: 1,154 medical MCQs, R²≈0.29 and RMSE≈1.37 logits on IRT b, with blueprint code and option-structure features among the top predictors. Workshop-level, single dataset.
23. **"Estimating item difficulty using large language models and tree-based machine learning algorithms" (2026).** *International Journal of Artificial Intelligence in Education*. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijaied.2026.100015 (preprint: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.08804) — **Tier B.** *Full text read.* r≈0.87 for LLM-feature + ensemble pipelines vs r≈0.72–0.87 for direct LLM ratings, and the finding that TF-IDF and metadata-only baselines are near-useless. Recent and unreplicated.
24. **Swaminathan, H., Hambleton, R. K., Sireci, S. G., Xing, D., & Rizavi, S. M. (2003). Small Sample Estimation in Dichotomous Item Response Models: Effect of Priors Based on Judgmental Information on the Accuracy of Item Parameter Estimates.** *Applied Psychological Measurement*, 27(1), 27–51. https://doi.org/10.1177/0146621602239475 — **Tier C as used here.** **Abstract only — paywalled.** Conceptually the most on-point source for "is DOK a reasonable prior," reporting modest 1PL and considerable 2PL/3PL gains from judgmental priors, but I have none of its numbers. **Worth obtaining.**
25. **Kang, H.-A., et al. Sequential Generalized Likelihood Ratio Tests for Online Item Monitoring.** *Psychometrika*. https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/psychometrika/article/abs/sequential-generalized-likelihood-ratio-tests-for-online-item-monitoring/D91664C408A56D4999FDF76B0CE2CD90 — **Tier B, abstract only.** The right modern framing for drift detection in a continuously-collecting product (joint multivariate monitoring, continuous or intermittent), but I have no performance numbers.
26. **Robitzsch, A. (2024/2025). Linking Error Estimation in Fixed Item Parameter Calibration** (*Foundations* 5(1):4) and **Bias-Corrected Fixed Item Parameter Calibration** (*Stats* 8(2):29). https://www.mdpi.com/2673-9321/5/1/4 , https://www.mdpi.com/2571-905X/8/2/29 — **Tier C for our purposes.** *Full texts retrieved, skimmed.* Useful for FIPC's linking-error theory and for the N=125 BCFIPC result — **but the target parameters are group μ and σ, not new-item parameters**, so the small-N figure does not transfer to item calibration. Included mainly to prevent that misreading.
27. **Albano, A. D. Introduction to Educational and Psychological Measurement Using R**, Ch. 7. https://www.thetaminusb.com/intro-measurement-r/irt.html — **Tier C.** *Read.* Source of the convenient textbook framing "100 minimum for CTT item analysis; 100–200 per item for Rasch; 500+ for 2PL/3PL," itself citing de Ayala (2009) and Embretson & Reise (2000). Useful for orientation, not as primary evidence.
28. **`irtsim` CRAN vignettes** ("Planning a Sample Size for an IRT Study"; "Model misspecification: generate 2PL, fit 1PL"). https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/irtsim/ — **Tier C.** *Read.* Operationalises Schroeders & Gnambs's procedure and gives the clearest available demonstration that Rasch misspecification bias is structural and does not shrink with N. Software documentation, not peer-reviewed.
29. **AAMC, How is the MCAT Exam Scored? / Your Questions Answered: The MCAT Scoring Process.** https://students-residents.aamc.org/mcat-scores/how-mcat-exam-scored — **Tier B for what it says, which is little.** *Read.* Confirms per-form scaling and equating and the presence of unscored items, but publishes no model, no per-item calibration N, and no pretest design. Companion: **IRT Equating of the MCAT (2001), MCAT Monograph**, ERIC ED462425 — **abstract only**, and its 3,638–8,494 samples are equating samples, not pretest samples.
30. **rasch.org, "Rasch dichotomous model vs. One-parameter Logistic Model"** (https://rasch.org/rmt/rmt193h.htm) and **mathematicalpsychology.com, "Three-Parameter Model"** (https://mathematicalpsychology.com/Three_Parameter_Model) — **Tier C.** *Read.* Used only for the Rasch-vs-1PL parameterisation/scale-origin distinction and for the "Beta prior near 0.20 on c is standard practice" convention. Both are secondary syntheses; neither should carry a load-bearing claim on its own.
